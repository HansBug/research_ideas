Since the release of early Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-2 and GPT-3 around 2020, rapid advancements in LLM
capabilities have significantly impacted the field of code intelligence, enabling automation across a wide range of coding
tasks, including code generation, program repair, debugging, and software testing etc. As the LLMs are widely used in coding
tasks, benchmarking their capabilities in a meticulous and meaningful way is essential.
Inthiswork,wesurvey 142relatedpaperspublishedbetweenJanuary2020andJune2025,covering 156uniquebenchmarks
and32different coding tasks, to provide a comprehensive review of coding benchmarks to evaluate LLMs, exploring
their characteristics, strengths, limitations, dataset construction, task coverage, alignment with real world challenges, and
performance metrics.
Our analysis indicates that Python was the most prevalent programming language, featured in 77% of the datasets, and
GitHub was the most common data source, used in 46% of the cases. Also, there is an increasing trend in the publication of
new benchmark datasets for coding intelligence over the past three years. Prominent publication venues are machine learning
conferences such as NeurIPS, ICLR, and ICML, while comparatively fewer datasets have appeared in software engineering
venues such as ICSE, ASE, and MSR. In addition, most of the benchmarks focused on the code generation task, with 86
datasets dedicated to this area. We identify key gaps and limitations in existing benchmarks, including bias in datasets, lack
of standardized evaluation protocols, and insufficient coverage of real-world challenges. Finally, we present a vision for
the future of LLM benchmark creation, advocating for more robust, adaptable, and better alignment with practical code
intelligence demands that ensure the reliability and practicality of LLMs in coding workflows.

2.1 Background on LLMs for Coding Intelligence
SincethereleaseofearlylargelanguagemodelssuchasGPT-2[ 144]andGPT-3[ 18]around2020,LLMshavebeen
increasingly integrated into the code intelligence domain, transforming the way developers approach complex
challenges. These models are trained on extensive datasets that include both natural language and source code,
giving them the ability to interpret, generate, and adapt text and code. Due to this versatility, LLMs are now

can also help with code summarization by turning large or complex functions into clear, concise explanations

3.3 Study Selection and Quality Assessment
The process of selecting studies to be included in our survey on LLM benchmark datasets involved the following
stages: (1) an initial screening based on the study titles, (2) a secondary selection after reviewing the abstracts,
and (3) a final decision after a thorough reading of the full articles. It is important to note that the initial search
results included many entries that were not relevant to benchmark datasets for large language models in code
intelligence. This discrepancy was primarily due to incidental keyword matches. To ensure the quality and
relevance of our dataset, each study was manually reviewed, and unrelated entries were removed. Additionally,
we observed several duplicate entries, as the same study was sometimes indexed by multiple sources. These
duplicates were manually identified and excluded.
Theinitialinclusioncriteriaforoursurveyareasfollows:(1)studiesmustbeeitherpeer-reviewedpublicationsor
recent arXiv preprints published between late 2023 and 2025 that have not yet completed the peer-review process;
However, preprints must explicitly pass the rigorous quality assessment outlined in Section 3.3.1, specifically
satisfying criteria regarding validation methodology, data integrity, and evaluation soundness. We include these
qualified preprints to ensure coverage of the latest developments, (2) studies must report experimental results, (3)
studies must introduce a novel benchmark dataset or aim to improve or extend existing benchmarks, and (4)
benchmark datasets must either utilize LLMs directly or are employed as baselines for comparison in studies
about benchmark datasets involving LLMs. We also applied the following exclusion criteria to filter out irrelevant
studies: (1) studies focused on unrelated engineering fields (e.g., electrical engineering, hardware engineering,
quantum physics), (2) studies published only in workshops, (3) survey or review articles, (4) studies centered on
traditional natural language processing techniques without the use of large language models, and (5) publications
categorized as books, book chapters, tutorials, or technical reports.
3.3.1 Title Filtering Stage. In the initial filtering stage, we examined the titles of the studies to determine their
potentialrelevancetoLLMbenchmarkdatasetsincodeintelligence.Giventhattitlesoftenprovidelimitedcontext
about the full scope of the study, our evaluation in this phase was guided primarily by the presence of predefined
keywords. The guiding research question for this stage was: Do the titles contain specific keywords or phrases
indicative of benchmark datasets for large language models in the domain of code intelligence? For example,
while a title such as “Benchmarking Large Language Models for Automated Verilog RTL Code Generation”
contains several relevant terms, namely, “benchmarking”, “large language model”, and “code generation”, a
closer inspection reveals that the study is related to hardware design rather than code intelligence or software
engineering. Such studies were excluded. Through manual selection of 2,377 records, we identified 404 unique
studies that met our preliminary inclusion criteria and were retained for further review.
Abstract Filtering Stage: Building on the results of the title screening, we proceeded to a more detailed
examination of the abstracts. For each study, the abstract was segmented into four key components: context,
objective, approach, and results/findings, in alignment with the common scientific abstract structure. This
decomposition enabled a more precise evaluation of each study’s relevance to our research objectives. As a result
of this stage, we retained 177 unique studies for subsequent verification and inclusion in our systematic review.
ContentFilteringStage: In this stage, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the full text of each candidate study
to ensure alignment with the objectives of our review. To systematically assess the relevance and quality of the
identified papers, we developed a set of evaluative criteria in the form of structured questions. These questions
were designed to verify the suitability of each study in the context of benchmarking LLMs for code intelligence
tasks. A study was retained for further analysis only if it provided satisfactory answers to 75% of the following
questions:

3.4 Inter-Rater Reliability
To mitigate subjective bias and ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the study selection process, two
researchers independently categorized the articles in the title, abstract, and full-text filtering stages. Following the
independent review, we assessed the consistency between the reviewers to validate the integrity of the dataset
selection.Thereliabilitybetweentheresearcherswasevaluatedusingtwometrics:overallagreementandCohen’s
Kappa coefficient ( 𝜅). The process yielded an overall agreement rate of 0.95. Furthermore, the calculated Cohen’s
Kappa was 0.89, indicating a high level of agreement according to standard interpretation guidelines. Despite the
high level of agreement, disagreements occurred in specific cases. To mitigate subjectivity and ensure quality, a
third researcher acted as an arbitrator to resolve conflicts that could not be resolved between the two primary
reviewers. This triaged approach helped minimize classification bias and improved the overall consistency of the
final survey corpus.

4 Result Analysis
4.1 RQ1: What Is the Current Landscape of Benchmark Datasets
This RQ targets understanding the current landscape of publications. We examined publication dates, venues,

4.1.3DatasetContextScope:FromSingle-FunctiontoRepository-LevelContexts .The table 4 depicts
the detailed distribution of context scopes for different datasets. One of the most significant evolutions in coding
benchmarks is the expansion of context scope (i.e., the amount of surrounding code or documentation an LLM
must process to solve a task). To categorize the varying levels of information available to models, we define
four distinct levels of context scope. These levels represent a hierarchy of complexity that ranges from isolated
algorithmic units to real-world repository environments.
•Single-FunctionScope: The most basic level focuses on isolated algorithmic logic. Tasks in this category
involve a standalone function based on a docstring or signature, with no external dependencies or global
state.
•Class-LevelScope: Introduces Object-Oriented Programming complexities. Tasks require generating
code that maintains internal state and interacts correctly with member variables and other methods
within a specific class definition.
•Multi-File Scope: Focuses on modularity and inter-file communication. Tasks involve handling explicit
dependencies, requiring the correct import and utilization of API calls or data structures defined in
external files.
•Repository-Level Scope: The most realistic and complex level, simulating a full software environment.
Tasks require navigating a complete codebase to identify implicit dependencies, necessitating the use of
advanced algorithms or autonomous agents to effectively handle the extensive context.

4.2.2 How are the benchmark datasets structured and organized? One of the most important aspects of LLM
benchmark datasets is their structure and practical usability. A well-defined and accessible structure is vital in
order to support future research. This will enable the application of more sophisticated theoretical methods and
facilitate a consistent evaluation by using the tests provided or canonical solutions of the benchmarking dataset.
Many of the datasets have a problem-centric structure, meaning that they are designed for evaluating LLMs by
providing a problem description for LLM and having unit tests to test the provided solution by LLM, or have
canonical solutions that will try to compare the LLM-generated solution with the canonical solution. Datasets
suchasHumanEval,MBPP,Math-QAPython,andAPPS[ 14,29,60]allfollowthisstructure.Theupdatedvariants,
such as HumanEval+ or LBPP [ 110,122], have kept the same structure, but have tried to increase the test coverage
and handle minor problems in canonical solutions, while datasets such as HumanEval-X and MBXP [ 13] have
extended these problems to multiple programming languages. The other pattern that we can see is for the datasets
designed for code translation [ 22,174,191]. These datasets are organized in a way that includes parallel or
equivalent codes in different programming languages, and even sometimes include unit tests to check runability
and semantics of the generated codes. Furthermore, the G-TransEval [ 76] dataset has gone further and has
grouped tasks into different types and complexity levels to ensure the LLM’s ability in code translation.
Datasets focused on bug detection and debugging [ 158,163] organized data as buggy code snippets paired
with explanations, fixes, golden patches, and unit tests. These datasets often classify the types of bugs or stages of
the debugging process.

or data contamination. Table 11 presents a detailed categorization of the quality control approaches employed
by each surveyed benchmark. It should be noted that a large proportion of 43% of datasets (67 datasets) did not
apply quality controls to their data collection pipeline. We can categorize the quality control measures described
in the reviewed articles into the following groups.
•Manual Inspection and Expert Review : Human inspection and expert knowledge remain among the
most reliable methods for validating results and processes in benchmark creation. Although minor human
errors can occur, this approach is particularly well-suited to the technical demands of code intelligence. As
shown in Table 11, 32% of the datasets employed this method. Quality assurance in this category includes
a detailed manual review by authors or experts in the domain of the entire data set [ 112,122] or selected
subsets [97, 191]. Some projects even used external experts specifically for validation [160].
•Multi-Annotator and Peer Verification : Several datasets (9%) employed independent annotators,
different from those who created the initial data, to validate the entries. This approach often involved
volunteers or professional engineers tasked with verifying and cross-checking the collected data to ensure

each annotator exchanges their work with another annotator for cross-refinement, in order to reduce
subjective bias and errors. Similarly, in MCEval [ 23], each sample is independently annotated by at least
two annotators to mitigate subjectivity, with any discrepancies resolved through consensus discussions
or adjudication by a senior annotator.

4.2.5 How Do Benchmark Datasets Mitigate Data Contamination? Data contamination, where evaluation data
inadvertently leaks into a model’s training corpus, poses a critical threat to the validity of code intelligence
benchmarks. When models have “seen” the test cases during training, their performance reflects memorization
rather than generalization. To address this, existing benchmarks employ four primary mitigation methodologies
(see Table 13):
•TemporalIsolation&KnowledgeCutoffs: Thisstrategyensuresthatevaluationdatastrictlypost-dates
the training period of the LLMs being tested. By selecting data sources or repositories released after a
specific “knowledge cutoff” date, these benchmarks prevent the model from having prior exposure to
the test set. For example, LiveCodeBench [ 72] and RepoEval [ 104] utilize continuous updates or recent
repository creation dates to maintain a fresh evaluation set that remains unseen by LLMs.
•Pre-trainingCorpusExclusion: Rather than relying on dates, this approach algorithmically purges
benchmark data that overlaps with common large-scale training corpora. Techniques include N-gram
overlapanalysis,MinHashdeduplication,andexactstringmatchingtoidentifyandremoveleakedsamples.
e.g., BigCodeBench [ 225] and AL-Bench [ 162] employ rigorous N-gram analysis to detect and remove
contamination. ExecRepoBench [ 194] explicitly removes samples with significant word overlap from the
training corpus to ensure independence.
•Perturbation and Novelty Construction: This methodology focuses on creating novel content or
modifying existing data so that models must reason rather than rely on memorized solutions. Authors
either hand-craft entirely new problems that do not exist on the web or apply semantic perturbations
to existing problems to render them unrecognizable to the model while preserving the underlying logic.
HumanEval [ 29] consists of hand-written Python problems specifically designed to be distinct from public
GitHub repositories. DS-1000 [ 87] utilizes extensive surface and semantic perturbations to prevent models
from recalling solutions from memory.
•EnvironmentalIsolationandPrivateHoldouts: Themostrobustmitigationinvolvesrestrictingaccess
to the data itself. These benchmarks maintain private “holdout” sets that are never released publicly or use
proprietary internal data, preventing web crawlers from indexing the answers for future model training.

4.5 RQ5: Future Directions
Based on our systematic analysis of existing LLM benchmarks in code intelligence, we identify five critical
directions for advancing evaluation frameworks to meet the field’s evolving demands. Each direction addresses
the gaps identified in current methodologies while aligning with emerging requirements for specialized, scalable,
and context-aware assessments.
4.5.1Multi-ModalBenchmarkDevelopment .Although early efforts like SWE-Bench Multi-Modal [ 193]
have begun exploring multi-modal evaluation, significant gaps remain in assessing LLMs’ ability to process het-
erogeneous code intelligence artifacts. Future benchmarks should integrate code with complementary modalities,
including UI/UX designs (e.g., Figma mockups), system architecture diagrams, and runtime execution traces,
particularly for front-end development tasks where visual elements and interactive behaviors co-evolve with
code [184,209]. New datasets could combine version control histories with design system changes to enable
evaluation of cross-modal reasoning capabilities. A pressing need exists for standardized metrics that quantify
alignment between code modifications and corresponding interface updates, as current benchmarks focus pri-
marily on textual quality assurance without addressing visual artifacts. This direction is critical for applications
requiring coordination between design systems and implementation, such as responsive web development and
accessibility compliance.
PracticalChallenges .The key challenge in multi-modal benchmarking arises from the high cost of collecting
and aligning heterogeneous data (e.g., synchronizing UI designs with codebase versions). Manual curation
is already resource-intensive; adding visual modalities increases this burden and introduces subjectivity in
evaluation (e.g., judging “visual correctness”).
MitigationStrategies .Recent advances in LLMs enable scalable alternatives to manual curation. Synthetic
data generation can produce code from structured UI descriptions, while automated rendering and validation
tools support efficient assessment of visual outputs. In addition, crowdsourcing pipelines leveraging front-end
developer communities can help distribute data collection and annotation costs, improving scalability while
reducing reliance on expert-driven curation.
4.5.2Domain-SpecificBenchmarkSpecialization .Current benchmarks exhibit excessive generality, lim-
iting their utility for specialized code intelligence subdomains. We advocate for benchmarks tailored to back-
end development (e.g., API design and distributed system patterns), DevOps (CI/CD pipeline synthesis and

infrastructure-as-code validation), and front-end tasks (design-to-code translation and cross-browser compatibil-
ity resolution). For instance, SWE-Bench [ 77] demonstrates the value of GitHub issue-based evaluations but lacks
specialization for domain-specific workflows. Datasets like DS-1000 [87] for data science libraries highlight the
benefits of targeted evaluations, yet coverage remains sparse for critical domains like deep learning frameworks.
Practical Challenges. Specialization requires deep domain expertise (e.g., identifying valid DevOps pipeline

Mitigation Strategies. To address expertise scarcity, benchmarks should utilize mining of official documenta-

tial aspects of industrial-scale software maintenance. Next-generation datasets must incorporate multi-repository
dependencies mirroring microservice architectures, legacy system migration scenarios with deprecated depen-
dencies, and cross-version API compatibility challenges. Furthermore, there is an urgent need for benchmarks
targeting low-resource programming languages like Go and Rust, which power critical infrastructure but lack
adequate evaluation frameworks. The MultiPL-T [ 21] approach, which generates validated training data through
code translation and test synthesis, offers a promising pathway for creating benchmarks in resource-constrained
environments; however, code translation fails to capture language-specific paradigms, runtime behaviors, and
idiomatic coding practices unique to emerging languages like Go and Rust.
PracticalChallenges. Simulatingrealisticenvironmentsiscomputationallyexpensiveandproneto“flakiness”
(non-deterministic execution errors), as seen in the complexity of existing benchmarks like SWE-bench [ 77]. The
infrastructure cost for executing multi-container environments for every evaluation run is challenging for many
academic labs.
MitigationStrategies. Benchmarks should be distributed as pre-built, lightweight Docker containers with
fixed dependencies to ensure reproducible evaluation. To further manage computational cost, tiered evaluation
settings can be provided, including a Liteconfiguration for rapid iteration with mocked dependencies and a Full
configuration for final validation using complete environment execution.

4.5.7 Unified Framework for Benchmarking. To address the practical challenges of implementation, we propose
aUnified Benchmarking Framework . This framework unifies isolated improvements into a coherent lifecycle,
extending the rigorous construction guidelines established by recent work [ 19], to address the specific demands
of modern Code Intelligence. The framework consists of four phases:
Phase 1: Dynamic Sourcing. To combat data leakage and ensure relevance, sourcing must move beyond static
snapshots. We propose traceable sourcing [ 19] combined with dynamic versioning, where benchmarks are explic-
itly versioned against specific library updates. Furthermore, sourcing pipelines must implement cutoff-aware
validation, automatically filtering repositories based on timestamps to prevent contamination.
Phase 2: Construction and Verification. Ensuring data quality at scale requires automated procedures. Bench-
marks should apply deduplication and denoising to remove redundant or low-quality samples. For multi-modal
tasks, additional checks are needed to verify cross-modal consistency, such as confirming that generated code
correctly renders the intended user interface, thereby aligning textual correctness with visual accuracy.
Phase 3: Reproducible Evaluation Protocols. To reduce evaluation inconsistencies, benchmarks should ensure
reproducible execution by distributing complete hardware and software setups, such as Docker containers, rather
than isolated code snippets. In addition, evaluation should consider how a model arrives at a solution, including
its use of tools and intermediate steps, instead of focusing only on the final output.
Phase4:SustainableMaintenance. To reduce maintenance overhead, benchmarks should move away from
one-time releases toward continuous updating. Automated processes can periodically check for data contami-
nation and collect new test cases from active repositories, helping keep benchmarks up to date with minimal
manual effort.

Finally, greater emphasis should be placed on creating high-quality test cases and establishing more robust
evaluation protocols to enable a more reliable and confident assessment of model performance.
5 Threats to Validity
ExternalValidity .One of the main threats to the external validity of our study lies in its scope and specificity.
We deliberately focused on collecting datasets explicitly designed to benchmark LLMs. To ensure this focus, we
include only benchmarks developed after the emergence of LLMs (that is, early 2020) and specifically intended
for their evaluation.
Internal Validity .A key concern in our data collection process is the potential for bias that arises from
subjectiveinterpretation.Thisbiasstemsprimarilyfromunavoidabledisagreementsduringthepaperclassification
stage,sincetworesearcherscollaborativelycategorizedarticlesaccordingtotheirtitles,abstracts,andfullcontent.
To mitigate this issue and ensure quality, a third researcher ruled out disagreements that could not be resolved
between the two, helping to reduce classification bias and improve consistency.
Construct Validity .The primary threat to the construct validity of our survey concerns the completeness of
our dataset collection and whether we have comprehensively covered all relevant LLM benchmarking datasets.
To ensure coverage, we systematically searched the major repositories of code intelligence, software engineering,
and artificial intelligence, including IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar, and
selected our candidates from an initial pool of 2,377 articles. A second threat to construct validity concerns the
degree of coverage achieved for each primary study. To address this, we systematically analyzed the studies from
four perspectives: overall statistics, construction processes and quality, evaluation pipelines, and shortcomings.
A third threat concerns the reliability of preprint studies. Although a structured quality-filtering process was
applied (as detailed in Section 3.3), arXiv preprints had not undergone formal peer review at the time of inclusion.
This may introduce risks regarding methodological maturity or result stability.
6 Conclusion
In this study, we conducted a systematic survey to investigate the characteristics of LLM benchmark datasets in
code intelligence. We utilized a custom web scraper to extract the initial set of studies from four widely used
online digital libraries (i.e., ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar). After manually
filtering out studies not related to LLM benchmarking, we identified 156benchmarks reported in 142papers. We
then developed taxonomies and systematically addressed each research question.
Our analysis reveals a notable increase in the number of benchmark datasets after 2023. We observed that 46%
of the studies sourced their data from GitHub, while 26% involved the manual creation of the dataset. OpenAI’s
GPT models were the most widely used LLMs, featured in 85% of the studies. Regarding quality assurance, 32% of
the datasets used manual inspection or expert review, while 43% lacked any formal quality control measures.
Moreover, 49% of the datasets aimed to ensure relevance in the real world by collecting data from practical
