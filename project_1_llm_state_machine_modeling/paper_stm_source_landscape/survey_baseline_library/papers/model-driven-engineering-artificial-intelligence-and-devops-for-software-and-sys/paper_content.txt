This paper presents a systematic mapping study classifying existing scientific contributions on synergies of Model Driven
Engineering (MDE), Artificial Intelligence/Machine Learning (AI/ML), and DevOps, with the overall objective of supporting
the continuous development of Cyber-Physical Systems (CPSs). We collected papers from bibliographic sources and selected
primary studies to analyse. Then, we characterised and classified the current state of the art, focusing on 1) main aspects
already tackled at the intersection of at least two of the three studied areas, and 2) findings emerging from the analysis as a
framework for potential future research, notably regarding the integration of the three studied areas. The results reveal that
fewapproachescombineMDE,AI/ML,andDevOpsforsoftwareandsystemsengineering.Incontrast,severalapproacheshave
combined two of them, specifically MDE and DevOps. Approaches combining AI/ML with MDE or DevOps are also becoming
more frequent and will most likely continue to progress in the future. These synergies cover a range of engineering activities,
from requirements and design to monitoring, maintenance, and evolution. Open research challenges include advancing AI/ML,
MDE, and DevOps integration, supporting scalable, data-oriented solutions, proposing new continuous engineering methods,
and adapting DevOps practices to diverse systems.

1Introduction
Nowadays,modernsystemsinthedomainsofIndustry4.0,healthcare,autonomouslydrivingcars,andsmartgrids
are examples of highly communicating systems in which software enables increasingly advanced functionality.
The growing complexity of these systems (e.g., Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS) [ 60], Cyber-Physical System of
Systems (CPSoS) [ 12]) poses several challenges throughout all system design, development, and analysis phases,
but also during their deployment, actual usage, and future maintenance.
With the advent of DevOps principles [ 24,37], system engineering would benefit from supporting a continuous
development involving a smooth continuum of information from design to runtime and vice versa. Moreover,
many leading companies have started envisioning the productivity boost of tomorrow to be brought by the
application of full-blown Artificial Intelligence (AI) principles and techniques, including Machine Learning
(ML) [53]. Through AI for IT operations (AIOps) [ 20], we can rethink the DevOps pipeline through continuous
monitoring, alerting, and remediation in a secure and reliable way.
In this context, Model Driven Engineering (MDE) [ 13,57] has gained popularity in software development
projects as it raises the level of abstraction and thus improves the ability to handle the complexity of systems.
Using models as purposeful abstractions of systems and their environment is a pillar enabling technology in
current and future system engineering platforms [60].
However, to the best of our knowledge, although its relevance is recognized, few approaches intend to support
full continuous software and system engineering processes integrating MDE, AI/ML1, and DevOps altogether.
Efficiently combining these three areas is a major objective of the AIDOaRt large collaborative project [ 1,15].
This three-year European effort, involving 31 academic and industrial partners from seven countries, aims to
support the AI-augmented automation of modelling, coding, testing, monitoring, and continuous development in
CPSs.
This paper reports on a Systematic Mapping Study (SMS) of the existing literature [ 50] we performed to explore
the current state of both research methodologies and practical solutions that rely on different synergies between
MDE, AI/ML, and DevOps. Thus, given the extensive body of work in the three areas, we focused on academic
papers that discuss and consider a synergy of at least two of these areas. As a result, the main contributions of
the paper are:
An up-to-date cartography of the existing literature that classifies approaches in our context concerning (i)
investigated areas and application domains; (ii) addressed problems and available tools; (iii) degree of proposed
automation support;
Based on this, a more detailed analysis of the potential innovation and future challenges to be faced at the
intersection of the three studied areas, notably regarding a more complete integration of MDE, AI/ML, and
DevOps altogether.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides information about the three pillar areas, i.e.,
MDE, AI/ML, and DevOps. Section 3 describes the protocol adopted to perform the SMS. Section 4 presents
the SMS results and subsequent discussion. Section 5 evaluates the quality and limitations of the current SMS.
Section 6 presents conclusions.

2Background
Model Driven Engineering (MDE), Artificial Intelligence/Machine Learning (AI/ML), and DevOps are the three
main research areas that together represent the contextof this study. The following provides a brief background
of these research areas.
2.1Basic concepts and their relationships
Over the last two decades, MDE has become increasingly popular in software development projects as it raises
the level of abstraction, thus improving the ability to handle complex systems (e.g., CPSs and CPSoSs). Using
models as purposeful abstractions of systems and their environment is increasingly important for industrial
applications [ 62]. The first-generation MDE tools primarily focused on generating code from high-level platform-
independent abstract descriptions [ 56]. Since then, the target scope of MDE has broadened and now addresses,
for example, model-based testing, verification, measurement, tool interoperability, software evolution, and many
more hard key challenges in software engineering.
In such a context, [ 9] presents a model-based approach to support variable products in a system development
environment. [ SP49] presents an approach to maintain consistency between design models, the system under
test, and test components. In addition, [ 54] uses a model-based methodology for model transformations to
enable verification of system models using the AADL modelling language. [ 39] introduces a methodology to help
developers maintain consistency in industrial model-based development projects by identifying inconsistencies
during system development and maintenance. [ 63] examines issues and challenges that drive organizations to
adopt model-based systems engineering as a solution to address the challenges of developing complex systems.
MDE benefits include (i) providing better abstraction principles and techniques, (ii) facilitating activities
automation, and (iii) supporting technology integration among all the covered design and development activities.
The heterogeneous data resulting from different engineering activities can be converted and then manipulated as
MDE artefacts, contributing to making explicit the implicit modelling as performed in other project areas. By
switching to model-driven approaches, businesses reap benefits through increased productivity and software
quality [57].
Artificial Intelligence can be defined as a branch of computer science concerned with building intelligent
machines [ 45] capable of finding solutions to complex problems in a more human-like fashion. AI research is
highly specialized and divided into different areas such as machine learning, automated reasoning, knowledge-
based systems, optimization, and natural language processing, which have successfully been applied in several
industrial applications to achieve increased profitability, efficiency, safety, and security [2].
AI and ML technologies are increasingly used in day-to-day applications, affecting existing development
practices by reducing the time-to-market and improving the quality of the software in general. The dissemination
of such principles and techniques in a regulated industry can rapidly enable systems to decide and act in a more
and more automated manner, sometimes even without direct human control [11].
Many AI and ML techniques have been used in several software engineering tasks. For example, in the
requirements stage, requirements have been extracted from user stories and natural language requirements
using natural language processing techniques [ 52]. During the development stage of the applications, machine
learning techniques have facilitated code generation, documentation, and management through code completion,
code search assistance, and automating code commenting and documentation [ 64]. Besides, numerous machine
learning-powered software testing and debugging tools are intended to find and address bugs already occurring
during the development of software systems and to aid in the test generation and during the execution of these
tests [68]. Regarding the post-deployment phases of software systems, several research projects have also used
machine-learning techniques to aid in software maintenance. Their proposed techniques are to identify cloned
code, suggest changes to the code in every version release, and categorize user reviews automatically. [27]

DevOps is a software development approach that bridges the traditional divide between development (Dev) and
operations (Ops) teams in an organization [ 37,42,SP96]. The primary goal of DevOps is to shorten development
and deployment cycles between Dev and Ops based on a more collaborative relationship and agile collaboration
between both factions. It enables continuous feedback and quick response to changes and uses automated
delivery pipelines, reducing cycle time. To this end, DevOps introduces automation into all stages of the software
development lifecycle—from integration, testing, and release to deployment and infrastructure management. As
a result, software development and evolution become more efficient and reliable.
Artificial Intelligence for IT Operations (AIOps) is the most prominent synergy between DevOps. The term
AIOps was coined by Gartner [ 8,53] to characterize solutions where DevOps challenges are addressed with the
help of AI/ML techniques. A fundamental challenge resides in integrating AIOps [ 20] in enhancing the DevOps
pipeline, for example, through continuous alerts and insights from data used for continuous deployment and
operationsmanagement.AIOpsusesdatascienceandcomputationaltechniquestoautomatecommonandroutine
operational tasks, digest metrics, and use inference models to extract actionable insights from data.
On the one hand, AI/ML is a relevant complementary means, along with MDE, providing more efficient and
flexible support to the continuous development of complex systems, such as CPSs [ 70]. On the other hand,
the DevOps toolchain can be enhanced by employing AI techniques, in particular ML, in multiple aspects of
the system engineering process (design and modelling quality, source code performance, testing coverage, and
predictive monitoring, to name a few) [43, SP63].
Data represents a key aspect of the synergy of MDE, AI/ML, and DevOps is concerned [69]. The information
recovered from a given system execution, either historical data or real-time traces (such as car sensor data or
radar-based traffic monitoring data), has a potentially very high value in assessing the validity and required
properties of a system design. However, dedicated methods and tools are needed to extract knowledge from large
volumes of such runtime data and analyze and (re)use it in a productive manner. Data science analysis draws
insights from structured and unstructured data (i.e., data, raw data, metadata, …) [ 71]. To this end, integrating
data-related technological innovations can ensure that systems are designed responsibly in a way that contributes
to our trust in their behaviour.
Thanks to the application of advanced MDE principles and techniques, the heterogeneous data resulting
from different engineering activities can be converted and then manipulated as MDE artefacts (i.e., models,
metamodels), contributing to making explicit the implicit modelling as performed in other project areas. All data
from these heterogeneous sources can be processed, collected, traced, and analyzed more homogeneously (e.g.,
relying on common shared repositories and model views [ 14]). This way, relevant data can be made available to
all the AI-based and/or DevOps-based services that require it.
As the purpose of this work, the study of the synergies among MDE, AI/ML, and DevOps can help researchers
andpractitionerstodevelopscalablesolutionsforefficienthandlingandmanagementofnumerous,heterogeneous,
and large models potentially covering several functional and non-functional perspectives of the system under
development (in a DevOps process) suitable for large distributed cross-functional working teams and allowing to
integrate feedback (notably from runtime data) to the system level models [SP101].
2.2Related work
To the best of our knowledge, no existing systematic mapping studies consider the synergies between MDE,
DevOps, and AI/ML to the extent of this article. Nonetheless, several secondary studies focus on subsets of our
investigation or address specific research fields that emerged as a consequence of such synergies.
In general, DevOps adoption poses significant challenges both from an organizational and a technical perspec-
tive: Laukkanen et al. [ 40], Shahin et al. [ 58], and Leite et al. [ 42] describe the results of systematic studies on the
adoption DevOps and Continuous Development/Integration/Deployment practices. While these works shed light

on recurring issues and possible solutions when pursuing continuous processes, they do not address the specific
problems and potential of including MDE and AI/ML in the context.
MDE is often conceived as a resource and a hindrance in adopting DevOps and, generally, continuous pro-
cesses [6]. Correspondingly, surveys related to DevOps and continuous processes often include MDE method-
ologies as a fundamental ingredient, notably for automated deployment [67] or continuous testing [51]. On the
other hand, MDE solutions might suffer (frequent) changes, e.g., to keep traceability links updated, to detect
and manage distributed modifications, and generally, to handle consistency needs [ 6]. Related to consistency
management, digital twins have emerged as a suitable technology to support the feedback loop from operations
to development: Djebali et al. [ 22] present a survey on continuous digital twins design with a particular focus on
the smart-grid domain of application. In our SMS, we investigate the cases where MDE supports DevOps and the
literature in which DevOps processes are adopted to realize continuous MDE.
AI/ML solutions are becoming ubiquitous as components of a target system and as technology to improve the
development process. Martínez-Fernández et al. distinguish between SE4AI (Software Engineering for AI) and
AI4SE (AI for SE, also known as intelligent SE) and present an SMS on SE4AI [ 44]. Moreover, Perkusich et al. [ 48]
discuss the results of a survey on AI4SE. Both studies agree on the infancy of these research fields, even though it
is expected to be a burst of new research results due to the recent advent of Large Language Models. Interestingly,
in a recent survey by Diaz-de-Arcaya et al. [ 21], the distinction between SE4AI and AI4SE becomes blurrier when
considering DevOps processes. These cases are referred to as AIOps or MLOps, and the AI/ML mechanisms are
both part of product components and contribute to the feedback loop for improving the product itself. This line of
reasoning is consistent with the goals of our SMS, which can be considered a superset of the investigation in [ 21]
since we are investigating the synergies and cross-fertilization opportunities between AI/ML and SE processes,
resources, and products. Naveed et al. [ 46] delve into the intersection of MDE and machine learning components,
which they term MDE4ML. Their study aims to analyze existing research on MDE4ML (a subset of MDE4AI). The
authors highlight the increasing adoption of ML as a component in various software applications, a trend driven
by the desire to harness large volumes of data for predictive capabilities and informed decision-making.
3Method
To understand state-of-the-art research practices on MDE, AI/ML, and DevOps to support the system engineering
process we conducted a systematic mapping study (SMS) of the existing literature. An SMS is a method for
aggregating systems and software engineering research. It provides a structure of the research results that have
been published by categorizing them and giving a visual summary of the results. This SMS is based on the
guidelines proposed by Petersen et al. [49, 50], and the snowballing process defined by Wohlin [65].

among the team members. This discussion is documented in the SMS to address the proposed RQs. The DEFs,
which each reviewer has filled out, are consolidated to compile comments and to categorize the selected studies
appropriately.
The protocol execution is documented in a spreadsheet collaboratively edited by the involved partners [ 3]. The
following Sections separately describe the protocol steps and activities in detail.
3.1Team
We rely on a team of experienced members with heterogeneous MDE, AI/ML, and DevOps competencies to
execute the protocol. It included members who were experts in at least one of the dimensions considered in the
SMS coming from academia/research organizations, SMEs, and large industries. The team was formed within
the European project AIDOaRt ( AI-augmented automation for efficient DevOps,amodel-based framework for
continuous development AtRunTime in cyber-physical systems) [ 1,15,26]. AIDOaRt focuses on supporting the
continuous development of CPSs via AI-augmented automation, incorporating methods and tools for continuous
software, system engineering, and validation.

3.3.2 Databases Considering the interdisciplinary nature of the SMS, we performed an automatic search on
leading research and rich databases in order to provide adequate literature coverage. The databases were selected
based on the guidelines and suggestions provided by [ 23,50]. Based on these guidelines, the databases consulted
are as follows:
Scopus(http://www.scopus.com/): it is a scientific and interdisciplinary bibliography database created by
Elsevier, which contains abstracts and citations for academic journal articles. We decided to use Scopus since
it is a meta-library and indexes publications by well-known and reputable publishers, such as ACM, IEEE,
Springer, and Elsevier.
ACM Digital Library (http://dl.acm.org/): it is one the most relevant academic databases primarily addressed to
researchers in the field of computer science. Even though Scopus indexes the ACM Digital library, we decided
to include it to ensure that no publications were excluded from Scopus’ indexing of ACM publications.
IEEE Xplore Digital Library (http://ieeexplore.ieee.org): it is the most well-known resource for discovering and
accessing the scientific and technical content published by the IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics
Engineers) and its publishing partners. Although Scopus covers IEEE Xplore, it was included to verify the
quality of the search strings.
DBLP Computer Science Bibliography (http://dblp.uni-trier.de/): it has become a relevant resource in the field of
computer science. We selected the DBLP database since it represents one of the most relevant sources in the
computer science landscape.
SpringerLink (https://link.springer.com/):itisascientificdatabaseoperatedbySpringerInternationalPublishing,
which represents a powerful central access point for Science, Technology, and Medicine researchers. The
SpringerLink database was selected based on expert opinion and to ensure good coverage in the area of
computer science and engineering.
During searching, indexing, and sorting a large number of references, different duplicate references appeared
due to the slight differences in the database indexing. To manage the references and to remove duplicates, the
obtained results have been cleaned with Tidy Bib Web Tool4and have been exported in BibLaTex (.bib), imported
in a shared Zotero5web library6[4], to remove duplicates, exported (e.g., as CSV documents) and imported in
Google Sheet for convenient collaborative sorting/filtering and documentation purposes7.

3.5.4 Ontology Several enhancements have been made to the analysis process of the collected keywords. A
major enhancement concerns the semantic relations between the keywords. First, equivalent terms have been
identified semantically and not only syntactically. Furthermore, the collected keywords have been organized in
a tree of correlated terms, like an ontology. To do this, we sketched a list of questions that ontology should be
able to answer using the competency questions [ 35] approach (e.g., Is Metamodeling a subclass of Model-Driven
Software Engineering or a child of Model-Driven ). Next, we created a list of all terms that we would like to explain,
and we developed a class hierarchy with a combination of top-down (general concepts into specializations) and
bottom-up (specific classes into more general abstract concepts) approaches [ 61]. Once we defined Classes and
SubClasses, we described their internal structure, namely the object properties of the concepts in these Classes,
so that an object property becomes a Slot of this Class (i.e., a leaf in the ontology tree). The produced Ontology
provides a basic structure for representing the relationships between the concepts found in the keyword list,
focusing on the relationships among the provided keywords and capturing the relationships between concepts.
The frequency of keywords metric, called Weighted Keyword (WK), has been used to weight the Classes (i.e.,
the value WKrepresents a semantical weight assigned to each Class/Slot obtained through the Class/Slot word
frequency in the keywords and the sum of the frequency of all SubClasses and Slots belonging to the considered
Classes). Moreover, we also extracted relationships among the ontology research area classes (i.e., MDE, DevOps,
Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning), extracting the keywords appearing together with each dimension
(Q1-Q7). For instance, we selected the keyword “Model-Driven Engineering” and retrieved all selected papers
containingitasakeyword.Subsequently,wecounttheoccurrencesofallkeywordsassociatedwiththeconsidered

3.5.5 TopicAnalysis Topicmodellingisanunsupervisedmachinelearningtechniquethatautomaticallydiscovers
thematic structures within a large collection of text documents (i.e., the so-called “corpus” ) [47]. Each document
addresses one or more topics. Unlike supervised learning, topic modeling does not require pre-annotated data
or training sets. The output consists of a set of “k”topics, each described by its most frequent terms and the
relevance score of each document to that topic. A single document can belong to multiple topics.
In this work, we used a publicly available online tool based on Scikit-learn libraries [ 34] to simplify document-
term matrix creation. According to the tool input’s data type, the selected papers have been stored in a single file
and formatted so that one paper appears on each line. The first step then involved tokenization (i.e., splitting text
into individual terms or tokens). In English, this is done by splitting on whitespace. After preprocessing the text,
where non-informative stopwords were removed, each paper was represented as a vector in an m-dimensional
space, where m is the number of unique terms across all selected papers. This set of terms is called the corpus
vocabulary. The number of times a term appears in a document determines its value in the vector. All documents
are then transformed and stacked into a full document-term matrix. We further enhanced the document-term
matrix by incorporating TF-IDF weighting, which assigns higher weights to more informative terms. TF-IDF
consists of two functions: 1) Term Frequency (TF) that measures the frequency of a term within a single document,
and 2)Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) that captures the term’s overall importance across the corpus. The
terms appearing in many documents receive lower weights. This weighting method emphasizes terms that are
frequentwithinadocumentbutrareacrossthecorpus,leadingtoamoreinformativerepresentationofdocuments,

4.3.5 RQ1 Discussion. Globally, there is a growing trend in the publication of conference papers over the years,
especially in software and systems engineering conferences. This is primarily due to technological advancements,
which have led to the increasingly pervasive use of AI/ML techniques and the high speed at which papers are
written and published in this particular domain. Authors tend to favour conferences with shorter response times
to publish their work as quickly as possible. Publishing longer papers presenting more extended works in journals
only comes as a second step. Software and systems engineering journals are the most prominent among those
we analyzed in this SMS. On the contrary, relatively few publications are coming from journals or conferences
focusing particularly on AI/ML aspects. This is possible since many research efforts involving the use of AI/ML
are instead published in venues specific to given application domains or areas of interest.
As introduced before, our search considers each of the 3 research areas covered by the study; the used query
does not exclude any of them. This means that when we discuss pairs of research areas, we discuss contributions
that fall within the complete topic but explicitly implement only 2 research areas. This premise leads us to state
that there is a predominant attention toward using MDE techniques to support the continuous development and
DevOps process.
This confirms the relevance of model-based approaches for DevOps as a novel research direction studying the
automated connection of CPS design models with CPS runtime monitoring in the form of Digital Twins [ 17].
All the other synergies are significantly lower, suggesting that many challenges involving DevOps and AI/ML
combined with model-based and model-driven technologies are still open.
Specifically,theoveralltrendinMDEandAIsuggeststhatlearning-basedapproachesdominatetheintersection
ofMDEandAI,stronglyemphasizingimprovingmodeltransformations,validation,andexecution.Reinforcement
learning, automated reasoning, and neural networks have proven to be valuable assets in enhancing model
adaptabilityandquality.Atthesametime,MDEtechniquessuchasmetamodelingandDSLsprovidethestructural
foundation for AI development. The integration of these methodologies has led to significant advancements in
AI-powered MDE ecosystems, yet challenges remain in areas such as automated reasoning, perception-driven
modeling, and communication frameworks. Future research should aim to bridge these gaps by developing novel
AI-driven techniques tailored to enhance reasoning capabilities in MDE, while also leveraging MDE principles to
further structure and optimize AI-driven systems. More robust, scalable, and intelligent MDE frameworks can

4.6Future research directions (RQ4)
This section discusses some open research directions that emerged from this study.
Classifying AI/ML techniques in synergies with MDE and DevOps. Integrating AI/ML with MDE
and DevOps can offer improved data analysis [ SP1], enhanced model development processes [ SP121], and
extend streamlined software engineering processes and platforms already integrating MDE and DevOps
aspects [ SP153]. Therefore, investigating the AI techniques utilized in the selected papers and creating subcate-
gories based on these techniques could be an interesting avenue for future research.
Bridging the gap of AI/ML synergies with both MDE and DevOps. One of the primary outcomes of
this study is the significant under-representation of synergies between AI/ML and both MDE and DevOps.
In the work by Cabot et al. (2018), the authors envision potential collaborations between AI/ML and MDE,
highlighting that AI/ML techniques require extensive knowledge bases to produce reliable results. For example,
in MDE, the lack of large, curated datasets containing well-classified examples of “good” and “bad” artifacts
poses a considerable challenge, limiting the training potential of these algorithms. In the context of AI and
ML models, having a clean and curated data set allows for making accurate predictions and better decisions,
which in turn fuels the models themselves. The quest to maintain the quality and availability of data is an
essential part, highlighted in many of the selected papers in this study (e.g., [ SP29,SP119,SP143]), which will
require coping with a diverse set of information sources and legacy systems. Furthermore, as Casale et al. (2015)
point out, successful synergies between AI/ML, MDE, and DevOps necessitate data-aware methodologies
to effectively manage development and operational stages. To address this issue, we propose guidelines and
recommendations to bridge the gap between AI/ML technologies and MDE and DevOps.
–Need foropen data repositories. In general, the availability and management of domain-specific data
still need to be improved. Ideally, the required improvement can be achieved through well-established,
community-driven repositories that provide high-quality data for domain-specific tasks. The objective
would be to provide the required data to train the next generation of domain-specific AI tools. To this
end, the implementation or adoption of standardized data-sharing schemes, e.g., Schema.org extensions
like Croissant [ 5] or DCAT [ 19], can be considered. This would notably enable data and web crawlers
to discover available datasets when progressively released by the community automatically. Moreover,

the MDE and DevOps communities must invest in creating and maintaining curated training datasets
tailored specifically to these fields’ unique needs. Identifying the specific tasks inside the domains that
would benefit from prospective AI tools (e.g., model transformation in MDE) should lead to the design of
these particular datasets. Once again, regular updates to these datasets would be crucial for reflecting

–Historicalperspectiveandchallenges. Creating,developing,andmaintainingrelevantdatarepositories
over the years is challenging. For example, in the MDE community, several initiatives have already been

failed to achieve great success due to the lack of clear long-term incentives (notably from an economic
perspective). We also observed that companies are generally reluctant to publicly share many of their
domain-specific modeling artifacts (models, transformations, generators, etc.) because these artifacts
provide them with a competitive advantage. As a result, the open data in MDE is often limited to generic
models, simple transformations, or toy examples.
–New opportunities with Machine Learning. We believe that the recent advent of ML techniques,
particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), could significantly help reduce these past and current
limitations. Thus, we are likely to witness an increase in contributions related to AI/ML integration within
MDE and DevOps in the near future. LLMs could assist practitioners in generating and curating the
necessary datasets. This is notably true for MDE, in which LLMs could be used to produce large collections
of models with specific structures and characteristics, e.g., for benchmark purposes. As a concrete example
of a solution that provides the kind of bridge connecting MDE and AI, CNNGen [ 33] offers a DSL to
describe CNN architectures. It allows you to automatically generate Python scripts to train and evaluate
the modeled architectures from these descriptions (i.e., models). Such CNN architectures could then be
used to populate target data repositories.
Supporting scalability. DevOps, MDE, and AI/ML focus on scalability. The practices and principles of these
areas play a crucial role in improving the scalability and management of high-demand applications or services.
DevOps principles such as infrastructure as code and containerization facilitate the scalability of software
systems; similarly, ML frameworks and cloud platforms enable the training and deployment of scalable models.
EvenintheMDEarea,muchattentionwaspaidtoscalabilityandflexibility.However,scalabilityisstillacritical
factor in this context (e.g., [ SP19,SP143]), as managing vast amounts of data and adapting to fluctuations and
increasing complexity is often necessary.
Continuoussoftwareengineeringandcontinuouslearning. WhileDevOpsandMDEfocusoncontinuous
integration, deployment, and monitoring, AI/ML adds complexity through continuous learning. As models
evolve, their behavior changes dynamically, raising challenges like model drift, stability, and explainability.
Maintaining AI-driven systems requires real-time monitoring and retraining, but avoiding unintended conse-
quences remains challenging. Software engineering can help with model validation, automated testing, and
traceability, as well as adapting techniques like automated versioning, runtime verification, and explainability
frameworks. Future research should explore how MDE and DevOps can enhance continuous learning, ensuring
stability and maintainability. AI-aware DevOps (e.g., MLOps for SE) could help detect and mitigate model drift,
improving software reliability in AI-powered systems.
Software Variability. A promising research direction is the integration of software variability modeling with
ML feature engineering. Variability modeling, widely used in Software Product Line Engineering (SPLE) and
MBSE, systematically manages configurable software artifacts, while feature engineering is key in optimizing
ML models. Recent studies suggest that bridging these approaches could enhance deep learning applications

5Quality Evaluation
In this research, we adhered to established guidelines for systematic studies. However, our work may still be
subject to specific validity threats. Below, we outline these potential threats and the strategies employed to
mitigate their impact.
5.1Construct Validity
Construct validity threats concern the accuracy of deriving correct conclusions from the relationship between
treatment and outcome [ 65]). Several measures have been taken to deal with threats to construct validity. For
example: (i) avoid mono-operation bias by selecting the reviewers based on different backgrounds (MDE, DevOps,
AI/ML research areas) and research experience, (ii) adopt accurate methods and protocols for all the steps of the
review process (spanning from databases selection through research questions formulations to and data/results
analysis). However, additional improvements are still possible, such as:
A thorough discussion of extracted results to ensure that all the members of the team share the same under-
standing of the tasks,
Including an exhaustive forward and backward snowballing search in the protocol to ensure that no important
contribution is missing,
Ensuring that papers match reviewers’ areas of expertise to provide a high-quality review
Taking into account potential conflict of interest, anonymity, and confidentiality guaranteed in the papers
review process to avoid evaluation apprehension.
Finally, the search string shown in section 3.3.1 enabled making this SMS feasible at this stage, as it focused
on the search of papers fully aligned with AIDOaRt research interest (in the sense of being likely to consider
the three research axes). However, it also introduced a bias in the search for works answering any of the three
former research questions RQ1, RQ2, and RQ3, involving only two of the three axes.
5.2Internal Validity
Internal validity threats relate to poor settings impacting the study design [ 66]. Exclusion and randomisation
have been included to address the threats to internal validity related to the confounding variables (a typical
potential source of bias in one empirical study). For the exclusion criteria, we have selected papers based on the
three research areas (MDE, DevOps, AI/ML) by excluding ones that, for example, (i) have not been published
(e.g., Bachelor, Master, and PhD thesis) or (ii) published from low ranked journals (higher than Q1/Q2 Scimago)
and conferences (GRIN-GII-SCIE).
We have selected papers targeting at least two of the three research areas (i.e., we excluded tool papers
mentioning only 1 of the 3 research areas, i.e., MDE, AI/ML, and DevOps). We intend to further improve such
classification of the papers by refining the selection based on their contribution to the three research areas. As
shown in Figure 5 and Figure 6, we have selected papers that both propose well-assessed techniques for MDE,
AI/ML, and DevOps and adopt their basic principles.
Moreover,toavoidthewell-knownproblemofselectionbias,wehaveusedarandomsampleofpotentialsearch
strings (for all three research areas). We have also addressed issues related to the ambiguity and incompleteness

6Conclusion
This paper presents an overview of state-of-the-art research approaches and solutions that rely on different
synergies of MDE, AI/ML, and DevOps to support various software and systems engineering activities. Because
of the large number of available resources from the literature, we performed the study and presented the obtained
results as a Systematic Mapping Study (SMS). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first SMS to address all the
synergies between the three areas considered.
As a consequence, the main objectives of our SMS are multiple: 1) Identify software and systems engineering
methodologies or approaches that explicitly combine principles and techniques of MDE, AI/ML, and DevOps,
and explain how they are currently combined mostly two by two. 2) Study which software and systems life cycle
steps are concerned by these methodologies and approaches, and to what extent. 3) Further analyze the particular
research fields and application domains targeted by these methodologies and approaches. 4) Extend this analysis
by providing a mapping between corresponding tools and targeted engineering activities, highlighting their role
within the identified MDE, AI/ML, and DevOps synergies. 5) Propose relevant future research directions for the
software and systems engineering community to explore, notably related to integrating these three considered
areas.
The results of our SMS show that few approaches explore the synergies between MDE, AI/ML, and DevOps
to support software and systems engineering. However, many approaches already combine two of these areas,
specifically MDE and DevOps. We also highlighted the increasing frequency of approaches exploring synergies
between AI/ML techniques with MDE or DevOps. The selected approaches generally cover various activities,
such as requirements engineering, modeling, coding, testing, and monitoring, to different extents. This is realized
thanks to corresponding tools, which mostly focus on such two-by-two synergies for particular engineering
activities in specific application domains (even though some identified tools aim to be more generic). Still,
many research challenges remain open. Potential research directions notably include pushing further the actual
integration of AI/ML with MDE and DevOps. They also include supporting more scalable and data-oriented
approaches, proposing new global methodologies for continuous software and systems engineering, and adapting
DevOps practices more efficiently to different systems (e.g., IoT- and/or Cloud-based, Digital Twins).
From our side, we intend to continue improving regularly the SMS to address additional threats to validity
(related, for example, to results generalization) and integrate new relevant scientific papers whenever they
are published. This SMS has been designed to be an open/live artefact, shared with the software and systems
engineering community, to be possibly refined and updated by different interested researchers. Additionally, we
plan to gain further insights by conducting developer questionnaires and incorporating the findings into the
current study to assess the applicability of our SMS results for industry practitioners.
