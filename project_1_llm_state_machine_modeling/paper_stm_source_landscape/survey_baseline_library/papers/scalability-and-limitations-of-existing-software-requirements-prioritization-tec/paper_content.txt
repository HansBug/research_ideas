ABSTRACT
Requirements prioritization puts more emphasis on the software requirements based on their importance, making it a crucial
activity in managing software requirements throughout the software development process. This work explains the concept of
prioritization techniques, their relevance, and related issues. Recent literature has described many requirement prioritization
techniques, but each comes with its own limitations and challenges, specifically regarding scalability. In this article, we provide a
systematic literature review (SLR) of the requirement prioritization techniques over the past decade and identify their limitations
and scalability issues. In this context, we develop a planned protocol that includes all the necessary steps required for the SLR
process. As a result of conducting the SLR, 53 primary studies were shortlisted for data extraction. The analysis of the results
indicates that there is a minimal focus on large-  scale functional requirements, with only 9% of the work addressing this area. To
the best of our knowledge, no work has been done on prioritizing requirements between ERP systems and mobile-  specific appli -
cations. Most existing studies focus on imaginary or simulated projects, and very limited actual work has been carried out during
the execution of the industrial projects. When requirements are prioritized correctly, the likelihood of successful implementation
will be high, and it will lead to a higher quality product.
1   |   Introduction
Requirement engineering (RE) can be defined as a software
engineering (SE) process that focuses on understanding how
the software requirements can be acquired systematically and
appropriately from or on behalf of the users [ 1]. There are four
types of requirements: business requirements (BRs), user re -
quirements (URs), system requirements (SRs), and process re -
quirements (PRs). BRs focus on the goals and objectives of the
system, including profit and benefits delivered by the system
to the clients or the business. URs represent the user and the
organization requirements that need to be incorporated in the
development of the product. SRs are typically subdivided into
two main categories, namely, functional requirements (FRs) and nonfunctional requirements (NFRs). FRs are those require -
ments that are basic or must-  have requirements for the software
system, ranging from a few to potentially thousands [ 2]. On the
contrary, NFRs refer to the quality characteristics of the software
that impact the performance, dependability, security, productiv -
ity, precision, accessibility, transferability, and compatibility of
the software systems [ 3]. PRs cover a wide range of criteria such
as cost control, development cost, time, customer satisfaction,
competitive advantage, time for development, marketing style,
quality of the constructed system, rate of production, and com -
pliance with standards [ 4].
Software requirements prioritization (RP) gives significance
or importance to one requirement over another; assigning

ful implementation of a project. The prioritization of require -
ments is an essential process for SE where different criteria, such
as business value, cost, and stakeholder preferences, determine
the ordering of software requirements. A proper requirement
prioritization (RP) process gives priority to fundamental needs
first, which minimizes resource waste and enhances software
quality [ 5]. Practitioners have advocated the employment of sev -
eral techniques, including pairwise comparison technique, cost
value approaches, and machine learning methods. The efficacy
of these techniques highly depends on the project's scale and the
degree of its measure [ 6]. Once the URs are collected and SRs are
listed, a process called requirement management (RM) is initi -
ated. RM is a systematic and structured approach of recording,
planning, evaluating the requirements, managing changes to
the requirements, and communicating those changes back to the
stakeholders. Although RM is an ongoing process throughout a
project, it becomes especially critical during the final phase of
software design and development. The priority has played a sig -
nificant role in requirements management as software develop -
ment has become more sophisticated in recent years [ 7]. Often,
when it comes to implementing a mammoth software solution
like the ERP system, the requirements are equally vast. When
the stakeholder aims to meet all the requirements that are set
within the available time and funds, it becomes imperative to
prioritize these requirements [ 8, 9]. This process of prioritizing
the requirements is particularly important because of the fact
that all the types of requirements are interrelated to each other.
This RP becomes even more critical in the scenario of a complex
system where many stakeholders and users have differences in
perceptions of the requirement priorities [ 8].
Scalability in RP refers to the ability of employing prioritiza -
tion techniques to a wide range of software requirements as a
project grows in size without compromising its performance.
This issue becomes particularly important in the context of
software projects that have large numbers of requirements
exceeding thousands in many cases and needs to be properly
captured and prioritized [ 10]. Traditional ranking methods,
such as AHP and numerical assignment, face scalability chal -
lenges because of performance issues and computing con -
straints. To address these limitations, machine learning and
automatic methods have been employed that not only improve
quality but also provide faster processing in large-  scale pri -
oritization processes [ 11]. To enable efficient incorporation
of prioritization techniques in large-  scale prioritization pro -
cesses, several techniques have been developed that use both
quantitative and qualitative approaches for prioritization.
These hybrid methods of decision making combine operations
research on multiple criteria with artificial intelligence. In
addition, some of the prior works advocate the use of prepro -
cessing techniques such as clustering and filtering to enhance
the efficiency of RP. There have also been interesting develop -
ments in the automation of requirement classification using
natural language processing (NLP) [ 12].
RP deals with the manner in which the requirements should
be implemented. This prioritization typically stems from the
stakeholder's perspective (URs) or the system perspectives
(FRs and NFRs). The RP process is typically divided into three
phases: (i) the planning phase in which the requirements are arranged and prerefurbished for prioritization, (ii) the imple -
mentation phase in which actual prioritization occurs, and
(iii) the documentation phase where the findings of the RP
process are captured and disseminated [ 13]. There are two ap -
proaches to RP [ 13]: The first approach prioritizes the require -
ments based on the preferences of the stakeholders through
negotiations, and the second technique prioritizes the require -
ments based on the decisions made by the software experts.
On completion of the prioritization process, it is possible to
compare both sets of requirements in terms of rank and im -
portance [ 14].
Although a significant amount of work has been done in the RP,
several challenges still exist. Many state-  of- the- art techniques
are suitable for BRs, PRs, and URs. However, none of these
techniques effectively address the dependency issues of FRs
while prioritizing PRs. Besides the importance, very little work
has been carried out to prioritize the requirements of complex
software systems like enterprise resource planning (ERP). This
article presents an SLR process aimed at analyzing existing RP
techniques, highlighting their advantages and limitations. To
address the scalability issues of existing techniques, we need to
identify how well these techniques scale in handling and prior -
itizing all types of requirements. Identifying these limitations
will enable the researchers to fill the gap in the RP. The rest of
the paper is structured as follows: Section  2 focuses on the lit -
erature review, Section  3 describes the research methodology,
Section  4 provides the results, and Section  5 discusses the obser -
vations resulting from the SLR, followed by the conclusion and
future work in Section 6 .
2   |   Background Study
The most prominent and widely used technique for RP is
the analytical hierarchy process (AHP) technique, which
performs pair-  wise comparison between all requirements.
This technique requires a total of n  (n − 1)/2 comparisons
for n requirements and is used to prioritize every new re -
quirement to its maximum level. For n  = 10, AHP will per -
form 45 comparisons of the requirements. As the number of
requirements (i.e., n ) increases, the time that it requires to
process them increases drastically. In the case where the re -
quirements size is in thousands, there will be a minimum of
1000  * (1000  − 1)/2 = 499,500 comparisons, which would take
a lot of time, making it hard to implement. Therefore, the
scalability issue of the AHP technique makes it unsuitable for
complex systems with large requirements because of the pair -
wise comparison for each requirement [ 15]. The framework
in [16] categorizes the requirements as a function of benefit
and cost that depicts the requirement interrelationships. This
study introduces six approaches to handling dependencies.
First, it assigns fixed values to costs and benefits for each
requirement. Second, it groups requirements independently
to handle complicated problems during actual calculations.
Third, it requires the benefits to be tangible (e.g., dollars or
time in hours). Fourth, it performs a pairwise comparison.
Finally, it employs the use of discrete values (e.g., 1, 2, and 3)
instead of continuous values. The main drawback of existing
conventional prioritization methods is that they are unable to
deal with the complexity, ambiguity, and inconsistency issues

parameters are determined. The author explained the binary
tree during the RP using an algorithm. Requirements are first
ordered, followed by the construction of a binary tree. After
that, it employs a sorting method to enable prioritization either
in ascending or descending order. This method is difficult to
apply in practice. However, it is very useful because there are
fewer comparisons compared with AHP. Thus, this technique
transpires more effectively for projects that fall under the me -
dium as well as large size of requirements [ 19]. However, the
main issue with this technique is that the methodology only
categorizes and sorts the requirements based on their priority
but does not give a value to the requirements. Other studies
on RP that utilize machine learning algorithms to prioritize a
large set of FRs fail to incorporate dependency factors for all
the FRs [ 20].
In addition to prioritizing FRs, the literature also rendered
works on the priority of NFRs. In the work [ 21, 22 ], the author
described three strategies for prioritization of NFRs. The first
strategy treats the set of nonfunctional with the highest priority,
as well as a set of FRs. The second strategy prioritizes the NFRs
in the manner that they are considered more important than the
FRs. The third approach involves prioritizing the NFRs as a sep -
arate list, though on a functional basis. It was evident from the
experiments that the third approach is the most efficient, fol -
lowed by the second approach, which is more effective than the
first approach.
Previous research works [ 23–29 ] have performed systematic
literature reviews (SLRs) on RP. However, none of them have
identified prioritization methods that are suitable for specific
types and sizes of requirements. In addition, these works have
not discussed techniques that use both types and sizes of re -
quirements using SL. Also, our research includes some more
analysis factors that are not raised and discussed in the pre -
vious SLR.
3   |   Research Methodology
The design framework for the proposed SLR is illustrated in
Figure  1. As recommended by previous studies [ 7, 30–33 ], we
have followed a structured process. This process begins with
posing the research questions, followed by determining search strategies, assessing and quantifying quality assessment crite -
ria, and finally, integrating the collected data.
3.1   |   Research Questions
It is worth mentioning that formulating the research questions
is the first step of the research design process. These research
questions guide the approach used in the research to produce
appropriate answers. This is followed by establishing research
objectives to answer the posed questions. In this research, the
research questions are formulated as follows:
RQ1:  What are the available techniques in the literature to pri -
oritize software requirements of any type?
RQ2:  What are the limitations of the current RP techniques,
and to much extent the existing RP techniques are scalable to
prioritize a large set of requirements?

The search strategy is a key step in conducting an SLR. In this
study, the search strategy is divided into the following three
steps based on the objectives and the formulated research
questions.

6   |   Conclusions
The purpose of this empirical study was to determine the scal -
ability of current prioritization techniques to address the issue
of software requirements and to explore their limitations and
shortcomings. To achieve these goals, some of the research
questions were refined based on an SLR. This was done by
searching, assessing, and interpreting key and related articles.
This research intended to identify areas of possible improve -
ment through the methodical review of prior and related work
on RP techniques as outlined in the literature. For this particu -
lar research study, a review protocol was developed and agreed
upon to guide the research. As part of this research, some as -
sessment questions or standards of quality were created and
utilized in the existing work to determine its significance and
perfection. This review protocol outlines each step of the re -
view process that includes research identification, study se -
lection, assessment of study quality, data extraction, and data
management. These steps were performed according to the re -
view protocol. During these changes, trends and related major
studies were conducted, and the quality of these studies was
assessed. After selecting various study types, information was
then extracted from suitable papers. As a result, the research
aim of the study was achieved, and the questions addressed in
the paper were answered. This research successfully identified
studies that addressed previous limitations and proposed im -
provements in prioritization techniques. Although a variety of
prioritization methods are available, our findings suggest that
further progress is still needed.
Key areas for enhancement include scalability, rigorous cal -
culations, ease of use, consistency, the degree of trusting the
result, industrial acceptance of the results, and conflicts of
requirements.
