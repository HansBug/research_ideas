Failures in systems that can lead to loss of life, property, and environmental damage, make them safety–
critical systems requiring the analysis and demonstration of dependability properties. When those systems
can connect/disconnect to each other, it raises security issues, making them also security-critical. Safety and
security are crucial in safety- and security-critical design. The complexity of a connected world impacts safety
and security, requiring dependability assurance processes in compliance with standards like ISO 26262, ISO
21434, IEC 61511, and IEC 61508. Model-Driven Engineering (MDE) plays a significant role by using models
to represent and analyze safety and security properties, facilitating their integration. This study aims to explore
the role of MDE in supporting safety and security co-engineering through a Systematic Literature Review. We
identified 119 studies that were analyzed and categorized based on the life-cycle phase, risk assessment activity,
application domain, methodology type (integrated or unified), methodology goal (co-engineering or cross-
fertilization), measurement (qualitative, quantitative, or both), modeling approach, representation, evaluation
method, and supporting tools. Though model-driven contributions are less prevalent than model-based ones,
tools widely support them. Studies primarily focus on transportation (especially automotive) and industrial
domains, but there is potential for broader participation with increasing IoT adoption.

1. Introduction
Safety and security  are important  quality  attributes  of any software
system  (Kenner  et al.,2021 ), especially  in the context  of safety–critical
domains  (e.g., automotive,  aerospace,  manufacturing,  and medical  de-
vices Knight ,2002 ), in which faults and failures  that cause harm to
the system,  its users, or to the environment  must be avoided.  Safety
is the absence  of unreasonable  risk of physical  harm to people  (serious
injuries  or loss of life), significant  damage  to the property,  and environ-
mental  damage  due to system  malfunctioning  behavior,  and its correct
operation  in response  to user’s inputs (IEC,2010 ).
Security,  on the other hand, is the prevention  of unauthorized
access from malicious  external  agents  aiming  to change  or damage
equipment,  information,  or services  (IEC,2009 ). For example,  au-
tonomous  cars must ensure  safe operation,  preventing  accidents  and
physical  injuries  to people  (Dajsuren  and van den Brand ,2019 ); cloud
computing  systems  and services  connected  to the Internet  must ensure
data confidentiality,  integrity,  and availability  (Dillon et al.,2010 );
and cyber–physical  systems  should  guarantee  the safety of involved
humans  (Biffl et al.,2019 ).

A security vulnerability is an internal system failure caused by
faults in the design (e.g., unintended feature interactions), devel-
opment (e.g., buffer overflow), or operation (e.g., configuration er-
ror) (McGraw ,2006 ). Thus, if the assurance of security properties is
imperative, then safety–critical systems can also be security-critical1.
However, safety and security are frequently addressed separately (Kruck
et al.,2021 ;Kenner et al.,2021 ). While the malfunctioning behavior
is addressed by safety engineering, the increasing risk of intentionally
caused harm is addressed by security engineering (Lisova et al.,2019 ).
But, with the increased connectivity between heterogeneous systems,
safety and security must be integrated to properly address the unrea-
sonable risk of harm due to malfunctioning behavior or malicious intent
from external agents (Lisova et al.,2019 ).
Since separating safety and security risk analysis is no longer feasi-
ble (Biro et al.,2017 ), we need methods that deal with them simulta-
neously because, in some situations, safety and security requirements
may conflict with each other. For example, encrypting a message for
security reasons may increase the delivery time to a value greater than
the required threshold to address safety. Moreover, existing standards
address safety and security separately. For instance, in the automotive
domain, ISO 26262 (ISO,2011 ) addresses functional safety, while ISO
21434 (ISO,2021 ) focus on cyber-security.
Another important concern is that treating safety and security sepa-
rately and dealing with their integration later at the development stage
implies a greater effort to identify inconsistencies between conflicting
safety and security requirements and common measures. Thus, the
late detection of inconsistencies demands repetitive iterations between
safety and security engineering to solve them. The early integration of
safety and security aiming to detect issues and trade-offs may reduce
the number of iterations to identify common measures (Urretavizcaya
et al.,2021 ). Both disciplines use an acyclic graph to describe the
propagation of failures/attacks across a system model. While safety
describes fault and error propagation across a system model using Fault
Tree Analysis (FTA), security considers attack paths and their links
to threats and security properties, like the CIA Triad, which stands
for Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability, and goals using Attack
Trees.
The design of critical systems demands the management of inter-
actions between safety and security requirements that can be conflict-
ing. In this context, model-based and model-driven approaches can
be employed to support the integrated analysis of security concerns
(e.g., expressed in attack trees) and their impact on system safety
properties using FTAs. While Model-Based Engineering (MBE) assists
the development, Model-Driven Engineering (MDE) is a Software En-
gineering paradigm in which models are primary artifacts, driving the
system development (Selic,2003 ). The benefits of automation and
consistency from MDE have been explored in the last decade in both
academia and industry to support the integration and analysis of the
interplay between safety and security engineering models.
In this paper, we present a Systematic Literature Review (SLR)
(Kitchenham ,2004 ) to investigate the current state of the art of mod-
eling approaches, namely model-based or model-driven, for safety and
security co-analysis. We aim to get a better understanding of existing
modeling approaches to support the analysis of the interplay between
safety and security. To achieve this goal, we identified the domains
where such techniques are applied, their principles, how they were
evaluated, and whether they have tooling support.
Based on the analysis of 119 selected publications, our findings
revealed several gaps concerning the analysis of safety and security
interplay while using modeling approaches. Those gaps include few

2. Background
In this section, we provide the key background concepts on model-
based and model-driven engineering, safety and security engineering
disciplines, formal methods, and the interplay between safety and
security necessary for a better understanding of the SLR results.
2.1. Modeling approaches
Models are abstractions from reality that can represent it in a partial
or simplified way. In the software industry, ‘‘these models are used
to exchange information between engineers to, for example, further refine
requirements, implement software/systems/architecture, including software
deployment’’ (Nigam et al.,2018 ). In this study, we adopted the model-
ing approaches classification scheme defined byBrambilla et al.(2012 ).
We distinguish between model-driven and model-based approaches.
Model-driven is an approach where models are used as the pri-
mary artifact of the development process. Usually, the resulting im-
plementation is semi or automatically generated from those models.
This approach has several acronyms, such as Model-Driven Develop-
ment, Model-Driven Engineering, Model-Driven Software Engineering,
and Model-Driven Architecture. Still, they all share the same line of
thought.
Model-Based approaches, on the other hand, are a softer version of
model-driven approaches. ‘‘The MBE process is a process in which software
models play an important role although they are not necessarily the key
artifacts of the development (i.e., they do NOT ‘‘drive’’ the process as in
MDE)’’ (Brambilla et al.,2012 ). For the remainder of this paper, aiming
to achieve a more consistent and straightforward reading, we use the
acronyms: MD for model-driven and MB for model-based approaches.
2.2. Safety
According toDraeger (2015 ), safety is typically concerned with
avoiding adverse effects of the system on its environment. Safety issues
are usually related to damage to people, property, and the environment.
As mentioned in Section 1, FTA and FMEA are the first techniques
for safety analysis, and they date from 1960 (Hecht et al.,2020 ;Nai
Fovino et al.,2009 ). FTA is a tree-based top-down analysis and the last
is a bottom-up supported by spreadsheets. There are also other relevant
techniques like System Theoretic Process Analysis (STPA) (Castiglione
and Lupu ,2020 ), which defines the system control structure and iden-
tifies its hazardous control actions; Hazard and Operability Analysis
(HAZOP) (Friedberg et al.,2017 ), which uses guidewords over system
parameters to identify deviations from the designed behavior; and
Hazard Analysis and Risk Assessment (HARA), widely used in the auto-
motive domain, which classifies hazards considering severity, exposure,
and controllability (Martin et al.,2020 ).The Journal  of Systems  & Software  220 (2025)  112251

2.3. Security
Security issues are mainly related with risk to the availability,
confidentiality, and integrity of systems threatened by intentional or
unintentional attacks. Those issues can be physical or virtual. As men-
tioned byAbdo et al.(2018 ), the increasing usage of the Internet for
connecting, remote controlling, and supervising systems and facilities
also increases the risk related to cybersecurity.
Security also has its own techniques (e.g. STRIDE Hernan et al.,
2006 ), some of which were based on safety analog techniques. AT,
already mentioned in Section 1, is analogous to FTA. The E-Safety
Vehicle Intrusion Protected Applications (EVITA) project proposed the
Threat and Operability Analysis based on the well-known HAZOP
method, which uses guide-words for the analysis (Dürrwang et al.,
2017 ). The Threat Analysis and Risk Assessment (TARA) method is
based on Common Criteria (Cybersecurity & Infraestructure Security
Agency ,2006 ) and EVITA, and it is very similar to HARA. The TARA
method considers the expertise, time, knowledge, access, and resources
needed to make an attack feasible (Förster et al.,2019 ).
2.4. Formal methods
Modeling approaches use structural, semiformal, or formal machine-
readable models instead of non-structured word processing documents
(Papakonstantinou et al.,2019 ). According toBrunel et al.(2014 ),
formal techniques (i.e., techniques using formal models), can be used
to support safety and security assessment due to their mathematical
foundation, which allows them to prove some requirements with higher
confidence than testing and manual review. Thus, using formalisms can
reduce or even eliminate possible defects in the software, maintaining
the system’s safety (Howard et al.,2017 ). Although we recognize the
important role formal methods play in modeling approaches, they are
out of the scope of this SLR, and another work should be conducted to
address it.
2.5. Safety and security interplay
As discussed in Section 1and previous Sections 2.2and2.3, both
disciplines are indispensable for acquiring a completely safe and secure
system, although a new challenge emerges when dealing with them in
the same software development process. The expertise required for en-
gineers to deal with those disciplines remains split, but the components
and connections that are assessed by safety and security analysis are the
same. Therefore, an effort must be made to align them.
According toSabaliauskaite and Mathur (2015 ), a weak align-
ment between those disciplines may produce inefficient development
and partially-protected systems. We can accomplish excessive costs
by defining redundant countermeasures or degradation of the whole
safety of the system by adopting mechanisms that increase safety and
decreases security at the same time, and vice-versa. Safety and security
can have different kinds of interplay:
•No Interference : Properties from one discipline do not interfere
with the other (e.g., requiring Multi-Factor Authentication for an
accounting system might not endanger safety, even though the
data is related to an industry where there are several safety–
critical systems);
•Reinforcement : Properties from both disciplines share the same
failure or attack paths and affect the same components (e.g. an
attacker can disable a car brake system through an intrusion or
the brake system can fail internally, resulting in the same type of
accident);
•Mutual exclusion : Properties from one discipline negatively af-
fect the properties from another discipline (e.g. adding cryptog-
raphy to a network connection will add security but will increase
latency and that can affect a safety property);3. Conducting the systematic literature review
The main goal of this SLR is to identify safety and security co-
analysis methodologies using model-driven and model-based
approaches and how they deal with the interplay between those disci-
plines, the domains they were applied in both academia and industry,
and their tooling support. Having this goal, this SLR was planned,
conducted, and reported following well-known review guidelines found
inKitchenham et al.(2015 ) and Petersen et al.(2015 ). The SLR
planning and conduction are detailed in the following.
3.1. Planning
This SLR addresses five Research Questions (RQ), which are pre-
sented as follows, along with the rationale and metrics for each RQ.
•RQ1. Which studies propose model-based (MB) or model-driven
(MD) approaches to address safety and security concerns in the
system engineering process?
Rationale : Understanding the existing model-based and model-
driven methodologies that address the co-analysis of safety and
security concerns in systems is crucial for advancing the field.
This knowledge helps to identify the strengths and limitations of
current approaches, fostering the development of new methodolo-
gies and tools, and it provides the foundations for future research.
Insights into the nature of these studies (i.e., whether they consist
in an approach, case study, comparative study, framework, lan-
guage, metamodel, method, or a pattern) are essential for guiding
research efforts and practical applications.
Metrics :
–The number of safety and security co-analysis methodolo-
gies using models (MB approaches) and treating models as
primary artifacts (MD approaches).
–The number of safety and security co-analysis method-
ologies categorized by year and by type of contribution
(approach, case study, comparative study, framework, lan-
guage, metamodel, method, pattern).
•RQ2. For which domains, life cycle phases, and risk assessment

(i.e., identification, analysis, control) where safety and security
co-analysis methodologies have been proposed and applied is
vital for understanding their practical relevance and applicability.
Additionally, it is important to identify if industry standards
are highlighted. This information provides valuable insights into
the effectiveness of these methodologies in various contexts, life
cycle phases of system development, and risk assessment. For
the research community, it highlights areas of success and gaps
that need further exploration, thus guiding future research and
development efforts in creating more robust and versatile safety
and security co-analysis methodologies and tools.
Metrics :

•RQ4. How was the methodology proposed by the studies vali-

Rationale : Understanding the validation methods employed by the
studies is crucial for assessing the reliability and applicability of
the proposed methodologies. This includes identifying whether
validation was conducted through comparative studies, empirical
studies, expert evaluations, or other methods. Such insights are
important for the research community to assess the robustness
of these methodologies and to identify best practices for future

Metrics :
–Number and types of studies related to the validation of

•RQ5. What are the tools used to support the proposed studies?
Rationale : Identifying the tools used to support the methodologies
proposed by the studies helps in understanding their practical ap-

From the analysis of the research questions, we identified the fol-
lowing relevant keywords: safety analysis, security analysis, model-
driven engineering, and model-based engineering. We combined these
terms using logical operators (AND, OR) to obtain the following search
string.
("safety analysis" AND "security analysis" AND ("model-driven
engineering" OR "model-based engineering"))
We avoided using abbreviations like ‘‘MDE’’ for model-driven engi-
neering or ‘‘MBE’’ for model-based engineering. Since the abbreviation
is not supposed to appear without its meaning, all papers retrievedwith ‘‘model-driven engineering’’ will also be retrieved with ‘‘MDE’’,
but the opposite is not true. Moreover, in this study we focused on iden-
tifying safety and security co-analysis methodologies using modeling
approaches. However, the term ‘‘analysis’’ is not adopted unanimously
in the literature. We mitigated this problem by conducting a manual
search step described in Section 3.2.
We aim to find studies that address safety and security analysis by
employing a MB or MD approach. However, it is important to notice
that not all authors use these terms in the same way, thus we have to
provide our analysis before categorizing the study so that the results
respect the definitions we have provided in Section 2.1. We applied
this search string to identify studies in the following databases: ACM
Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, Scopus, and SpringerLink.
This search string had to be adapted for each database.
We also defined the inclusion criteria (IC) to identify relevant stud-
ies from each database and exclusion criteria (EC) to exclude studies
that do not add relevant knowledge to this SLR. A study must meet at
least one IC to be included, and it is excluded if it meets at least one
EC. The inclusion and exclusion criteria used were:
•IC1: The study reports a methodology based on or driven by
models to support the analysis of the interplay between safety and
security concerns.
•IC2: The study proposes tool support for safety and security
co-analysis.
•IC3: The study presents a case study describing the usage of a
methodology or tool to perform safety and security analysis in
an application domain.
•EC1: The study does not propose contributions based on or driven
by models.
•EC2: The study does not propose a methodology or tool for safety
or security co-analysis.
•EC3: The study does not provide enough detailed information
about the methodology applied, which makes it impossible to
apply the Data Extraction Form.
•EC4: The study was written in another language than English.
•EC5: The study addresses (proposes or uses) a methodology or
tool that addresses both safety and security, but another more
complete study related to the same methodology exists.
•EC6: The full text of the study was not available through the
author’s institutional access, and there was no response from the
authors contacted via email.
•EC7: The study is not peer-reviewed or only available as grey
literature (Kamei et al.,2021 ).
•EC8: The study must be published in the last 12 years.
•EC9: The study must be at least four pages long.

studies  use a method,  approach,  or technique  defined  in other works

5. Discussion
In this section, we discuss several aspects that emerged from the
analysis of the SLR results.
5.1. Process-based versus component-based methodologies
After observing the results of the 119 selected studies, two groups
of methodologies were identified: process-based and component-based.
In the context of this work, process-based methodologies consider the
data flow and how the system interacts with the physical environment.
The following process-based methodologies were identified in this
SLR: (i) STAMP S&S (Kaneko et al.,2020 ;Kaneko and Yoshioka ,2020 );
(ii) STPA-SafeSec (Friedberg et al.,2017 ); (iii) Systems-Theoretic Likeli-
hood and Severity Analysis (Temple et al.,2017 ); and (iv) Uncontrolled
Flows of Information and Energy (Carreras Guzman et al.,2021a ) as
process-based methodologies.
In the same way, component-based methodologies focus on smaller
pieces of the system, and the results can be merged for an artifact
representing the entire system. The Attack Tree BowTie (Abdo et al.,
2018 ), Attack-Fault Trees (Kumar and Stoelinga ,2017 ), Parametric
weighted timed automata (Andre et al.,2019 ), Pattern Engineering
Lifecycle (Amorim et al.,2017 ), and Failure Mode, Vulnerabilities and
Effect Analysis (FMVEA) (Schmittner et al.,2014b ) are some of those
methodologies.
5.2. Modeling approaches
As presented in Section 4.1, we identified 76 primary studies of MB
methodologies against 43 MD methodologies. This represents 76,74%
more studies on MBE than in MDE. Another fact we have detected is
that primary studies proposing MD methodologies addressed less risk
assessment activities (RAA) than MB methodologies, as presented in
Table 6. A possible justification for this is the effort needed to develop
meta-models, implement changes to existing modeling tools, or even
develop a brand-new tool to support the proposed meta-model and
methodology. Only 25 tools were cited along with MB methodologies,
against 68 tools cited by MD methodologies. More about tooling is
presented in Section 5.6.
MDE proposes the reuse of modeling artifacts for code generation
and model transformations. This facilitates the achievement of lower
development costs in the long term by increasing the efficiency of
engineers in safety and security analysis. It is worth mentioning that
the initial effort, cost, and learning curve can be higher when applying
MDE, and it can configure itself as an obstacle to be overcome.
5.3. Application domain
Safety and security are more applicable to critical systems, such as
transportation and industrial domains. The answer to our RQ2 (pre-
sented in Section 4.2) showed the majority presence of those domains
in primary studies. That does not mean exclusivity since 37 primary
studies (31,09%) addressed other domains. Safety and security con-
cerns are becoming widely used, but there is still room for addressing
other application domains other than transportation and industrial.

and those domains. Supporting the Automotive domain, there is the
already mentioned ISO 26262 (presented in Section 4.2regardingTable 5

when applied  to systems  where non-safety-related  security  issues are
important.  In such a case, security  analysis  would  have to be performed

Regarding  the maturity  of safety and security  disciplines,  the first
study that proposed  an approach  with aSafety-informed  Security goal

the most common  strategies  start with a safety analysis  and increment
it with security  concerns  or consider  both from the beginning.  Secu-
rity analysis  will likely achieve  better maturity  and become  another
possible  starting  point for assessing  critical  systems  in the following
years.
This SLR has identified  several  distinct  approaches,  methods,  tech-
niques,  and processes,  but another  relevant  aspect  of safety and security
co-analysis  is worth mentioning:  community  acceptance.  Since safety
and security  have been treated  separately  (see Sections 1and2.5),
security  and safety analysts  also work individually  and know too little
about the other discipline.  The work ofKruck et al.(2021 ) is among  the
identified  studies  and tackles  this issue by proposing  a MB workflow
that allows  safety and security  analysts  to learn about propagation
considerations  of the respective  other discipline.  The workflow  was
created  over a metamodel  and gives suggestions  to analysts  instead  of
running  an analysis  at the tool’s backend  and outputting  a result that
might not need to be clarified  to all analysts.
5.6. Tooling
As presented  in Section 4.5, the majority  of tools support  both
disciplines  with native features,  such as TTool,  which is based on the
SysML-Sec  methodology  (Zhao et al.,2019 ); CMSS,  a prototype  tool
created  by the authors  of the study (Liu et al.,2021 ); STAMP-based
Ontology  Tool (SOT),  also created  by authors  for this purpose  (Pereira
et al.,2019 ); and PolarSys  OpenCert.3(Javed et al.,2021 ) This identi-
fied majority  highlights  the importance  of tools that approximate  safety
and security  disciplines  when conducting  their co-analysis.

tensions,  such as CHESS,4improved  with SafeConcert  (Gallina  and
Haider ,2020 ); Safety Architect,5which supports  security  with an exten-

Some primary  studies  defined  their methodology  around  tools,
which can use general-purpose  tools for system  design,  behavior  mod-

examples  are presented  byKriaa et al.(2015a ,2019 ), which use KB3
Workbench,  Figaro  language,8and Figseq  in their S-Cube  approach;  and
byMartinez  et al.(2020 ), which uses Safety Architect,  Cyber Architect,9
and Concept-aware  analysis  library  in their interference  analysis.  A
very present  combination  in primary  studies  uses a modeling  tool
that supports  dependability  properties  (e.g., TTool and CMSS),  along
with UPPAAL  and ProVerif  as backend  verification  tools for safety and
security,  respectively.
Some studies  accomplish  safety and security  co-analysis  by using
attacks  as some kind ofexternal  failure . Therefore,  every tool that can
be used for safety now also supports  security.  This approach  may work
but has a conceptual  limitation.  As mentioned  in Section 2.3, an attack
consists  of expertise,  time, knowledge,  access,  and resources  needed  to
make an attack feasible.  Other properties,  such as vulnerabilities,  can
also integrate  the concept  of an attack and can be decomposed  into
several  properties  on their own. Using this approach  may put these rel-
evant properties  aside. This conceptual  limitation  can be overcome  with
more meta-models  that address  safety and security  in completeness.

External Validity . Some publications described their research in great

and security co-analysis. The generalizability of these conclusions to
methodologies that do not use models may be limited. Future research
could explore the applicability of our conclusions in non-model-based
methodologies to further validate and refine the insights presented in
this study. To overcome this threat we applied the exclusion criteria
EC3, which excludes papers that do not provide detailed information,
and at least two authors assessed all papers.
Internal Validity . Some authors provided ambiguous information
or used concepts in a different way than we did. For example, the
publication uses the term model-based , but in our understanding, it
proposes a model-driven approach. We classified the studies according
to our interpretation. This threat was overcome by the same measures
described in external validity. When the study did not provide enough
detailed information to be part of the results, it was excluded during the
selection steps. Also, all papers were assessed by at least two authors,
which elevates our interpretations to agreement.
7. Related work
Safety and security co-analysis is a relevant topic presented in sev-
eral secondary studies, and it can be enhanced by adopting MDE. Those
studies were our source of inspiration and drove the classifications used
in this current work. Table 8presents these classifications by secondary
study.
7.1. Systematic literature reviews
The work ofLisova et al.(2019 ) is very similar and was our primary
source of inspiration. They researched the most adopted techniques
for co-analysis of safety and security but focused on the requirements
life-cycle phase of software development. Their results showed a need
for more evaluation of the proposed methodologies. This gap was
addressed byPirbhulal et al.(2021 ), who added a specific research
question to understand how the identified techniques in their study
were evaluated, and results showed that a more extended investigation
is necessary to estimate the efficiency and accuracy of those validation
methods. Their work focused only on Graph Theory and Bayesian
Network models. The work ofPekaric et al.(2023 ) is a complete work
specific to self-adaptive systems, categorizing hazard sources, cause
and handling, and attack mechanisms and surfaces. They analyzed the
usage of models regarding safety, security, and architecture.
Our work differs from the previous SLRs on safety and security co-
analysis by removing limitations of scope. There is a model limitation in
the (Pirbhulal et al.,2021 ) study, which focuses only on network mod-
els. A limitation regarding the system type is present inPekaric et al.
(2023 ), since they focus on self-adaptive systems. Finally, the (Lisova
et al.,2019 ) study limited its scope to the requirements life-cycle phase.7.2. Systematic mapping studies
According toPetersen et al.(2015 ),‘‘While systematic reviews aim at
synthesizing evidence, also considering the strength of evidence, systematic
maps are primarily concerned with structuring a research area’’. We found
three systematic mapping studies that also targeted the co-analysis
of safety and security. The work ofKenner et al.(2021 ) focused
on configurable systems, and the results are enumerated as literature
gaps, which provide clear future directions for scientific production.
A mapping study of safety and security analysis methods restricted to
autonomous vehicles is presented byBoyer et al.(2021 ). The study
from Mashkoor et al.(2022 ) is the only mapping study that defined
research questions and aimed to map MDE methods for safety and
security, as we did. Thus, they presented fewer classifications in the
results.
7.3. Comparative studies, reviews and surveys
Other secondary studies extracted relevant information that drove
our classifications (Pietre-Cambacedes and Bouissou ,2013 ;Kriaa et al.,
2015b ;Paul and Rioux ,2015 ;Abulamddi ,2016 ;Chockalingam et al.,
2017 ;Torkildson et al.,2018 ;Kavallieratos et al.,2020 ;Lyu et al.,
2019 ;Oueidat et al.,2020 ). These works are surveys, reviews, and com-
parative studies. Therefore, they did not follow a systematic protocol
like those in SLRs and systematic mapping studies.
A comparison study between FMVEA, CHASSIS, and STPA-Sec
techniques applied to an autonomous boot case study is presented
byTorkildson et al.(2018 ). They evaluated the level of abstraction,
replicability, reusability, scope of analysis, suitability to risk rating, and
adaptability to the context of those techniques. The work ofOueidat
et al.(2020 ) also compared several approaches by applying them to a
beer production system. They evaluated if the approach is generic or
MB, qualitative or quantitative, level of detail, integration principles
between safety and security, software life-cycle phases, and application
domains.
With a focus on CPSs, we can highlight the works from Lyu et al.
(2019 ),Kavallieratos et al.(2020 ), andAbulamddi (2016 ). Other works
focused on safety and security interplay (Kriaa et al.,2015b ;Pietre-
Cambacedes and Bouissou ,2013 ;Paul and Rioux ,2015 ). Finally, the
last related work contributed with a survey relating to safety and
security integration with risk assessment (Chockalingam et al.,2017 ).
In summary, we took advantage of the categorization and limita-
tions presented by 15 secondary studies related to safety and security
co-analysis to define the planning of our SLR. In this SLR, our focus
was on safety and security co-analysis by using modeling approaches.
We addressed all the identified aspects without putting any restrictions
on application domains or types of systems (e.g., CPS or self-adaptive
systems).
8. Conclusion
We performed an SLR to obtain a panorama of the current research
on safety and security co-analysis methodologies with the aid of mod-
els. We identified 119 primary studies by answering the RQs regarding
the interplay between safety and security analysis methodologies, high-
lighting potential future research directions such as model-driven safety
and security methodologies dedicated to formal analysis and verifica-
tion; case studies addressing safety and security co-analysis in other
application domains (e.g., avionics and CPS); secured-informed safety
analysis; and the need for more mature tooling support for safety and
security co-analysis.
We have observed that although the current state-of-the-art offers
practical aid for co-analyzing safety and security using model-driven
methodologies, its adoption rate is lower than expected. Specifically,
