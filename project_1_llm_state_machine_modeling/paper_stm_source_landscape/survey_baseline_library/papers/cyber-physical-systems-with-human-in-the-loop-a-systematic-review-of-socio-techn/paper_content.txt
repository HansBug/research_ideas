Understanding and designing Cyber-physical systems (CPS) with humans in the loop (HITL) is a basic cross-
scientific research problem with large implications for industry. The current software engineering knowledge
already explains how to include the humans in the operation of the machines in terms of interfaces,
architectures, adaptive systems, and design methodologies for including the Human-in-the-Loop. This paper
extends existing knowledge with a systematic review of socio-technical perspectives on CPS with HITL. The
review was software engineering focused, as it searched the body of research on CPS with HITL, and only within
that body, those papers that included socio-technical perspectives. The results indicated four main areas in the
ST literature. Validating these insights by expert interviews with industry CPS experts showed some alignment
and also fundamental differences between the socio-technical literature (ST literature) insights and the industry
experts’ viewpoints. The discussion identifies useful crossings between the ST literature and research into CPS
with HITL adaption, and touch on the issues of non-alignments in industry practice. The conclusion is that
the ST perspectives on the body of knowledge on CPS with HITL has much to offer researchers in terms of
innovative ways to look at the HITL, but the literature needs further development before industry experts can
effectively use it. Future research possibilities are outlined.
1. Introduction
In recent years, Cyber-physical  systems  (CPS) with humans  in the
loop (HITL)  (Nunes et al.,2018 ;Jirgl et al.,2018 ;Samad ,2023 )
have garnered  significant  attention  across various  domains,  marking  a
transformative  step in integrating  computational  processes  with phys-
ical operations.  These systems,  which include  critical  infrastructures
such as built environments  (Bengler  et al.,2024 ), transportation  net-
works (Gil et al.,2019 ), and manufacturing  processes  (Emmanouilidis
et al.,2019 ), leverage  the synergy  between  human  intelligence  and
automated  processes  to enhance  operational  efficiency  and reliability.
However,  as technology  progresses,  the integration  of human  factors
within  CPS has become  increasingly  complex,  necessitating  sophisti-
cated interactions  between  humans  and machines.  This integration  is
crucial  not only for the operational  success  of these systems  but also
for their adaptability  and resilience  in dynamic  environments.
Thus, despite  the advancements  in CPS, a fundamental  challenge
persists:  the diverse  ways of integrating  human  dimensions  and their
conceptualization.  Current  approaches  often prioritize  technological
aspects  without  adequately  considering  the human  operators’  cognitive

2. Background on cyber–physical systems
Cyber-physical systems (CPS) are sophisticated integrations of com-
putational algorithms and physical components, facilitating seamless
interaction between the digital and physical worlds (Greer et al.,2019 ;
Baheti and Gill,2011 ). CPS comprises interconnected networks of sen-
sors, actuators, and computational elements, enabling real-time mon-
itoring, control, and decision-making processes (Moghaddam et al.,
2022b ). CPS often overlooks critical human aspects (Wang et al.,2022 )
integral to their successful integration and operation in human-centered
design and development and use, which considers human factors, er-
gonomics, user experiences in system development, safety, and comfort.
Humans interact extensively with CPS, yet insufficient attention is
given to their cognitive and behavioral requirements (Moghaddam
et al.,2022a ), leading to usability issues, decreased efficiency, and
potential safety issues. CPS may also neglect socio-technical considera-
tions (El-Haouzi et al.,2021 ), such as the impact on societal structures,
human behavior, and ethical implications. For instance, in autonomoustransportation systems, the ethical dilemmas surrounding decision-
making algorithms in critical situations remain largely unaddressed,
posing significant societal acceptance and trust challenges (Reine et al.,
2021 ). Considering such deficiencies, bridging the gap between CPS
and human aspects requires a multidisciplinary approach called Cyber-
physical systems with Human-in-the-Loop (CPS with HITL). The effi-
ciency of the current CPS with HITL suffers from the following issues:
•Incoherent Human-Centric Design and Development: CPS are
often developed with a primary focus on technical functionality
and efficiency, with a brief representation of human needs, capa-
bilities, and limitations, especially in the broader organizational
perspective. This lack of a human-centric approach can lead to
usability issues, cognitive overload, and reduced operator per-
formance, ultimately impacting system effectiveness and safety.
•Non-comprehensive Consideration of Human Factors: Human
factors, encompassing cognitive, behavioral, and ergonomic as-
pects, are essential for ensuring effective human-CPS interaction.
However, CPS often fails to adequately account for these factors,
resulting in suboptimal quality of experiences and potentially
compromising system performance. Factors such as workload,
situational awareness, decision-making processes, and user inter-
faces are crucial considerations that warrant greater attention in
CPS design and operation.
•Limited Incorporation of Socio-Technical Perspectives: CPS
with HITL are complex socio-technical ecosystems, involving in-
teractions between technology, organizations, tasks, and human
users. However, current CPS design and adaptation processes
often lack a holistic socio-technical perspective, leading to chal-
lenges in addressing organizational dynamics, social impacts, and
ethical considerations. Integrating socio-technical insights into
CPS development can enhance system resilience, organizational
readiness, and societal acceptance.
•Insufficient User Involvement: User involvement throughout
the CPS lifecycle, including requirements elicitation, design iter-
ation, and testing phases, is crucial for developing user-friendly
and effective systems. Yet, many CPS projects fail to adequately
engage end-users, resulting in mismatches between system ca-
pabilities and user needs. Incorporating user feedback, usability
testing, and participatory design approaches can bridge this gap
and ensure that CPS meets users’ expectations and requirements.
•Ethical and Societal Implications: CPS decisions and actions
can have significant ethical, legal, and societal implications, yet
these considerations are often sidelined in favor of technical
functionality. Issues such as algorithmic bias, privacy concerns,
and societal impacts require careful attention and deliberation
throughout the CPS lifecycle to ensure responsible and ethically
sound deployment.
•Lack of Adaptive Software and Feedback Mechanisms: CPS
software interfaces often lack adaptability and fail to react in
real-time and provide real-time feedback to users, hindering their
ability to effectively interact with the system. Adaptive interfaces,
context-aware feedback mechanisms, and intuitive user interfaces
are essential for facilitating seamless human-CPS interaction, par-
ticularly in dynamic and uncertain environments.
As the last issue points out, in CPS with HITL, adaptation en-
tails intricate interactions between computational entities and human
stakeholders (operators, programmers, architects, managers, etc.), ne-
cessitating a comprehensive understanding of their dependencies, links,
interactions, and uncertainties. These uncertainties arise from various
sources, including environmental dynamics, infrastructural changes,
coordination complexities, and decision-making processes (Moghaddam
et al.,2022b ).The Journal  of Systems  & Software  226 (2025)  112348

Theenvironmental aspect encompasses the real-world context in
which CPS operates, comprising both physical and virtual elements
within or even beyond the system’s direct control. These contextual
dynamics significantly influence adaptation requirements and neces-

ment, interacting with CPS, thereby introducing additional layers of

Infrastructure adaptation in CPS with HITL involves the reconfigu-
ration of hardware and software components to accommodate changing
operational needs. This entails the dynamic restructuring of sensor
networks, computation resources, and actuators, alongside considera-
tions of quality attributes such as performance, scalability, and security.
While advancements in edge computing enhance CPS capabilities, chal-
lenges persist in ensuring seamless coordination and interoperability
across heterogeneous components.
Coordination in CPS with HITL involves orchestrating interactions
between dynamic infrastructures and evolving environments, including
humans. Embedded distributed systems play a significant role in facil-
itating this coordination, leveraging sensors and actuators to influence
the environment while adapting to contextual changes. Effective coor-
dination strategies mitigate risks associated with system failures and
resource constraints, ensuring operational and interaction resilience.
Decision-making processes in CPS with HITL depend on timely
responses to uncertainties, necessitating proactive or reactive adapta-
tion strategies. Decision methods, including model-based, rule-based,
data-driven, optimization-based, and program-based approaches, guide
adaptation processes based on system characteristics and goals. Proac-
tive adaptation, enabled by (sophisticated) data-driven techniques, is
favored for its ability to preemptively address quality degradation while
respecting humans’ quality of experience.
While existing literature often focuses on technical aspects of CPS
adaptation, recent attention has shifted towards socio-technical per-
spectives such as human-centered approaches e.g., Lou et al.(2024 ),
Liu et al.(2023 ),Lesch et al.(2023 ). Socio-technical systems involve
the intertwined interactions between technology, organization, tasks,
and human users, emphasizing the importance of human-centered de-
sign and development and organizational capacity enhancement. Inte-
grating socio-technical insights into CPS with HITL can enhance system
performance, user experience, and organizational resilience.
3. Method
As discussed below, the overall process can be divided into four
main phases of search, selection, data extraction, and data synthesis.
To achieve the research goal, we arranged for a set of questions along
with their rationale and a definition of the key concept of the review.
3.1. Socio-technical concept and research questions
Socio-technical systems involve the interaction of hard systems
(e.g., machines) and humans in ways that either cannot be sepa-
rated or are thought to be inappropriate to separate (Law,2009 ).
The socio-technical concept emerged from the post-WWII, UK-based
Tavistock institute’s new approach to facilitating social change for
groups of people, by system design. Many of the institute’s staff had
experiences from war time services of personnel selection, resettle-
ment, and psychological warfare during WWII. Many of the staff were
interested in individual psychological issues, since many of the staff
were psychiatrists who believed in the power of deep understanding
of humans, and because they saw human wellbeing as an important
goal of industry practice. These experiences were the background on
which the ‘‘action research’’ of socio-technical approaches developed,
and why empirical analysis and theory development became embed-
ded in organizational and individual change (Mumford ,2006 ). Later
socio-technical system design approaches were build directly on theoriginal Tavistock institute’s approach, with its focus on innovative
and balanced relations between technology, organization, tasks, and IT
users. Today, socio-technical system design is more relaxed theoreti-
cally and less guided by rigorous use of system theory and approaches
that view technology and technology development as mostly technical
issues (Bjørn-Andersen and Clemmensen ,2017 ). On the other hand,
socio-technical design is more human-centered and less participatory in
its ambitions compared to approaches often associated with Scandina-
vian system design (Bannon et al.,2018 ). Socio-technical system design
in its many varieties aims for organizational capacity enhancement
and support managements’ interest, while at the same time caring for
employees’ experiences with human–computer interactions at work in
industry.
On this backdrop, we developed a set of questions that look at CPS
with HITL from social science and humanities point of view and helped
to answer the overall research question: What are the socio-technical
perspectives on CPS with HITL in the literature?
•RQ1.1. What are the socio-technical capacities of CPS with HITL?
•RQ1.2. How could we define a human-centered HITL approach
in CPS.
•RQ1.3. What human–computer interaction technologies could be
used for CPS with HITL?
•RQ1.4. What are dependencies of human work and CPS with
HITL?
In addition to answering the RQs, we validated the insights from
the literature review by using interviews to investigate the practical
and industrial dimensions of CPS with HITL. Here we focused on how
the application of CPS with HITL could be evaluated in practice and
what are the associated challenges. We explain the method and results
of that in Section 5below, but first we present the selection, analysis,
and results of reviewing the literature.
3.2. Search strategy development
A good search strategy is expected to provide practical solutions to
the following questions: which, where, what, and when (Zhang et al.,
2011 ) .
Which Approaches? The search strategy consists of two phases:
(i) automatic search in scientific databases; and (ii) snowballing. The
first step was performed using the search string (see below) based on
keywords from the research questions and areas of study. The search
strings are used to retrieve potential primary studies through web
search engines provided by digital libraries. Snowballing refers to using
the reference list of a paper (backward snowballing) or the citations to
the paper (forward snowballing) to identify additional papers (Wohlin ,
2014 ). The start set for the snowballing procedure is composed of the
selected papers retrieved by the automatic search, namely the primary
studies, which are selected by applying inclusion/exclusion criteria to
the automatic search results. In any case, the inclusion/exclusion crite-
ria will be applied to each paper. If a paper is considered to be included,
snowballing will be applied iteratively, and the procedure ends when
no new papers can be found. In our case, instead of snowballing, we
applied a second search string to systematically focus on the social
science part of the identified software engineering literature on CPS
with HITL (see below).
Where to search? The electronic databases used to search for
relevant literature were ACM, Compendex and Inspec, IEEE and Scopus.
Specifically to include SSH-oriented relevant research AIS e-library was
added. In an experience report based on an SLR conducted in the area
of agile methods, Dyba et al.(2007 ) found that electronic databases
relevant to software engineering research, such as ScienceDirect and
Wiley Science, returned similar search results as ACM, Compendex and
IEEE. Hence, ScienceDirect and Wiley Science databases were excluded
in our review. In addition, Scopus was selected since it claims to be
the largest abstract database for peer-reviewed papers. For the firstThe Journal  of Systems  & Software  226 (2025)  112348

3.3. Selection strategy
3.3.1. Paper selection
The paper selection process included steps for search, cleaning, and
verifying the data set,Fig. 1. The final set of papers included 60 papers.
3.3.2. Data extraction
In this step, we classified the knowledge from selected studies
by analyzing their content, as well as the general classifications in
software engineering domain using keywords. When all studies had
been analyzed, all keywords and concepts were combined to identify
the research’s context, nature, and contribution. We then performed
a qualitative clustering operation to identify a set of representative
attributes regarding specific aspects of CPS with HITL. The resulting
taxonomy is presented in the results section, see also (Clemmensen ,
2025 ).
3.3.3. Data synthesis
This activity involved collecting and summarizing the data extracted
from the selected papers with the goal of understanding, analyzing,
and classifying current research on CPS with HITL dimensions. Data
synthesis was structured in two phases: vertical analysis and horizon-
tal analysis. In vertical analysis data were analyzed individually to
track the trends and collect information of each study. In horizontal
analysis the possible relations across different dimensions and facetsof the research was focused. Based on the literature review step, we
designed a primary framework to design the interviews around its
various dimensions and components.
3.4. Interview study method
Following the expert interview method (Bogner et al.,2009 ), we
recruited CPS experts from a body of industry companies engaged in
the overall research project (see acknowledgment), with the purpose
of doing a systematic interview study to validate the insights from
the literature review. The criteria for selecting experts were that they
should be in a position to put their own interpretations of CPS with
HITL into practice. The interviewers were the first and second authors
together with the aim of ensuring both social science and software
engineering knowledge enough to do the interviews. Details on the
participants and procedure and the derived interview questions are
given below in the interview data section.
4. Analysis of the literature
In this chapter, we answer the question: What are the socio-
technical perspectives on CPS with HITL in the literature?, by present-
ing and discussing relevant concepts extracted from the included body
of 60 selected papers. See the method section for clarification. Fig. 2
summarizes the themes and sub-themes resulting from the literature
review.
4.1. Socio-technical interactions in CPS with HITL (answer to RQ1.1)
The papers in this section discuss the role of humans in Human-in-
the-Loop (HITL) within Cyber-physical systems (CPS) socio-technical
systems. The focus is on the integration and governance of human
roles within CPS with HITL to improve performance and decision-
making. The section outlines how different levels of interaction be-
tween social and technical subsystems create multiple human roles.
Examples include users reacting to changes in CPS, varying trust levels
in interactions, and human-machine collaborations. It also touches
on human-algorithm interactions, where humans act as agents, and
how algorithms can influence task allocation, and how humans makes
sense of algorithm interactions. The section introduces the concept
of socio-technical co-agency where humans and IT systems interact
as equals, and humans are considered ‘holons’ in a holonic architec-
ture. Additionally, the section describes socio-technical CPS networks
as complex interactions among diverse agents, emphasizing the need
for governance. Finally, it explores the importance of joint cognitive
systems for continuous human-machine interaction, highlighting the
enhancement of machine learning through human contribution.
4.1.1. The role of the human in socio-technical interactions
Socio-technical interactions include the interactions between the so-
cial (HITL) and the technical system (CPS). Socio-technical interactions
create not one but several different human roles in CPS with HITL. For
example, humans are seen as users in broad situations when the CPS
displays report changes in the user’s situational context that the user
may have to react upon (Shao et al.,2019 ). Alternatively the humans
are seen as someone with different and dynamic degrees of trust in
the interactions (Brauner et al.,2019 ). A third example on a human
role is as a part in human-machine interactions between the cyber and
physical world, and in the interaction between the different actors and
intelligent components in physical world (Tuunanen et al.,2015 ). A key
question for all socio-technical interactions is thus who interacts with
the technical.

4.1.2. Cognitive  capabilities  in socio-technical  systems
The cognitive  capabilities  in socio-technical  systems  concern  the
cognitive  contribution  of human  activities  towards  the overall  effec-
tiveness  of CPS with HITL. It is linked  to the human  ability  to cap-
ture and act upon the industrial  or other context  of such interac-
tions (Emmanouilidis  et al.,2019 ). This view includes  examples  of
having  the human  in the visual analytics  loops (Emmanouilidis  et al.,
2019 ). In addition,  techniques  to address  the cognitive  capabilities  at
different  levels in the socio-technical  system  (Gil et al.,2020 ) have been
studied.
Having  HITL canamplify  machine  learning (ML), for example  by
condition  monitoring,  and by extending  ML capabilities  by contributing
to the (machine)  learning  process  (Emmanouilidis  et al.,2019 ). This
adds human  expert  knowledge  to data driven  capabilities.  It includes

4.2. Human-centeredness of CPS with HITL (answer to RQ1.2)
The papers in this section emphasize the ’human-centered’ enhance-
ment of human sensory and actuation capabilities through CPS with
HITL. Several papers attempt to define or demonstrate human-centered
design by modeling humans as a system element. Other papers depart
from psychological understandings of humans and their individual and
group differences. They highlight the importance of understanding
general psychological phenomena for reducing latencies and providing
multi-modal capabilities, e.g., by establishing latency requirements for
different senses. In addition, three forms ofgroup-centered CPS with
HITL are presented: human control models, human machine networks,
and human team/group models, each with distinct roles and method-
ologies. Finally, individual-centered CPS with HITL addresses rational
human behavior, system verification, and the need for reliable human
behaviors, considering cognitive limitations and behavioral biases. An-
other group of individual-centered studies discusses reliable human
behaviors, focusing on the study of human task performance and the
use of simulators for training and assessment. Here, we also find papers
that discuss digital human tactile skills, where wearable devices like
HoloLens enable the transfer of expert skills to machines, enhancing
task performance and allowing for the storage and reuse of these skills
in similar situations. Overall, the section underscores the necessity of
human-centered design and verification processes, agile methods, and
the handling of uncertain interactions in CPS.
4.2.1. Human-centered design
Human-centered design of CPS with HITL models in various ways
humans as system elements. Several papers see the human as a complex
component that contributes errors to the CPS due to variations in
human conditions. For example, variations in human health conditions
can give errors, in which case the CPS, if the human is found to be un-
engaged with the task, disconnects the human from the on-going task
to hinder further errors (Chui et al.,2018 ). Besides this treatment of
humans as irrational decision makers that are best modeled as simple
stochastic disturbances, it has been attempted to model humans as
independent, intelligent agents who act within a control system to their
own idiosyncratic benefits and who are influenced by incentive signals
that can be used to control them (Bae et al.,2019 ). A closed-loop
paradigm may generally model the human as a component involved
in identification of semantic concepts and relationships, protocols for
interaction, and iterative memorizing actions (Ma et al.,2018 ).
A slightly different modeling of the human as a component is the
use of modeling average humans in iterative travel-demand simulations
of physical infrastructure to provide feedback that real urban planners
can use to do city planning (Feygin et al.,2020 ). Similarly, there is
a focus on modeling abstracted humans when modeling user profiles,
such as privacy profiles based on data sharing preferences, for enforcing
compliance and ensuring user consent in software use scenarios (Kotut
and McCrickard ,2022 ). Finally, human-centered computing includes
modeling of typical human behavior is central to many applications of
machine learning for CPS with HITL, for example, machine learning to
improve smart cameras abilities to answer questions (Guo et al.,2018 )
On a higher level, CPS modeling of humans in complex organiza-
tions can also be done by modeling workflows and other activities using
motion sensors to enable management (scheduling) of rooms, with for
example management of operation rooms in clinical settings (Joerger
et al.,2018 ). Even higher level modeling of technology, organizations
and people in multi-component networks are important to function
construction and discovery of CPS with HITL, such as when framing the
overall problem of designing systems for human missions to Mars (Boy,
2018 ).4.2.2. General psychological phenomena of CPS with HITL
The sensing and actuation role of human users in CPS with HITL
may be enhanced by the systems due to their physicality and ability
to support physical capabilities of humans (Nunes et al.,2015 ). This
differentiates the capabilities of CPS with HITL from cognitive tech-
nologies that can upgrade human communication skills and inference
capacities by augmenting human capabilities in processing and sharing
information (Cimini et al.,2020 ).Human needs for sensing and actuating
are thus important for CPS with HITL (Fitzek et al.,2021 ). Earlier
promises of IT fulfilling human needs for information, entertainment,
and social connectedness are now being extended by IoT and CPS to
fulfill human needs for sensing and actuating (Fitzek et al.,2021 ).
However, to understand human needs for sensing and actuating, that is,
for multisensory perception and action, requires considering individual
human operator differences, including different expertise levels and
age-related differences that are important to multisensory integration
and delay requirements (Fitzek et al.,2021 ).
Human multisensory perception and action are shaped by human-
sense specific latencies in CPS with HITL. For example, reducing the
interaction delay between the device and the user are important for
in particular multi-device interactions (Hasan et al.,2019 ). Current
research focuses on each of the human senses individually, and have
so far established latency requirements from around 10 ms for video
over about 3 ms for audio to only 1 ms for haptic, so the challenge is to
both reduce the latencies and provide multi-modal capabilities (Fitzek
et al.,2021 ). For example, in intelligent lighting applications, human
perception is important, but making predictions in a perceptual context
involves several nonlinear, non-deterministic factors. These include
psychophysics (with lots of noise in measurements), psychology (bi-
ases in perception of reality), cost/lifestyle (depend on the persons
economy). Thus, new performance metrics that suit perceptual ML are
required (Gopalakrishna et al.,2017 ). A somewhat related case is that
of active vision with HITL, where tactile, sound, and verbal feedback
combines in multi-modal interfaces to shape long-term co-adaption of a
human-smartphone system (Gallina et al.,2015 ). Thus, general models
of human performance can used as components in co-adaptation that
allows systems to make inferences about changes in the users’ cognitive
and motor skills (Gallina et al.,2015 ).
4.2.3. Group-centered CPS with HITL
Group-centered CPS with HITL appears to come in three forms:
human control models ,human machine networks , andhuman team/group
models . The idea in human control models for CPS with HITL is to
consider the human as a crucial part of the whole system, which is very
different from and in contrast to autonomous CPSs that do not have
any human components (Jirgl et al.,2019 ). The human control model
in CPS with HITL builds on human reliability assessments assuming
that the human is important to deal with unexpected situations and
tasks, but also suffers from fatigue and makes human errors (Jirgl
et al.,2019 ). In addition, the human control takes place by the hu-
man interacting with other parts of the CPS through human-machine
interfaces (Jirgl et al.,2019 ). Thus, the human control models related
to CPS with HITL need to be complex enough to predict task-specific
human behavior on different levels of sensing and cognition, and also
able to adapt to changes over time in human-machine collaboration.
The idea inhuman machine networking for CPS with HITL is to enable
the machines to dynamically augment the network operations to the
benefit of humans, beyond simply relaying information, and towards
dealing with tight latency requirements for multisensory and high
bandwidth requirement scenarios in adjustable networks (Fitzek et al.,
2021 ). One example is swarm collaboration systems for smart manu-
facturing that are design to create human-machine social networks for
harmonious interaction between human beings and computer systems
in scenarios with multiple dynamic IoT events (Yang et al.,2018 ).
Another example of this practice is leveraging AI and machine learning
techniques for audio quality improvements (e.g., Fitzek et al.(2021 )),The Journal  of Systems  & Software  226 (2025)  112348

Human team models comprise a third approach to group-centered

from a technical-rational perspective and modeling from a human actor

and actively ensure the well-being of the human operators in-the-loop

4.2.4. Individual-centered CPS with HITL
Individual-centered CPS with HITL concerns issues ofrational human
behavior ,human-centered system-verification ,reliable human behaviors in
HITL, anddigital human tactile skills towards the physicality of the CPS.
Rational human behavior is central for human decision making from
a behavioral economics perspective, which points out that behavioral
biases affect decision making and thus may hamper CPS with HITL
by introducing sources of uncertainty (Protte et al.,2020 ). In par-
ticular, humans have bounded rationality in the sense that they are
strategic thinkers who aim to maximize their benefits but are limited
cognitively, hence prone to behavioral biases such as over-confidence
and misperception of random sequences of events (Protte et al.,2020 ;
Bae et al.,2019 ). The latter happens when the decision maker comes
to believe that the sequence of events exhibit a pattern that should
occur again; this may occur in closed-loop scenarios where the decision
maker receives feedback about the success of its actions, so in that
sense open-loop scenarios without such feedback information may be
safer (Protte et al.,2020 ). Human rationality considerations include
how to design for fairness with adaptive CPS with HITL that can address
intra-human and inter-human variability by monitoring the human
state and environment changes through IoT sensors; the idea is to
ensure fairness for multiple humans sharing the same IoT application
space such as cars and homes (Elmalaki ,2021 ).
The idea in having thehuman at the center of verification is that
the human must be a persistent center for all design and verification
processes of CPS with HITL, including those done post-implementation
in everyday work situations with real sensor data at play. The human-
centeredness concerns the design and verification of all physical and
electro-mechanical embedded systems, as well as integrated high-level
software executed on mobile devices, computing platforms, and cloud-
based resources (Cuckov et al.,2017 ). This complexity can be handled
by agile design and construction methods with feedback loops be-
tween consecutive development stages for immediate and rapid testing
and re-design, and between non-consecutive stages for requirements
checks, enforced through measurements and systematic compliancetests (Cuckov et al.,2017 ). Involving humans introduces uncertain
or chaotic interactions in CPS. Human interpretation of sensor data
is often vague and of qualitative nature. There are different ways to
deal with this. Similar but slightly different, it has been suggested
to let users choose from trace-generated trigger-action rules to let
individual users follow their own automation priorities (Zhang et al.,
2020 ). So in contrast to either fully relying on users to provide input
or fully relying of automated learning to infer automation models,
a combination of these approaches may help effectively aligning the
humans with the computer (Zhang et al.,2020 ). Another approach is
to let sensor information can be formally approximated, verified, and
identified by humans in graded terms such as low, medium, high, etc.,
that is, noise from different sources (i.e., sensors) in the overall system
can be expressed ‘‘linguistically’’ rather than as quantities. Banerjee
et al.(2020 ). Thus, an assessment of the CPS with HITL’s safety can be
done and delivered with a human-interpretable metric (Banerjee et al.,
2020 ). To do this may be of key importance for positioning the human
at the center of verification of CPS with HITL.
Evaluating reliable human behaviors is another individual-centered
issue in CPS with HITL. A general approach to human reliability is the
attempt to model the probability of individual human error based on
models of control feedback, coordination rules, and organization and
cognition knowledge (Jirgl et al.,2018 ). Thus the principle of human
reliability of CPS with HITL consists in studying human task perfor-
mance within various operating scenarios and evaluating the reliability
of the individual human behaviors with the purpose of failure and
human error prevention (Jirgl et al.,2019 ). These evaluations are based
on the assumption that human behavior is explainable as information
processing in the brain. However, information about behavior related
to the observed CPS with HITL task is often unavailable, and instead
the assessment relies on predefined probabilistic values of parameters
for tasks and operations provided in standards (Jirgl et al.,2019 ). An
alternative to naturalistic observation of CPS with HITL is to use a
suitable simulator equipped with data acquisition to analyze (and train)
human behavior within different standard and critical scenarios. A sim-
ulator approach to data collection also allows qualitative assessment of
the human behavior to describe more accurately the phenomena (Jirgl
et al.,2019 ).
The idea ofdigital human tactile skills is related to the physicality
of the CPS with HITL. With wearable devices (e.g., HoloLens) and their
built-in tracking of human motion and mapping the sensed information
to the human behavior and robot actions, experts can transfer skills
from human to machines (e.g., how to close the water flow to repair
a sink). In addition, the combination of sensors and actuators can be
employed in the wearables, constituting part of these user interfaces,
and control the HITL to enable expert task performance by novices
following instructions and guidance from the machines. For example,
specific gestures or motion paths can be generated and the human
operator be notified when deviating from the planned path (Fitzek
et al.,2021 ). Furthermore, such digital human tactile skills can then
be stored and used in new situations of the same type (Fitzek et al.,
2021 ).
Thus, individual-centered CPS with HITL offers both challenges of
how to design for qualitative and vague human beliefs and interpre-
tations (e.g., human-centered system-verification, rational and reliable
human behaviors in HITL), and great promises of shareable digital
human skills (e.g., digital human tactile skills towards the physicality
of the CPS).

4.3.1. Human factor
Across HCI technologies, notions of the human include human factor ,
human actor, human decision maker, and human operator, depending
on the context and work role of the human. The classic human factor
notion considers humans as a system component just as or perhaps even
more important than the software and hardware, but with a tendency
to be the most unreliable component of any CPS with HITL (Cuckov
et al.,2017 ). For example, human factors (e.g., human errors and driver
medical conditions) are critical reasons for car crashes (Chen et al.,
2018 ). In this view, human factors include training level, stress, or
fatigue, and the range of ways in which humans can participate and the
roles they can play when collaborating with autonomous CPS should be
explored (Gil et al.,2019 ). Human factors may be seen even broader
– and more positively – and include supervision, setting adjustment,
knowledge and competences, situation diagnosis, and in general as
those providing additional degrees of freedom to the CPS, as ’flexibility
drivers’ (Fantini et al.,2016 ). The human factor can include engineer-
ing, monitoring, controlling, and performing operational maintenance
roles, with the human component being different from the other parts
of the CPS in the sense of being biological (Fass and Gechter ,2015 ).
Other notions of the human, however, consider the human as more than
an unreliable factor. Instead, the human is seen as an actor; thus the
human becomes central in the design of CPS with HITL when moving
from the notion of information to one of sensing and actuating, and
with human and machine operators seamlessly connected (Fitzek et al.,
2021 ). CPS with HITL builds on collective evidence from the technical
and human system actors (Emmanouilidis et al.,2019 ). The human
decision maker is a risk factor in CPS with HITL. This implies that it is
necessary to know about human decision makers’ abilities, weaknesses,
and potential biases in each task-domain or risk-context, in order to
design support (e.g., visualizations) for human decision makers’ trust,
(blind) compliance, and the restoration of trust after a failure (Brauner
et al.,2019 ). Finally, the human operator in CPS with HITL is always
a perceiver of the environment (Fitzek et al.,2021 ), so designing for
work contexts is of key importance.
4.3.2. Human–computer interface
While HCI in general is about interactions, the /em human–
computer interface is a specific material component in the computer
system in the same way as the digital, analogue, and cyber compo-
nents (Cuckov et al.,2017 ). These components, including the human–
computer interface, are connected within the system in complex
ways (Cuckov et al.,2017 ). For example, to establish a human–
computer interaction platform for safety testing of HITL for take-
over situations in autonomous electrical vehicles driving, Logitech
G29 Driving Force Steering Wheels and Pedals were used as human–
computer interfaces to enable human control of vehicle steering angle,
gear, gas pedal position and brake pedal position (Hong and Aparow ,

human–computer interface.
4.3.3. Human-machine interface
Human-machine interfaces historically precede human–computer in-
terfaces. Analogue gauges, buttons, levers, alarms, switches, etc. haveappeared in transport, manufacturing, and military domains for many
decades (Singh and Mahmoud ,2019 ), and actually throughout human
history. While the interfaces may always have been human-centered,
today there is a strong need for human-machine interfaces that support
changes in the user’s situational context and minimize human attention
costs (Shao et al.,2019 ). This requires capture and analysis of what
happens in the human-machine interface to detect human errors and
support human performance in CPS with HITL in real time (Singh
and Mahmoud ,2017 ). Related to this, it remains a specific problem
how to replace or monitor existing legacy human-machine interfaces
with all their human-sense-dependent analogue controls and displays
and related expert human skills (Singh and Mahmoud ,2019 ). HCI
has a variety of notions of interface history and a long tradition for
ergonomic interfaces.
4.3.4. Ergonomics of CPS with HITL
Ergonomics traditionally covers most of human–computer interac-
tions (the term is sometimes used synonymous with HCI), but it also
covers physical ergonomics such as how workers interact with chairs,
lifts objects, use machines, etc. For CPS with HITL it is important to
look at all aspects of ergonomics, including ergonomic methods such as
novel methods for machine and human sensing for safety, adjustment
of HITL over timeas human skills evolve, and the provision ofhuman
autonomy .
While traditional ergonomic methods of CPS with HITL aim to ensure
the safety of humans are passive structures, newer methods provide
sensing-driven safety, that is, considers machine and human sensing
for safety. This includes what have been called neuroergomics, that
is, physiological measurements to understand an human behavior by
recording physiological changes of CPS with HITL (Nuamah and Seong ,
2017 ). For example safety around factory robots involve the use of
cages, while newer Cobots promise to provide sense-driven solutions
for human safety around robots (Dani et al.,2020 ). The assumption is
that it is possible to infer human intentions from sensing information;
challenges for this include ensuring quality of long term intention
estimation (e.g., does the human behave in the same way today as
yesterday?) and fusing information from multiple sensors including
some that humans do not have such as Lidar and physiological sig-
nals (Dani et al.,2020 ), and then reliably use this information about
human intentions to increase safety in real time Cobot scenarios, fol-
lowing the principles of human-centered design in the ISO standard
on ergonomics of human–system interaction (Gil et al.,2020 ). Fur-
thermore, in ergonomics humans can be viewed as not only service
consumers consuming, e.g., safety, but also as service providers, who
are sensed by sensors or directly answers sensing requests from the
CPS (Domaszewicz and Parzych ,2022 ). The humans are not in charge
within the CPS in these cases, but deliver ‘sensing’ services to the or-
ganization owning the CPS (Domaszewicz and Parzych ,2022 ). Among
the different benefits of viewing humans as service providers in the CPS
with HITL is that this view utilizes the vagueness of human sensing
as a strength; thus human employees can offer not only facts, but
also vague opinions and judgment (e.g., about safety in a broader
sense such as the ergonomics of a chair, work overload, satisfaction,
mobbing) (Domaszewicz and Parzych ,2022 ).
CPS with HITL can beadjusted over time by capturing changes
in motor and cognitive abilities of the users over time, and contin-
uously adjust the human-machine interface to the level of the users
skills (Gallina et al.,2015 ). For example, the joy-stick operation of
excavators, cranes, forklifts, electric-powered wheelchairs, and tele-
manipulated robots can be adjusted over the years as users learn to use
it, so that the human and the interface progressively co-adapt (Gallina
et al.,2015 ). This raises questions about how smart human interfaces
with micro-learning opportunities, easy programming, and presentation
of a diversity of information generated by model-integrated AI, can
enable task, context, and user-adaptive interactions (Brauner et al.,

4.4. Human work design for CPS with HITL (answer to RQ1.4)
The papers in this section examine the role of CPS with HITL
within the context of human work. The section discusses the scarce
literature on HITL and CPS in work-related fields, and presents findings
on interaction designs, work design, and work environment. Interac-
tion designs for work emphasize iterative testing and redesigning to
achieve suitable interaction within smart work environments. Work
design focuses on socio-technical approaches, integrating human and
machine collaboration, and considering extreme work scenarios like
Martian colonies. Human work environment highlights the importance of
human control in automated systems for maintaining productivity and
satisfaction, suggesting a need for innovative, safe, and trustworthy CPS
with HITL. The overall aim is to create work systems that are adaptable,
efficient, and enhance human-machine collaboration.
4.4.1. Interaction designs for work
Designing prototypes can be viewed as a tool for helping to improve
CPS with HITL interaction designs for work, since it serves to iteratively
test and redesign the prototype until a suitable interaction design is
achieved (Gil et al.,2020 ). Interaction designs for CPS with HITL share
the common goal of changing the way interaction is realized towards
new forms of smart work environments in which the boundaries ofvirtual and physical worlds dissolve and novel forms of interaction are
applied (Magerkurth ,2012 ). In human work, CPS with HITL requires
the interaction design to follow push and pull principles so that both
sides can request resources (e.g., the computer can employ an active
learning approach and signal the need for additional labels as a data
modification from the human, and the human can request information
and operations from the computer) (Zschech et al.,2021 ). Moderating
factors on the interaction design can include work task’s criticality,
e.g., criticality of vision-based detection tasks in automotive, energy,
manufacturing, agriculture, architecture work domains, the experience
of the users (workers), and other factors (Zschech et al.,2021 ). For
example, interaction designs of alarm systems for vulnerable groups,
such as migrants, homeless, and home violence victims, require CPS
with HITL to protect against attacks by gesture and voice recognition
that can be activated either automatically or by the human pressing
the alarm button (Silva et al.,2019 ). A similar need for an interaction
design for work that aims to balance the HITL is obvious when it comes
to designing the best moment to let smart speakers talk to the humans
next to them; for example participants in their own homes accepted to
be interrupted by smart speakers in 18 percent of the cases while doing
paid work versus 85 percent while doing unpaid household work (Cha
et al.,2020 ). CPS with HITL requires interaction designs that relates
humans to the objects around them, and the wider infrastructure in
which the CPS are embedded (Murray-Rust et al.,2022 ).
4.4.2. Work design for CPS with HITL
Work design for CPS with HITL that enable smart workplaces and
smart work environments may concern technologies that support hu-
man work, collaborations between humans and machines, social inter-
actions in complex systems, and more (Cimini et al.,2020 ). The human
worker configuration of CPS with HITL is a part of this; the context of
work design includes the type of production system in which work takes
place, including task allocation between machines and humans, and the
machines’ autonomy and the general level of automation in the work
environment (Cimini et al.,2020 ). To achieve high quality work design,
theapproach to the customers regarding the design of the manufacturing
process and human work design should be done in a socio-technical
way, that is, designing the social and technical sub-systems together.
For example, solutions for structuring image data in the fields of
medicine, traffic and machinery production require pre-defined sets of
images that first must be provided to enable crowd workers to perform
the labeling (Schuir et al.,2021 ). The crowd work of labeling, crowd
workers’ knowledge, organization of the crowd work, norms, policies ,
and suitable technical tools (e.g., a and augmented reality solution),
must all be designed concurrently (Schuir et al.,2021 ).
The human work design technology itself may need to be further
developed to design for a variety of novel work systems (Brauner et al.,
2019 ;Cimini et al.,2020 ). Toinnovate work design for CPS with HITL,
it pays to consider extreme scenarios. Imagine the work environment
on future Mars or Moon colonies. A Martian workplace (Linder et al.,
2022 ), may require a very different work design from current work-
places on Earth. The type of work task, isolation from other human
societies, and extreme setting call for work design for adaptability,
extreme asynchronous communication, CPS with HITL guided by an
extremely small number of qualified people, and solutions for stress
mitigation in highly distributed work contexts. In general, work design
encompasses the design of organizational structure, employees/people,
tasks, and CPS with HITL, as one entangled socio-technical work system
b (Brauner et al.,2019 ). Interestingly, for extreme scenarios like the

4.4.3. Work environment
Workplaces are increasingly found within smart work environments
such as provided by building automation and connected services.
Building automation and centralized control systems provide indoor
comfort, e.g., HITL for CPS that provide climate management, light
(Gopalakrishna et al.,2017 ), security registration, and other services,
and conserve energy. However, limiting or removing employees’ con-
trol over their work environment by automation with CPS results
in dissatisfaction, and hence decreases productivity (Loengbudnark
et al.,2022 ). Having CPS with HITL and hence human control is thus
directly and positively influencing the productivity. The collaborative
work environment between humans and robotic automation in building
automation and other work environments may require innovations in
safe, efficient, and trustworthy CPS with HITL (Dani et al.,2020 ).

CPS with HITL from socio-technical perspectives. The insights gained
were validated for their usefulness to industry and potential areas
for industry-oriented research by interviews with industry experts and

from socio-technical perspectives in environment, infrastructure, coor-

6.1.1. Environment and ST perspectives
CPS with HITL theory suggests that environmental contextual as-
pects such as physical, temporal, social, computational, historical, pro-
file, and also humans are a part of the environment that interact with
the CPS, that the associated constraints and limitations impact the
adaptation requirements (Da et al.,2014 ). An ST perspective suggests
to study Human-algorithm interactions between algorithms and human
operators in key decision-making interfaces, and between algorithms
and (company) social media users in small ‘‘filter bubbles’’ of reactions
and likes (Valdez and Ziefle ,2018 ). The principle of human reliability
from the ST literature suggests to study naturalistic human task per-
formance and errors in CPS with HITL in operating scenarios (Jirgl
et al.,2019 ). Thirdly, in dynamic CPS theory, handling context-aware
adaptation by capturing and analyzing the context within the evolu-
tive environment is complex. The ST literature on ergonomics of CPS
with HITL suggests that it is possible to infer human intentions from
past sensing information, by fusing information from multiple senses
including some that humans do not have such as lidar and physiological
signals (Dani et al.,2020 ). Finally, CPS with HITL theory argues that
such systems are situated in the environment which most often include
both physical and virtual elements (Weyns ,2017 ), which resonates well
with the ST literature that suggests that workplaces are increasingly
found within smart work environments provided by automation and
connected services. However, the socio-technical perspective is that
limiting or removing employees’ control over their work environment
by automation with CPS result in dissatisfaction, and hence decrease
productivity (Loengbudnark et al.,2022 ), while having CPS with HITL
and hence human control is thus directly and positively influencing the
productivity, given innovations in safe, efficient, and trustworthy CPS
with HITL (Dani et al.,2020 ).
6.1.2. Infrastructure and ST perspectives
CPS with HITL theory states that the CPSarchitecture component
determines variations in both software and hardware architectures,
which are designed by stakeholders. The ST literature suggests to study
such architectures as socio-technical CPS networks. These constitutes
interactions between a variety of agents — humans, machines, produc-
tions, software, AI tools, and as such there among other things networks
of social interactions (Cimini et al.,2020 ). Second, the theory states
that infrastructures consist of various hardware (HW) and software
(SW) component. An adaptive CPShardware architecture can be re-
structured in run-time to add, delete, replace, and combine its elements.
The CPS HW elements include sensors, network facilities, computation
resources, andactuators . The ST idea in having the human at the center
of verification is that the human must be a persistent center for all
design and verification processes of CPS with HITL, including design
and verification of all physical, electro-mechanical, embedded systems,
and the integrated high-level software executed on mobile devices,
computing platforms, and cloudbased resources (Cuckov et al.,2017 ).Thirdly, the CPSarchitecture component determines variations in both
software and hardware architectures. These architectures are designed
by stakeholders and self-adapted by the autonomic control element
during system execution. The ST literature suggests that today there
is a strong need for human-machine interfaces that support changes
in user situational context and minimize human attention costs (Shao
et al.,2019 ), which requires capture and analysis of what happens
in the human-machine interface to detect human errors and support
human performance in CPS with HITL in real time. Finally, a CPS might
be in interaction with the dynamic surrounding environment by taking
advantage of sense and actuate elements that sense elements frequently
retrieve raw data Since the software system does not fully control
the environment, the data transmission is continuous. Related to this
issue of tackling the contextual dynamics, the ST literature suggests
that the context of work design for CPS with HITL includes the type
of production system in which work takes place, and hereunder task
allocation between machines and humans, and the machines’ autonomy
and the general level of automation in the work environment (Cimini
et al.,2020 ).
6.1.3. Coordination and ST perspectives
In CPS theory, dynamic computation infrastructure, in coordination
with the dynamic environment management, is the more general case
where CPS belongs. In this space, both dimensions have to be managed,
but keeping a separation of concerns with adequate coordination can
help to face the complexity of the design. In the ST literature, socio-
technical co-agency is when humans and machines/IT systems work
together on an equal footing. CPS is seen as a separate entity from
the human in the HITL. The relationship between them is named or
at least called out as important (Fass and Gechter ,2015 ). Humans
are autonomous and cooperating conceptual entities that embed the
interface needed to communicate with machines (Cimini et al.,2020 ).
The resulting new system that includes both humans and machines is a
‘‘socio-technical CPS’’ (Cimini et al.,2020 ). Second, in CPS theory, co-
ordination in embedded systems with specific computing capacity and
low energy consumption happens to adapt to e.g. changes in the control
system itself (e.g., fault recovery). In the TS literature, the idea in
human team models for CPS with HITL is that the actions and reactions
of the humans play a central role to self-awareness, self-adaptation and
self-integration of the various artificial agents in the system, and the
human on their side need data and visualizations to become aware of
interactions between system constituents (Botev ,2020 ). In addition, to
co-adapt models of humans for CPS with HITL, the HITL component is
also there to ensure the well-being of the human operators (Fitzek et al.,
2021 ). Third, in CPS theory, the coordination is carefully analyzed and
addressed mainly through distributed embedded CPS devices that are
distributed across the system. In the ST literature, the human–computer
interface is a specific material component in the computer system in
the same way as the digital, analogue, and cyber components (CuckovThe Journal  of Systems  & Software  226 (2025)  112348

et al.,2017 ), which then becomes part of the coordination. Finally,
hard real-time systems requiring strict predictability is used in very

The human work design technology itself may need to be further

6.1.4. Decision making and ST perspectives
In CPS theory, users prefer proactive adaptation because of its
ability to avoid quality degradation within the system. However, the
proactive feature requires running complex prediction algorithms that
depend on the correctness of entry data as parameters. The ST literature
suggests that the amplification of machine learning by humans, for
example in condition monitoring, may add human expert knowledge
to data driven capabilities (Emmanouilidis et al.,2019 ;Banerjee et al.,
2020 ). Second, adaptation policies depend on the time of the system’s
reaction to uncertainties, i.e., before or after risk occurrence. Here for
example the time aspect and IoT system characteristics and goals im-
pact the strategy and decision method established for adaptation, and
the ST literature suggests to add that human multisensory perception
and action needs require adding new performance metrics for CPS with
HITL, such as psycho-physical and psychological (Gopalakrishna et al.,
2017 ). Third, ST theory argues that the human decision maker is a risk
factor in CPS with HITL, so it is important to design support for human
decision makers’ trust and compliance (Brauner et al.,2019 ). Finally,
much research focuses on reactive adaptation. The monitoring and
execution activities are very much the same in reactive and proactive
methods, but the analysis and planning phases make the difference. The
ST literature suggests that designing interactive prototypes for work can
help to improve decision making in CPS with HITL (Gil et al.,2020 ),
e.g., by providing novel forms of interaction (Magerkurth ,2012 ), and
by pointing to moderating factors on the interaction design such as the
work task’s criticality and the competence of workers (Zschech et al.,
2021 ).
6.1.5. Lessons learned
The discussion above of the findings from the literature review teach
us that areas of concern for CPS with HITL adaption can successfully
be viewed from four different ST perspectives. There is therefore a
possibility for better theorizing CPS with HITL by integrating these
perspectives in new control models. Perhaps these models need to be
work domain specific, e.g., different for driving and for manufacturing
etc. Most importantly, the idea of CPS with HITL is that the human
is continuously, in real time, a part of the CPS and contributes to the
outcome.
An important lesson from this study is however that the potential
of ST perspectives on CPS with HITL is strong in the literature, but it
is weak or ignored by industry experts, Table 8. The industry experts
who we asked to respond to the insights from the literature review,

importance of classic HCI. There is also some agreement about human

6.2. Implications for professional practice
Based on insights discussed above and on the general ST litera-
ture, we propose the practical solution that producers, vendors, and
customers/end users significantly enhance their co-design activities. As
our expert interviews indicated, there is increasing awareness among
producers of the importance of going beyond the simple scenario that

6.3. Implications for future research
The insights from our expert interviews, which showed industry
experts’ awareness of issues with CPS in HITL in industry due to more
complex automation, see e.g., Section 4.1, indicate that there is a poten-
tial need for identifying concrete examples of successful socio-technical
integration in industry settings. Noteworthy, the literature review indi-
cated that giving humans control over data relevant to heir work envi-
ronment is positively influencing the productivity (Loengbudnark et al.,
2022 ). From an ST perspective, such studies could start with studying
human workers’ positive practices and positive experiences (Klapperich
et al.,2018 ). Potentially there is a need for analysis of CPS with HITL
successes within the range of work domains and application domains.
Our study of the software engineering literature identified four main
socio-technical approaches to CPS with HITL-related social science.
There were few if any traces of the possible emerging shift from existing
human-centered approaches towards current trend of human-AI collab-
oration, but see e.g., Section 4.3andFitzek et al.(2021 ). Generative
AI may however change CPS with HITL radically due to the promise
of AI-powered chatbot interaction, as illustrated in a recent study from
the general HCI community (Wu et al.,2022 ). Previous focus on human
interaction with buttons and displays and the user experience of those
interactions, may be partly or in some cases wholly replaced by human-
GenAI collaboration. CPS with HITL could thus in the future be studiedas human-CPS collaboration, in addition to applying traditional human-
centered approaches of studying the usability and UX of the CPS with
HITL.
Given that our approach was a literature review with an additional
validation done by qualitative research interviews with industry ex-
perts, there are possibilities for future research to apply other methods.
Firstly, a significantly larger validation study engaging a diverse range
of industry experts from a variety of industries will perhaps add im-
portant nuances to the identification of gaps between the literature
and industry experts. Second, the use of AI for literature selection and
extraction is rapidly increasing (Wagner et al.,2022 ), which potentially
can enable future studies to increase the range of the review to include
more relevant social science and humanities approaches in the study of
CPS with HITL.
6.4. Limitations
There are several limitations of this research, which we have tried
to overcome. First, the literature could have been even broader by not
including CPS with HITL as keywords, but we found that would have
scoped the paper out of interest for CPS researchers. Second, the litera-
ture did not discuss how CPS systems accommodate vulnerable groups
including individuals with physical or cognitive disabilities; however,
we did identify issues with cognitive limitations in the literature re-
viewed, see Section 4.2.1 . Third, the interviewed industry experts had
technical backgrounds, but we included one with business background
to get that perspective also represented in the viewpoints. Fourth,
they came from tech providers, not end-user companies/clients so
discussions of ST literature were somewhat limited from that position,
which we tried to overcome by additional translation and interpretation
during the interviews and during data analysis. Overall, we believe
that the selection of literature and industry experts were valuable and

7. Conclusion
The answers provided by this systematic literature review of ST per-
spectives on CPS with HITL are that four perspectives: socio-technical
capacities, a human-centered approach, human–computer interaction
technologies, and human work design, are worthwhile including in
CPS research on the environment, infrastructure, coordination, and
decision making. In addition, we validated the literature review with
interviews with industry experts, which revealed agreements and dis-
agreements between their view and the ST perspectives on CPS with
HITL. Future industry-oriented research may be better at theorizing
CPS with HITL by integrating these perspectives in new control models.
Among possible topics for future research are positioning of humans
and technical devices in the CPS with HITL networks, bring in the rich
variety of ideas in ST literature on how to enhance HITL, embrace a
ST terminology, and do detailed field studies of manufacturing workers
and their interactions with a full range of humans and technical devices
in the CPS networks.
