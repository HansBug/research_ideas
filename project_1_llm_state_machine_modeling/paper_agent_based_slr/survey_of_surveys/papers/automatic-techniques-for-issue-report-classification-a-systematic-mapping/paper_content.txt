Abstract
Several studies have evaluated automatic techniques for classifying software issue
reports into bugs and non-bugs to assist practitioners in effectively assigning relevant
resources based on the type of issue. Currently, no comprehensive overview of
this area has been published. A comprehensive overview will help identify future
research directions and provide an extensive collection of potentially relevant
existing solutions. This study aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the
use of automatic techniques to classify issue reports. We conducted a systematic
mapping study and identified 46 studies on the topic. The study results indicate
that the existing literature applies various techniques for classifying issue reports,
including traditional machine learning and deep learning-based techniques, and
more advanced large language models. Furthermore, we observe that these studies
(a) lack the involvement of practitioners, (b) do not consider other potentially
relevant adoption factors beyond prediction accuracy, such as the explainability,
scalability, and generalizability of the techniques, and (c) mainly rely on archival
data from open-source repositories only. Therefore, future research should focus on
real industrial evaluations, consider other potentially relevant adoption factors, and
actively involve practitioners.

Automated Software Engineering           (2026) 33:72 1 Introduction
Issue management is a complicated and costly process encompassing multiple
activities, such as reporting, assigning, and resolving issues (Zou et al. 2018 ;
Parnin and Orso 2011 ; Laiq et al. 2023 ). In large-scale software development, a
large number of issue reports are submitted by end-users, developers, and testers.
For example, Mozilla received 224,408 issue reports in 12 months, and Firefox
received 126,967 issue reports in 18 months (Fan et al. 2018 ). In the next step, these
reports are assigned to the relevant developers. Developers then use the submitted
information to reproduce the issue, identify root causes, and fix it. This process
can be time-consuming and resource-intensive, and can involve back-and-forth
discussions among several stakeholders. Moreover, these reports vary significantly in
nature. Some reports may be submitted due to misunderstandings, while others might
suggest improvements to the documentation or request new features. Some reports
describe issues that require a code fix (Antoniol et al. 2008 ). For example, Kallis
et al. ( 2023 ) gathered 1,275,881 GitHub issue reports, of which 670,951 (52.6%)
were bugs, 472,216 (37%) were feature requests, 76,048 (6%) were questions, and
56,666 (4.4%) were related to documentation.
Consequently, each issue report requires a different action. Thus, practitioners
need the ability to identify the type of a newly submitted issue report early in the
issue management process to effectively assign the relevant resources. Consider,
for example, a developer who is assigned a report flagged as a bug. After several
hours of attempting to reproduce the issue, he finds it was actually a feature request
mislabeled as a bug. Such false positives12 waste valuable developer effort and delay
the resolution of real bugs. On the scale of hundreds of thousands of reports, these
misclassifications accumulate into substantial costs for organizations. Therefore,
classifying issue reports into bugs and non-bugs would enable routing only issue
reports classified as bugs to developers, saving the time they would otherwise spend
on understanding, reproducing, and debugging issue reports that will turn out not to
be code-related.
Manually identifying and classifying issue reports is labor-intensive and error-
prone (Herzig et al. 2013 ). Thus, several automatic techniques (e.g., Zanetti et al.
2013 ; Laiq et al. 2022 ; Cho et al. 2022 ; Siddiq and Santos 2022 ) have been evaluated
to assist practitioners in automatically classifying issue reports into bugs (requiring
a code correction) and non-bugs (all other categories, e.g., feature requests and
questions).
Despite the growing number of studies on automated issue report classification,
there is currently no comprehensive overview that organizes and structures this body
of work. Such an overview is essential for compiling the various techniques and
datasets used in the literature and the evaluation approaches and metrics applied.
By systematically mapping this information, researchers and practitioners can better
understand the current state of the field, identify gaps and underexplored areas, and
observe emerging trends. For example, while many studies focus on improving

developed a comprehensive map that covers various aspects, including the study’s

The main contributions of this mapping study are as follows.
 ●A comprehensive overview/map of the existing work on issue report classifica -
tion with detailed information about the studies.
 ●Several insights from the existing literature on issue report classification. For
instance, BERT-based models, such as RoBERTa, are recognized as state-of-
the-art for this task. Furthermore, traditional ML techniques such as logistic
regression and SVM can achieve prediction performance comparable to complex
deep learning methods and BERT-based models in classifying issue reports.
 ●Several potential future research directions for issue report classification. For
example, the need for evaluations of the proposed techniques in real industrial
settings and a careful assessment of the reliability of ground-truth data used for
training and evaluating ML models in issue report classification.
This paper is structured as follows. Section 2 describes the background knowledge
and related work on the topic. Section 3 describes the search strategy, selection
criteria, data extraction, data analysis and classification, and validity threats to this
study. The results are presented in Section 4. Section 5 discusses the overall findings,
while Section 6 concludes the paper.
2 Background and related work
Issue management is a costly and complicated process that includes several activities
such as reporting, assigning, and resolving issues (Zou et al. 2018 ; Parnin and Orso
2011 ; Laiq et al. 2023 ). In large projects, developers, testers, and end-users submit a
large number of issue reports (Fan et al. 2018 ; Zhang et al. 2015 ). An issue report3 is
an essential software artifact and plays an important role in the debugging and fixing
of issues (Zou et al. 2018 ). To assist practitioners in managing the workload of issue
reports and streamlining the issue-fixing processes, significant research in software

assignee recommendation (Borg et al. 2024 ), bug localization (Kim et al. 2013 ), and
duplicate issue report detection (Zhang et al. 2023 ).
Similarly, to assist practitioners in assigning resources effectively to issue reports,
several studies have investigated the use of automatic techniques to categorize issue
reports, such as into bugs  (those that require code correction) and non-bugs  (e.g.,
feature requests, questions, and documentation) (Antoniol et al. 2008 ; Herzig et al.
2013 ; Zanetti et al. 2013 ; Colavito et al. 2024 ). Several automatic techniques have
been evaluated in the literature for classifying issue reports into bugs and non-bugs,
including traditional ML (Fan et al. 2018 ; Laiq et al. 2022 ) and deep learning-based
techniques (He et al. 2020 ; Kwak et al. 2023 ) and more advanced large language
models (Siddiq and Santos 2022 ; Heo et al. 2024 ; Laiq et al. 2024 ).
While a number of primary studies have been conducted on issue report
classification, to the best of our knowledge, no comprehensive overview has been
conducted. Such an overview would help identify future research directions and
provide an extensive collection of relevant existing solutions. Köksal and Öztürk
(2022 ) reviewed the literature on issue report classification and identified 18 primary
studies. However, only seven of these studies focus on classifying issue reports into
bugs and non-bugs. The findings of their study indicate that traditional ML techniques,
including logistic regression, SVM, and random forests, are predominantly used.
Only a few studies have explored deep learning-based techniques. The reviewed
literature extends up to 2021, and only one study from 2022 is included. This
may explain why large language models and advanced deep learning techniques
are not included in their analysis. Furthermore, the study does not provide data or
insights, nor does it discuss important aspects such as evaluation metrics, evaluation
approaches, practitioner involvement, or the quality of ground truth data. The review
is not systematic in nature (Kitchenham et al. 2016 ) as well. In addition, it only
reports limited information about primary research, i.e., the techniques used, the
number of issue reports, and their sources (i.e., proprietary or open source data). In
contrast, our review contributes by providing (a) a comprehensive/map overview
of the area following a systematic approach (Petersen et al. 2008 ), (b) detailed
information on primary research, (c) key lessons learned from the existing work,
and (d) future research directions.
Moreover, several reviews related to issue management have been conducted
in the literature. For example, Zhang et al. ( 2015 ) reviewed the literature on issue
report analysis. Zou et al. ( 2018 ) reviewed the literature on automatic techniques to
manage issue reports and conducted a survey with practitioners to gain their insights.
Gomes et al. (2019 ) reviewed the literature on predicting the severity of issue reports.
Uddin et al. (2017 ) reviewed the literature on prioritization of issue reports. While
these studies relate to our work, they do not answer the research question posed in
this study. Therefore, we conducted a systematic mapping study to answer the posed
research question (Petersen et al. 2008 ).

Automated Software Engineering           (2026) 33:72 3.2 Study selection and outcome
We selected papers based on title, abstract reading, and full-text reading. We read
full-text only when we could not decide whether to include or exclude a paper based
on the title and abstract reading. The following criteria were followed for the study
selection:
Inclusion criteria:
I1: The paper is peer-reviewed.
I2: The paper is written in English.
I3: The paper focuses on classifying issue reports according to the classification
criteria as defined by Antoniol et al. (2008 ) and Herzig et al. (2013 ): (a) those
that require code fixes, that is, bugs. (b) non-bugs, e.g., documentation, feature
requests, and questions. Exclusion criteria:
E1: The paper is not peer-reviewed.
E2: The paper is not written in English.
E3: The paper does not address the classification of issue reports into bugs/non-
bugs: (a) those that require code fixes, that is, bugs. (b) non-bugs, e.g., documen -
tation, feature requests, and questions.
Figure 1 shows the number of included and excluded papers at each stage. From
the initial 210 papers, we identified 109 duplicates and 12 proceedings summaries,
leaving 89 unique papers for the selection phase. The first author applied the selection
criteria mentioned above to all 89 papers. As a result, 46 papers were selected for data
extraction. See an overview in Fig. 1.
In total, 43 papers were excluded according to the exclusion criteria mentioned
above. The reasons for exclusion were as follows. Thirty-nine papers were excluded
because they did not focus on the classification of issue reports into bugs and non-bugs.
Additionally, two papers were not written in English; one was a vision paper that lacked
empirical results, and one could not be accessed despite our efforts. The same reviewer,
the first author, reconsidered all 43 excluded papers after a gap of seven weeks from
the first iteration of selecting papers to reduce the risk of excluding a relevant paper. In
addition, the list of the excluded papers with reasons for excluding a paper at this stage
is made available at https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.31438093  for transparency.

3.4 Analysis and classification
The extracted information was tabulated and visualized using graphs. We also
categorized the proposed techniques into higher-level categories, such as traditional
ML and deep learning techniques and BERT-based large language models. Similarly,
we grouped studies based on evaluation approaches, evaluation metrics, and pre-
processing techniques.
The classification scheme for techniques was developed using the extracted data.
After completing the data extraction, both authors jointly reviewed the identified
techniques and designed an initial classification scheme, grouping techniques
into higher-level categories: traditional machine learning techniques (e.g., SVM
and Random Forest), deep learning techniques (e.g., CNN and LSTM), and large
language models (e.g., BERT-base and RoBERTa). The first author then assigned
each study to the agreed categories, and the second author independently reviewed
the classifications for consistency and accuracy. A minor discrepancy concerning
whether few-shot/zero-shot learning approaches should be grouped under general
LLM-based methods was resolved through discussion and consensus, leading
to the final agreed-upon categorization. As a result, we placed few-shot and zero-
shot learning, T5 models, and BERT-based models in separate categories rather
than grouping them under a broader “large language models” category. To support
transparency and reproducibility, we have made the complete classification scheme

Automated Software Engineering           (2026) 33:72 3.5 Validity threats
We used the guidelines by Ampatzoglou et al. (2019 ) to consider the potential validity
threats to our study and identify mitigation actions. In the following subsections, we
discuss some of the validity threats and our mitigation actions.
3.5.1 Study selection validity
A potential threat to this mapping study is the omission of relevant studies on
issue report classification. We mitigated this by carefully developing our search
string that focuses on identifying relevant studies on issue report classification. In
addition, we have used two indexing (i.e., Scopus and Web of Science) and two
publisher-specific databases (i.e., IEEE Xplore and ACM) without restricting our
search to papers from specific years to have sufficient coverage. These databases
sufficiently cover the software engineering literature (Kitchenham et al. 2016 ;
Usman et al. 2023 ). Moreover, we also complemented our search strategy by
manually identifying relevant studies from a relevant venue dedicated to the topic
(see details in Section 3.1).
Another threat to this review is bias during the paper selection process. We
mitigated this risk by ensuring that every excluded paper was reconsidered after
a seven-week gap by the same reviewer. A similar approach (i.e., test-retest) has
been applied and recommended when a single reviewer conducts steps of systematic
reviews or mapping studies (Kitchenham 2010 ; Petersen 2011 ). The involvement
of a single reviewer may still pose a validity threat of missing relevant studies.
However, since this work is a systematic mapping study rather than a systematic
literature review, the goal is to obtain a good sample of papers instead of exhaustive
coverage as recommended in the literature (Kitchenham et al. 2012 ; Wohlin et al.
2013 ; Petersen et al. 2015 ). In this context, the potential risk of excluding a few
relevant papers due to a single reviewer does not pose a major threat to the validity
of the mapping study. We believe that we have obtained a reasonable sample that
includes a diverse set of studies from 2011 to 2024, including publications from top
software engineering venues. In addition, we have manually collected all papers
on issue report classification from the workshop on topic (NLBSE workshop, see
Section 3.1) from 2022 to 2024.
3.5.2 Data validity
Another potential threat to this study is bias in the data extraction and analysis of
primary studies. Two reviewers were involved in the data extraction and analysis
process to mitigate this threat. The first author performed data extraction on all
46 studies. To increase confidence in data extraction, the second author reviewed
a randomly selected subset of ten primary studies to verify the correctness of the
extracted data. No discrepancies were found between the two authors (see detailed
discussion in Section 3.3).

Automated Software Engineering           (2026) 33:72 A limitation of this study is that we did not conduct a quality assessment of the
included primary studies. This is not a major threat to the results of this mapping, where
we show what techniques have been used for issue report classification. Mapping stud -
ies are exploratory (Petersen et al. 2015 ). Their primary aim is to provide a structured
overview of a research area, identify trends, and highlight gaps rather than evaluate the
strength of evidence. Thus, quality assessment is not mandatory for them. Therefore, in
our study, we did not apply a formal quality assessment, since our objective is to map
the techniques that have been applied for the studied task and not to determine their
relative effectiveness. However, we acknowledge this as a limitation of our work.
3.5.3 Research validity
The research method chosen for this study is a systematic mapping study based on our
aim, i.e., to structure and provide an overview of existing work on issue report clas -
sification. Before conducting this review, we developed a review protocol following
the systematic mapping guidelines (Petersen et al. 2008 ; Usman et al. 2023 ). More than
one researcher was involved in the design and execution of the study, and the protocol
was iteratively improved before the final execution of the review process.
The data collected in the process of this study have been made available for
transparency and replication (including the list of primary studies, the list of papers
excluded from the review after full-text reading, and a complete map in the form of a
spreadsheet that readers can use to navigate the literature map).

Automated Software Engineering           (2026) 33:72 5.2 Lack of consideration of potentially relevant adoption factors
We observe that in the existing literature on issue report classification, there is
generally a lack of consideration of other potentially relevant adoption factors
beyond prediction accuracy. Other factors of concern for practical adoption are, for
example, maintenance cost, scalability, explainability, and adaptability (Rana et al.
2014 ; Paleyes et al. 2022 ). These factors do not always complement each other and
may require a trade-off. For example, while one technique may be more accurate
than another, it may score lower in terms of explainability .
Approximately 89% of the studies (41 out of 46) evaluated the proposed techniques
solely based on prediction accuracy. Only five studies took into account the factors
beyond prediction accuracy that may influence the adoption of these techniques in
practice. Among those, three focused exclusively on explainability; however, their
approach was limited and lacked real evaluation. Only two studies [PS26, PS32]
thoroughly studied the adoption factors for ML techniques. These two studies
emphasize that practitioners view these factors as important when deciding whether
to adopt an ML technique in their practices. These findings also align with the
existing literature that investigates factors influencing the adoption of ML techniques
in practice (Rana et al. 2014 ; Paleyes et al. 2022 ).
5.3 Lack of industrial validations and involvement of practitioners
We observe that existing research on issue report classification primarily uses archi -
val data from open-source repositories. Approximately 93% (43/46) of the studies
used archival data. In contrast, only a small number, 3 out of 46, have used propri -
etary or industrial data. It is important to evaluate these techniques in real industrial
settings to assess their performance, generalizability in other contexts, and practical
applicability.
Furthermore, the involvement of practitioners in these studies is notably scarce.
Approximately 91% (42/46) of the studies did not involve practitioners. The involve -
ment of practitioners is essential for gathering feedback on the proposed solutions
regarding their practical relevance and applicability.

Automated Software Engineering           (2026) 33:72 5.4 Need to use robust evaluation metrics and approaches
The data from the issue reports is often unbalanced (Laiq et al. 2023 ; Fan et al. 2018 ).
Therefore, it is important to use evaluation metrics that are robust to data imbal -
ance, such as AUC and MCC, when assessing automatic techniques. However, we
have observed a lack of these metrics in the literature. Only 6 of 46 studies have
used AUC, and only 5 of 46 have used MCC. This underutilization of robust metrics
can lead to misleading conclusions about the effectiveness of proposed techniques.
For example, Yao and Shepperd ( 2021 ) reanalyzed 12,471 pairwise comparisons of
defect prediction models and found that 21.95% of conclusions drawn using F1 were
reversed when MCC was applied instead, showing that some methods previously
reported as superior were, in fact, worse when evaluated using a balanced metric.
Similarly, Huang and Ling ( 2005 ) empirically showed that AUC is a better measure
than accuracy.
Similarly, we observe that robust evaluation approaches, such as 10-fold cross-
validation, are lacking in the reviewed studies. The majority (29 out of 46) rely
solely on a fixed test set for performance evaluation. In contrast, only 17 studies
employ cross-validation techniques, which are generally considered more reliable
to assess the performance of ML techniques. Relying on a single fixed test set
increases the risk of biased performance estimates. For example, Ghasemzadeh
et al. ( 2024 ) found that single hold-out evaluations often overestimated accuracy
compared to nested ten-fold cross-validation, leading to increased confidence in
the generalizability of a model. They reported that the statistical confidence in the
model, based on nested k-fold cross-validation, was four times higher than the
confidence obtained from the single holdout-based model.

the small data set and reported that it could improve performance compared to the
models trained on noisy labels. These findings emphasize the effectiveness of incor -
porating small, high-quality labeled datasets to overcome labeling inconsistencies.
Despite the proven impact of noisy ground truth on model accuracy and validity,
very few studies explicitly address the quality of issue reports classification data in
the current literature.
A promising approach to generating annotated data could be the use of LLMs,
given their capabilities, for example, to understand and generate code, process
natural language, and automate various software engineering tasks. This could be
done fully automatically or in a human-in-the-loop setting where LLMs assist human
annotators by pre-labeling, suggesting revisions, or highlighting uncertain cases. For
example, Colavito et al. ( 2024 ) showed that GPT-like models can label issue reports
in high agreement with human annotators (Cohen’s kappa > 0.7) in the absence of
labeled data. Such findings suggest that LLMs can substantially reduce the time and
cost associated with manual annotation. However, LLMs have their own challenges,
for example, LLMs may hallucinate, propagate biases present in their training data,
or exhibit sensitivity to prompt formulation and context. Consequently, we do not
advocate fully automatic labeling without verification. In line with previous work
(Colavito et al. 2024 ; Wang et al. 2021 ), we recommend using LLMs as part of an
annotation team. In this setup, LLMs can, for example, pre-annotate data or resolve
straightforward cases, while human experts validate complex or ambiguous instances,
thereby balancing scalability with reliability.

Automated Software Engineering           (2026) 33:72 5.6 Practical impact and adoption challenges of automated techniques for issue
report classification
The results of this study indicate that while several investigations have evaluated automated
techniques for classifying issue reports, there is limited research on the effectiveness of
these techniques in real industrial settings. A recent study by Laiq et al. (2024 ) offers
valuable insights from industrial validation in practical settings, demonstrating that
automated classification can significantly help triage processes in large-scale software
development. However, their study also points out essential adoption-related challenges.
For example, they report that practitioners’ feedback indicates that classification models
need to be explainable to build trust, must integrate seamlessly with existing toolchains,
and should adapt to changing contexts to handle concept drift.
Moreover, Laiq et al. (2024 ) noted that to improve the likelihood of adoption,
researchers should go beyond accuracy-focused evaluations using archival data and
address practical aspects that practitioners consider important when deciding to adopt
automated techniques (Rana et al. 2014 ; Paleyes et al. 2022 ). While the accuracy
of the models is essential for the technical effectiveness of classifiers, Laiq et al.
(2024 ) emphasize that it alone is insufficient. Their industrial study demonstrated
that even when a model achieved strong predictive performance on historical data,
practitioners remained skeptical due to concerns about trust, false positives, integra -
tion challenges, and model degradation over time. Their findings also highlight that
explainability, usability, and maintainability are equally important for adoption. If
these aspects are not addressed, classifiers may be technically accurate, but fail to
deliver value in real-world workflows. Therefore, evaluations must consider factors
such as how easily the tool fits existing processes, how transparent and interpretable
its predictions are, and how resilient it is to evolving project contexts.
To systematically address adoption factors and to guide the deployment of their
classification tool in an industrial setting, Laiq et al. (2024 ) used a technology transfer
model (Gorschek et al. 2006 ). This model provides a structured and iterative process
for moving research innovations into practice through stages of validation, feedback,
and gradual scaling. By following this approach, the researchers were able to involve
practitioners from the outset, conduct focus groups to elicit concerns, and conduct initial
pilot deployments, iteratively refining the tool based on feedback. The technology
transfer model thus helps ensure that technical, organizational, and human factors are
addressed in parallel, building confidence and buy-in at each stage. This structured
method is especially important for tools such as automated issue classifiers, which can
affect core development workflows and require trust to be effective. Therefore, for
future studies, we recommend using similar approaches to improve the likelihood of
adopting an automated technique in practice for classifying issue reports.
5.7 Issue report classification in the era of large language models
Recent research indicates that strong generative LLMs can often match the performance
of fine-tuned encoders in issue labeling, without requiring project-specific training. For
example, Colavito et al. ( 2024 ) report that GPT-3.5 achieves competitive zero-shot results
compared to fine-tuned BERT-style baselines in labeling GitHub issues. These findings
1 3Page 27 of 34    72

--- Page 28 ---
Automated Software Engineering           (2026) 33:72 suggest that cross-project generalization, which has traditionally been a challenge for
traditional models, may now be possible with promptable models, particularly when
combined with light prompt engineering or a few-shot examples. In addition to static
classification, LLMs (e.g., GPT-5) can facilitate interactive triage by asking clarifying
questions, extracting structured fields, and suggesting assignees. This implies that LLM-
centric pipelines could be promising for issue routing in industrial settings.
At the same time, using LLM-based classification involves practical trade-offs.
Relying on API-based usage raises concerns about cost and privacy, while closed
models can change over time, impacting reported metrics’ reproducibility and long-
term stability. The performance in zero or few-shot scenarios is sensitive to how
prompts are designed and worded, so evaluations need to ensure that prompts are
reliable and robust to small changes in phrasing. Furthermore, benchmarks should
continuously track the recency and origin of issues to prevent hidden data leakage
and accurately measure true generalization. To address cost and improve reliability,
hybrid designs, such as utilizing LLM embeddings alongside lightweight classifiers,
or employing retrieval-augmented prompts based on project documentation, can help
mitigate costs and enhance determinism, while maintaining a high accuracy level.
Looking ahead, we see three concrete research directions. (1) Few-shot and active
prompting: develop a budgeted selection of in-context examples drawn from a project’s
history, with explicit comparisons to fine-tuning and encoder baselines; report cost/
latency alongside accuracy. (2) Interactive triage loops: evaluate LLMs that ask for
targeted follow-ups, summarize ambiguous reports, and propose labels/assignees with
calibrated confidence, using human-in-the-loop protocols and task-time/overrides as
primary outcomes. (3) Evaluation standards: Besides standard metrics such as F1,
report prompt templates, model version/date, stability across re-runs, privacy posture,
and failure analyses (e.g., systematic mislabels, nondeterministic outputs). Overall, we
believe that BERT-based fine-tuning remains a strong baseline in data-rich, privacy-
constrained settings, while GPT-4-class (and newer models) models enable data-sparse,
cross-project, and interactive workflows that traditional pipelines struggle to match.
6 Conclusion and future work
In this study, we provide a comprehensive map of the automatic techniques for issue
report classification. Our results indicate that the existing literature applies a broad range
of solutions for issue report classification, including traditional ML and deep learning-
based techniques and more advanced large language models. As reported in the literature,
BERT-based models (e.g., RoBERta) are state-of-the-art models for issue report
classification. In addition, traditional ML techniques, such as logistic regression, SGD-
based classifiers, and SVMs, can achieve prediction performance comparable to complex
deep learning techniques and BERT-based models for the classification of issue reports.
We found that these studies lack practitioner involvement and fail to consider
other potentially relevant adoption factors beyond prediction accuracy, such as the
techniques explainability, scalability, and generalizability. In addition, the litera -
ture’s main focus is on archival data from open-source repositories. Therefore, future
1 3   72  Page 28 of 34

--- Page 29 ---
Automated Software Engineering           (2026) 33:72 research should address realistic/industrial settings, consider other potentially rel -
evant adoption factors, and actively involve practitioners.
This study focused on automatic techniques for classifying issue reports into bugs
and non-bugs (e.g., questions, feature requests, and documentation issues). In future
work, we will explore the potential of diverse labeling methods (e.g., skills, time to
solve, and GFI) to support issue triage. These labeling methods are essential for a
thorough triage process.
