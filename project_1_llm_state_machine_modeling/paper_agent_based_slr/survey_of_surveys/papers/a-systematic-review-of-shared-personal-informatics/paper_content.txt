Quantified  us A B S T R A C T
Personal  informatics  (PI) has gained great attention  and become ubiquitous  in people’s  everyday  lives. Although
an increasing  number of studies set out to explore the social aspects of PI, there remains  an unaddressed
opportunity  for a structured,  systematic  review to understand  why and how the sharing happened,  to inform
future design and research.  This systematic  review summarizes  the last 13 years of research  on the diverse
cases of shared PI practice  from ACM, PubMed,  and IEEE. 100 papers were analyzed,  and four types of sharing
were identified:  Interpersonal  targeting,  Public broadcasting,  Group monitoring,  and Community  exchanging.
Notably,  sharing extends beyond data exchange,  evolving  into a collaborative  process across different  PI stages.
The review offers a taxonomy  of shared PI practices  and delineates  design possibilities,  facilitating  future
exploration  in the field. Additionally,  it identifies  trends and patterns  within existing work, suggesting  design
opportunities  for future explorations  of shared personal  informatics.

1. Introduction
Personal  Informatics  (PI), also known as quantified  self, describes
the process  of people engaging  in self-tracking  activities  for the pur-
pose of gaining  an understanding  of oneself and achieving  a healthier
life [1]. The ubiquitous  sensing  technologies,  such as smart wearable
and mobile applications,  have largely reduced  the barrier for self-
tracking.  PI has grown out of the quantified-self  group and got accepted
by and integrated  into the general  public’s  daily life [2]. People track
various  aspects  of their lives, such as physical  activity,  mood, finances,
location,  dietary  intake, or anything  that can be tracked  and recorded
using emerging  sensors  or apps. Self-tracking  can sensitize  people about
multiple  aspects  of their daily life and thus support  them to gain
quantified  understandings  about themselves  and make progress  toward
achieving  a healthy  lifestyle  [3]. Moreover,  in the clinical  field, sensing
technologies  have been widely adopted  by clinicians  due to their
accessibility  and flexibility  for patients’  self-tracking  [4]. The process
of personal  informatics  practice  was described  in a classical  model as
a series of 5 stages: ‘‘preparation , collection , integration , reflection , and
action ’’ [1]. Preparation , where individuals  determine  their motivations
and methods  for collecting  personal  information;  Collection , involving
the observation  and recording  of personal  data such as thoughts,  behav-
iors, and interactions;  Integration , where collected  data is processed  and
transformed  to enable reflection;  Reflection , during which users explore
and interpret  their data, often through  visualizations;  and Action , where
insights  from the data inform decisions  and behaviors  to align with
personal  goals.
Recent researchers  pointed  out the trend of PI has transferred  from
‘‘self-centric’’  into interpersonal  contexts  constantly  [5] and showed
the inherently  social natures  of some PI tools [6,7]. Sharing  plays an
increasingly  significant  and inevitable  part of PI systems.  People share
their self-tracking  information  for various  purposes,  such as to gain
social support  [8], to create social comparison  [9–11], to engage social
sensemaking  [4,12,13], or to promote  behavior  change  [4]. As ex-
pected,  concerns  on privacy  [14], data control  [15], workplace  surveil-
lance [16,17], and commodification  [18] had also been noticed.  Col-
lective PI practices  are attracting  an increasing  amount  of research  en-
deavors.  The self-experimenting  members  continued  transferring  from
self-tracking  and self-awareness  into health collaboration  communities
like PatientsLikeMe  [13], to make their self-discoveries  ‘‘less anoma-
lous, statistically  significant,  and scientifically  rigorous’’  [19].
Previous  work has shown great interest  in addressing  the social
aspects  of PI and has provided  abundant  cases for us to study with [20–
24]. However,  the focus of a majority  of systems  has been emphasizing
on the sharing  of data (e.g., visualization)  [25–27] and the shared
reflection  [28–31] among multiple  users. Nonetheless,  in other stages of
personal  informatics  [1] (i.e., preparation,  collection,  and action stage),
a growing  body of work spotted  sharing  behaviors  and sharing  effects as
well. For instance,  a family member  may share the process  of choosing
the tracking  tool and implementing  the tracking  with the actual user who needs special care (shared  preparation  and collection)  [32]; a
group of runners  collect their pace and distance  data together  during
running  (shared  collection),  which improved  group performance  and
helped group members  stay together  (shared  action) [33]. Beyond  these
contributions,  several existing  frameworks  have explored  social sharing
in personal  informatics  mainly from a sharer-centric  approach,  such
as individual-to-population-level  transitions  [5,34] and collaborative
communities  [19]. Moreover,  current  research  has rarely systematically
examined  these diverse  cases of sharing  in personal  informatics  to
inform and inspire future design or research.  Therefore,  an opportu-
nity arises to systematically  summarize  current  design cases to more
comprehensively  understand  shared PI practices.
As such, we conducted  a systematic  literature  review of current
shared PI cases in the past thirteen  years (2010–2023)  to generalize  and
characterize  the sharing  practice  of PI to support  future designers  and
researchers.  A systematic  search was conducted  in the ACM, PubMed,
and IEEE databases,  and the papers were selected  based on our dedi-
cated criteria,  and summarized  using inductive  thematic  analysis  [35].
To generate  design-oriented  knowledge  relevant  to the domain  of HCI
(Human–Computer  Interaction),  we specifically  targeted  HCI due to
its interdisciplinary  focus on human behavior,  technology,  and design,
making  it a suitable  domain  for actionable  insights  that enhance  user
interaction  with technology.  This review addresses  the following  as-
pects: (1) Teasing  out and classifying  current  shared PI practices  across
the classical  PI stages [1]: context  (preparation),  collection,  media
(integration),  and evaluation  (reflection  and action),  to identify  why
and how sharing  happens;  (2) Identifying  potential  trends and design
opportunities  for future explorations  of shared PI.
Overall,  our study introduces  a novel perspective  by categorizing
shared PI practices  based on sharer-receiver  network  typologies.  We
identified  four major types of sharing:  Interpersonal  targeting , Public
broadcasting , Group monitoring , and Community  exchanging . Then we
characterized  these shared PI cases addressing  the classical  stage-based
model [1]. Besides  the commonly  addressed  ‘‘shared  data’’ (integration
stage) and ‘‘shared  sensemaking’’  (reflection  stage), we argue that
sharing  could actually  be carried out in all stages of a PI system.
Our results in general  show an increasing  research  interest  in the
past thirteen  years of social sharing  in personal  informatics.  Research
interest  in shared PI emerged  and was explored  in widespread  popula-
tions and clinical  fields beyond  self-tracker  communities.  The sharing
content  covers wider facets of people’s  life (e.g., mental health and
emotions)  than before (e.g., physical  activities,  food). We found that
current  shared PI research  places emphasis  on small groups with rela-
tively close relationships  or online groups with community  peers. We
also see an opportunity  in designing  shared PI for co-located  groups
(e.g., co-workers,  classmates,  lecture audiences,  etc.) under their spe-
cific co-experienced  context.  This review of the literature  characterized
various  shared PI cases, evolved  the definition  of this class of tools, and
addressed  specific  design opportunities  for future shared PI research.Information  and Software  Technology  185 (2025)  107759

2. Method
2.1. Literature  search strategy
To address  the research  question  of why and how sharing  occurs
in shared PI cases, we followed  Kitchenham’s  systematic  review pro-
cess [36] to gather relevant  cases within the field of HCI. We first
conducted  a preliminary  search to identify  existing  systematic  reviews
and potentially  related studies [36]. We assessed  our review of interest
in the field of design,  computer  science,  engineering,  and biomedical
databases:  Association  for Computing  Machinery  (ACM),  life science
and biomedical  (PubMed),  and technical  literature  in engineering  and
technology  (IEEE Xplore)  were identified  as our database.
Then we use various  combinations  of search terms derived  from
our research  question  [36]. In order to tease out articles  regarding
shared PI practices,  two keywords  clusters  were defined:  ‘‘PI’’ cluster
and ‘‘Share’’  cluster.  We identified  ‘‘PI’’ cluster by extracting  keywords
from key references  in this field [1,21,37–41], and we used the terms
that informed  in their papers as the searching  synonyms  of ‘‘PI’’ cluster.
The ‘‘Share’’  cluster was identified  based on synonyms,  alternative
spellings,  and the authors’  knowledge  of relevant  publication  head-
ings [36]. Such as ‘‘shared’’  [28], ‘‘sharing’’  [42], ‘‘social’’  [20,23,24],
‘‘interpersonal’’  [5,43], ‘‘collective’’  [5]. Entries were included  if the
title or abstract  contained  the ‘‘PI’’ cluster AND the ‘‘Share’’  cluster
(Table  1). The same query was used for every database.  In addition
to our search of three databases,  we manually  searched  for relevant
references  within highly-regarded  articles  [5,21,43,44] that addressed
shared PI, even if these articles  were not indexed  in the aforementioned
databases.  This approach  was adopted  to ensure the inclusion  of crucial
literature  that might have been missed in the initial database  search,
particularly  those with exceptional  relevance  and specific  alignment
with our review’s  scope. In terms of time span, our review covers
articles  published  from Jan. 2010 to Sep. 2023.
2.2. Article selection  and extraction
According  to the guidelines  in making  inclusion  and exclusion
criteria  [45], we first defined  what we mean by ‘‘shared  PI’’ in our
research  question.  We define shared PI as sharing practices  in PI studies,
instead  of narrowing  down the definition  to a class of systems.  Shared
PI includes  cases that embody  sharing  practices  appears  in any stage or
stages of a PI (preparation,  collection,  integration,  reflection,  action).
Previous  works defined  shared PI as ‘‘a class of tools that allows groups
of users to collect,  aggregate,  analyze,  and share personally  relevant
information’’  and name these tools as ‘‘interpersonal  informatics’’  [43].
They emphasize  on understanding  self better by comparing  with others
in their social network,  thus implying  that the sharing  is from multiple
sharers,  commonly  among peers. To build upon this definition  while
also involve  as many sharing  contexts  as possible,  we include  articles
addressing  a PI system with sharing  function  beyond  sharing  with
peers. For example,  there are cases including  one sharer sharing  data
with his/her  healthcare  providers,  which also fit the selection  criteria  of Table 2

An overview  of the article selection  process  followed  the PRISMA
guidelines  [46] and was described  in Fig.  1. An overall of 666 articles
were retrieved  through  the searches  within the three databases.  After
screening  the title, abstract  and full-text,  575 articles  were filtered  out
by our exclusion  criteria.  On top of that, 8 articles  which are proposals
or extended  abstracts  describing  a system development  in progress,
were excluded  because  no related follow-up  studies were found at the
end of the database  screening.  17 additional  works were included,  due
to the aforementioned  extra manual  search based on the references  of
highly relevant  articles.  Articles  describing  the same shared PI system
were combined  and counted  as one case in our list. Finally,  100 items
met all the inclusion  criteria  and were included  in this literature  review.
The data were extracted  using inductive  thematic  analysis  [35] to
formulate  the predefined  categories  of features  among the selected
articles,  as listed in Fig.  2. After the first exclusion  process,  a cross
review was performed  by the second coder (name).  Then the two coders
settled the disagreements,  summarized  the characteristics,  and refined
the subcategories  through  a series of collaborative  sessions.
3. Results
This section provides  an overview  of the selected  papers and identi-
fies four generic  types of shared PI practices.  It examines  the contextual
aspects  of sharing  and emerging  design trends while analyzing  data
collection,  media, and evaluation.  Together,  these analyses  highlight
key design aspects  and research  interests  in the HCI community.
3.1. Overview  of selected  papers
An overview  of the shared PI systems  in the selected  100 articles  are
characterized  and summarized.  The detailed  features  of these systems
are listed in Fig.  2 align with the publication  year.
3.2. Four generic types of shared PI
First of all, we categorize  these shared PI cases based on the ty-
pology of their sharer-receiver  network  (Fig.  3). Four types of sharing
are identified:  Interpersonal  targeting  (often one person to one person,
or O2O), Public broadcasting  (often one person to many persons,  or
O2M), Group monitoring  (often many persons  to one person,  or M2O),
Community  exchanging  (often many persons  to many persons,  or M2M).

•Interpersonal  targeting  (n = 25) often describes  a sharing  activ-

•Public broadcasting  (n = 10) often describes  a mono-directional
sharing  activity  without  necessarily  hoping others to share back.
For example,  a stroke patient continues  updating  his rehabilita-
tion vlog and shares his experiences  through  a public YouTube
channel  [47]. Another  example  is, one captures  his/her  memories
and feelings  about a specific  place using wearable  accessories
when cycling,  and post them online through  social networks  [48].
•Group monitoring  (n = 9) often describes  passive  or implicit  shar-
ing of relevant  information  from clients to professionals  under the
clients’ consent.  For example,  a healthcare  provider  monitors  mul-
tiple diabetes  patients’  medication  adherence  and blood glucose
through  cloud-based  applications  [49].
•Community  exchanging  (n = 56) often describes  a reciprocal  shar-
ing activity  within an ad-hoc community  where people have
similar goals or interests.  For example,  a group of primary  school
children  share their physical  activity  data in their classroom
through  a shared display  [14]. Another  example  is, users with mo-
bility impairments  share their activity  data with peers who have
similar impairments  through  the website  PatientsLikeMe  [13].
Several  related frameworks  have also characterized  social sharing  in
personal  informatics  from different  perspectives.  Paton et al. presented
a three-level  framework  to describe  self-tracking  information  sharing
that can develop  from self-experimentation  to group experiments,  and
ultimately  to the entire population,  which can enable sharing  between
health systems  [34]. Swan sets a vision of the shift from ‘‘n = me’’ to
‘‘n = we’’, as self-tracking  individuals  come together  in collaboration
communities  [19]. Murnane  et al. explored  the individual  patient’s
social relations  in PI-supported  treatment  for bipolar  disorder  [5] based
on the ecological  system theory [50]. However,  as different  from
these mentioned  frameworks,  in our work, we formulate  the shared PI
categories  based on the typology  of sharer-receiver  networks  (instead
of a sharer-centric  perspective).  As a result, our categories  reveal
agreements  with, as well as new insights  in addition  to these prior
categories.  For instance,  the receivers  of Interpersonal  targeting  (O2O)
category  are aligned  with the receivers  described  to have a ‘microlayer
relationship’  with the sharer in  [5]. And the Public broadcasting  (O2M)
category  has receivers  that correspond  to receivers  who have an ‘exo-
layer relationship’  with the sharer as described  by [5]. Moreover,  our
categories  also surface  two distinct  types of shared PI practices  that are
not explicitly  differentiated  by the prior frameworks:  Group monitoring(M2O),  and Community  exchanging  (M2M).  By differentiating  these four
types of shared PI practices  that have rather different  sharing  cases, we
hope to build on the prior works while also help designers  to more
concretely  see the possibilities  in the design of shared PI.
3.3. Contextual  aspects of shared PI
To understand  the context  of shared PI practices,  generalize  pat-
terns for specific  user groups,  and engage sharing  in future shared PI
design,  we characterize  the diverse  shared PI cases from the following
contextual  aspects:  sharing purpose  (why people share), sharer (who is
sharing),  receiver  (sharing  to whom),  group size (how many people are
involved),  sharing content  (what content  is being shared),  and sharing
platform  (the tools for sharing).  In this subsection,  we first extract the
contextual  aspects  of sharing,  followed  by comparing  the four types of
sharing  addressing  each aspect. Then, we map these contextual  aspects
of reviewed  cases over time and summarize  the trend.
3.3.1. Identify  the contextual  aspects
We identify  six contextual  aspects  of these shared PI cases. The
extracted  keywords  and descriptions  are presented  in Fig.  4. We further
cluster the four types of sharing  addressing  each contextual  aspect in
detail (Fig.  5).
•Sharing  purpose:  We characterize  the purposes  that motivate  peo-
ple to share their self-tracking  data to others into 6 sub-classes:
exchange  support  (48%); get information  (21%); comparison
(9%); connectivity  (10%); collaboration  (11%); and reward  (1%).
Phrases  in the articles  like ‘‘support  others in rehabilitation’’  [47],
‘‘receive  and give support’’  [51], ‘‘community  empowerment’’  [52]
are categorized  as ‘‘Exchange  support’’,  because  the sharing  pur-
pose is for giving and receiving  social support.  When people share
their data for gaining  answers,  feedback,  or comments  from oth-
ers, the purpose  is categorized  as the sub-class  ‘‘Get information’’.
The purposes  that describe  a situation  to ‘‘leverage  the data of
others to support  the understanding  of one’s own data’’ [53], ‘‘to
compare’’  [20,27,54], or ‘‘social sensemaking’’  [23] are classified
under the ‘‘Comparison’’  sub-class.  Sharing  to improve‘‘social
connection’’  [55], ‘‘social connectedness’’  [56] are categorized  as
‘‘Connectivity’’.  ‘‘Collaboration’’  describes  the purpose  of ‘‘sup-
port collaboration’’  [57], ‘‘collaborative  sensemaking’’  [58], or
‘‘facilitate  a collaborative  process’’  [18]. The sharing  in [59] is
stimulated  by monetary  incentives,  so the purpose  is described  as
‘‘Reward’’.  The sharing  purpose  of Public broadcasting  is mainly
for exchanging  support  (80%). On the contrary,  Group monitoring
mainly aims at getting information  from experts  and profession-
als (78%) (Fig.  5a). Community  exchanging  commonly  aims to
exchange  support  (61%) and social comparison  (14%).Information  and Software  Technology  185 (2025)  107759

3.4. Data collection  of shared PI
Tools for data collection  in shared PI practices  are not limited  to
use PI tools, which means the initial purposes  of inventing  these tools
are not necessarily  designed  for PI practice.  For example,  people use
commercial  systems  like social media [51] as data collection  tools for
food-tracking  in social PI practice.  Understanding  how people use these
techniques  to collect data and social aspects  in PI practice  can facilitate
future designers  and researchers  to identify  key areas (e.g., what tool
can be used in specific  PI data collection,  what roles can be involved  in
data collection  process)  for data collection  in future sharing  practice.
Therefore,  in this subsection,  we will put our emphasis  on the data
collection  tools, techniques  as well as how social aspects  affect the data
collection  process.
3.4.1. Techniques  of data collection
There are three ways for data collection  addressing  different  levels
of automation  in shared PI practices:  self-report,  mixed sensing  (semi-
automatic),  and automatic  sensing.  Regarding  current  collection  tools
that applied  in these shared PI practices,  23% cases use self-report,
44% systems  use automatic  sensing  devices,  and the remaining  33%
use mixed methods  to combine  sensor data with self-report  data to
enrich the context  of the collection.  The selection  of data collection
tools depends  on the data type (Fig.  9). Automatic  sensing  devices
can efficiently  and automatically  facilitate  the self-tracking  process.
Steps [78], locations  [77], heart rate [23], skin conductivity  [79], and
blood glucose  [49] become  accessible  anytime  and anywhere  by the
increasing  sensing  devices.  However,  technologies  cannot cover every

3.5.1. Forms of feedback
The form of feedback  is content-oriented,  it depends  on the explic-
itness of the shared information.  98% of all the shared PI cases use
the visual channel  as a feedback  format because  of its efficiency  in
delivering  information.  Some case explores  other feedback  channels
(e.g., taste [26], hearing  [74]), which reveals the potential  possibility  in
other formats  of data sharing  when the requirement  of data explicitness
is not high. The feedback  carrier in these systems  are often services
for the diverse  sharing  request.  When the sharing  aims to deliver
information  efficiently  and precisely  (e.g., for clinical  use [49]), the
feedback  carrier places a priority  on general  platforms  such as mobile-
based applications  [75] and web-based  online platforms  [86]. When
the sharing  aims to reach wider audiences,  social media can be the
best choice.  For instance,  a stroke patient shares his rehabilitation
experiences  using live-stream  on YouTube  [47]. When the sharing
happens  within a small group of co-located  users, a shared display  may
fit the best for interaction  and communication  [31,33,74,81]. When the sharing  happens  within two people,  face to face talk (e.g., [25]) or other
creative  formats  (e.g., ambient  lighting  [79], drinks [26]) get a chance.
3.5.2. When the feedback  is provided  to users?
The aggregated  data can be presented  timely or be delayed,  and
the timing is usually  driven by the users’ needs. When the system is
designed  to immediately  create awareness  or to motivate  upcoming
action [79], real-time  feedback  is generally  preferred.  For instance,
ASHA is a self-tracking  application  with timely feedback  addressing
the user’s self-performance  compared  to their peers [54]. When the
user needs an aggregated  results of a day, the feedback  would presents
daily accordingly.  For instance,  TastyBeats  create drinks as a reward  for
users based on their activities  during the day [26]. When the feedback
is needed  in the regularly  scheduled  meetings  [4], the gathered  data
might be sophisticated  in a high-resolution  format and be prepared  for
the counseling  [30]. One case describes  a highly-engaged  PTSD (Post-
Traumatic  Stress Disorder)  patient to collect symptom-related  data and
discuss it with his therapist  during the therapeutic  sessions  [87].
3.5.3. Privacy concerns  of feedback
The social aspect drives privacy  concerns  on the sharing  contents.
With the presence  of others, the sharing  content  may differ from the
collection  content.  Some data that be collected  may only for personal
reflection  rather than sharing  with others. 45% data collection  con-
tents are overlapped  with those going to be shared.  55% of cases
describe  users interact  with or make reflections  on the full data set,
and leave selective  part for sharing.  Commonly,  low-private  level data
(e.g., physical  activities,  food, thoughts)  are generally  accepted  for
sharing;  whereas  high-private  level data (e.g., body data, stress, sleep)
are commonly  collected  and kept for self-reflection.  Besides,  the re-
ceivers’  role can also make a difference  in the sharing  and not-sharing

There are three approaches  to achieve  privacy  protection  in current
shared PI cases: (1) anonymity;  (2) ambiguity;  and (3) aggregation.
Anonymity  is a direct way for privacy  protection,  it is widely used
in offline sharing  [14,31], as well as online sharing  [12]. Either users
themselves  take control  of the anonymity  level [83,88] or the system
is designed  with an untraceable  identity  [27,31]. Anonymity  is widely
applied  and is applicable  for most sharing  contexts.  Ambiguity  makes
abstract  the sharing  either from the source (data) or from the end
(format).  When the system designed  for interaction  and entertainment
over precise number-delivering,  the collected  data can be ambiguity
from the source.  For example,  Moodlight  [79] uses an ambient  lighting
system to represent  emotion  ambiguously;  social HUE supports  the
abstract  share of physical  activity  data to create social connectivity
between  the elderly and their caregivers  [89]. When users need to
access the results clearly and precisely  by themselves,  the ambiguity
may happen  at the tail before sharing.  For example,  DiaBeaThis  system
obfuscates  blood glucose  data into fuels and embeds  it in a social
game [90]. Aggregation  is commonly  applied  when the aggregated
result is meaningful  or interests  the users. For instance,  users may
need an aggregated  result to understand  the overall performance  of
a running  group [33] or a community  [27,74,81], to compete  with
other communities  as an entity [91], or to view and interact  with
a cooperated  activity  [79]. Though,  as can be noticed  in the paper
list (Fig.  2), not-aggregated  presentations  (visible  self and others in
the group) stay the most popular  feedback  content  in these shared
PI practices.  Noticeably,  some scenarios  concern  less about privacy
protection  under specific  conditions.  Either the shared data has low-
private level (e.g., steps [33,78], open-shared  memory  [92]), or the
receivers  are trusted people (e.g., partner  [26], friends [79], HCPs [76],
co-workers  [88]).
3.5.4. Summary
We described  the feedback  of shared PI in this section addressing:
feedback  forms, timing as well as privacy  concerns.  The feedback
format depends  on the resolution  of the delivered  information,  visu-
alization  shares the majority  part than other formats  in current  shared
PI cases. The feedback  is commonly  made approachable  to the users
and the delivery  time is often driven by users’ needs (real-time,  daily,
weekly,  etc.). Meanwhile,  not all the collected  data can be shared.  Users
should take control  of what to share and what not. Three ways can
be leveraged  to tackle privacy  concerns  in current  shared PI cases:
anonymity,  ambiguity,  and aggregation.  The aspects  of feedback  forms,
delivery  time, and privacy  concerns  discussed  in this section correspond
to the ‘‘integration  stage’’ in the classical  PI model [1].
3.6. Evaluation  of shared PI
We categorize  the evaluation  of the shared PI practices  according
to the evaluation  goal and approach.  Table  3 describes  the goals and
approaches  to evaluation  in the literature.  22% of the cases were eval-
uated to understand  the interactions  among users (coded as ‘‘UU’’). For
instance,  evaluate  to understand  the interpersonal  interactions  between
patients  and clinicians  [4,57]; to understand  users’ data-sharing  behav-
ior [25] and sharing  motivations  [74]; or to understand  events [72] or
group effects [84] on users. Among  these, a majority  part of the evalu-
ation (68.2%)  deploys  qualitative  methods  to understand  users’ social
behaviors  and collaborations.  34% of cases emphasize  ‘‘Users–System
interaction’’,  coded as ‘‘US’’. For instance,  evaluate  how users interact
with the system [32]; how the system affect users’ awareness  [26,79],
reflection  [30,31,93], modified  behaviors  [85,88], social interactions
and group performance  [33]. The evaluation  method  is relatively  di-
verse and commonly  accompanied  by semi-structured  interviews  or
surveys.  3% of cases focus on ‘‘System’’  evaluation  (coded as ‘‘S’’).
Namely,  to evaluate  system performance  [94], system usability  [95],
and system feasibility  [86]. Cases to evaluate  system acceptance  and
usefulness  are often accompanied  by user experiences  [64,83]. 20% studies focus on evaluating  interpersonal  interaction  and user–system
interaction  (coded as ‘‘UU + US’’). And 17% cases focus on user–system
interaction  and system evaluation  (coded as ‘‘US + S’’). Four papers do
not mention  specific  evaluation  methods  [87,94,96,97].
Previous  research  either evaluates  the work as a PI system or a PI
practice.  It is rarely seen works emphasize  evaluating  the effects of
sharing  practices  in each stage of PI. Echo with Rooksby  et al. [39],
the evaluation  of a shared PI is beyond  the measurement  of ‘‘be-
havior change  on gained insights’’,  rather, it is also likely to focus
on the gaining  of shared understanding  and social interpreting.  For
instance,  more reflection  [30], increased  awareness  [26,79], improved
motivation  [33], engaged  logging  [85], social collaboration  [62] are
commonly  addressed  in these shared PI cases. As for the evaluation
approach,  qualitative  research  methods  can facilitate  researchers  to
understand  why and how questions,  and are mostly addressed  to un-
derstand  users or users’ experiences  of the design.  Quantitative  analysis
is mostly applied  through  collecting  users’ usage or interaction  through
device logs, mostly addressed  to evaluate  the system.  By understanding
the effects of sharing  on each stage of PI from various  cases, it is
beneficial  to develop  evaluation  approach  that applied  specifically  for
shared PI practice.
4. Discussion
This paper reviews  current  shared PI practices  generated  over the
past 13 years. We characterize  diverse  shared PI cases in light of
the classical  model of PI stages (preparation,  collection,  integration,
reflection,  action) [1]. In this section,  we discuss some major insights
into ‘‘shared  PI’’ gained from this review.  Moreover,  we summarize  the
design opportunities  based on our findings,  followed  by implications
for future research  in designing  shared PI systems.
4.1. There is more to shared PI than the sharing of data
Personal  informatics  (PI) is ‘‘a class of tools that help people collect
personally  relevant  information  for the purpose  of self-reflection  and
gaining  self-knowledge’’  [1]. As shown in our review,  an emerging
number  of studies set out to explore  the social aspects  of PI. Over-
whelmingly,  current  explorations  involving  social aspects  of PI cases
have been focused  on the sharing  of data visualization  or sense-making
activities  among people,  i.e., in the stage of integration  and reflection.
Nonetheless,  by classifying  and extracting  a diverse  set of shared PI
cases, we also find that the act of social sharing  of PI does not only
concern  the data display  (integration)  or sense-making  (reflection),  but
also concerns  other stages of PI, such as the stage of preparation  [32,
49,57,62,88], collection  [33,56,79,85], and action [33,54,76,84,85,91,
106]. And in each of the PI stages, the act of social sharing  can be a
‘‘double  edged’’  sword that implies  both benefits  and risks, and thereby
needs to be carefully  designed  for.
Therefore,  a key insight generated  from our review is that designing
shared PI is beyond  merely designing  shared data visualization,  or
shared sense-making  activities.  Rather,  it can also mean designing
shared preparation  (e.g., the targeted  user is not familiar  with new tech-
nologies,  an intermediary  help to prepare  the tracking  and support  the
target beneficiary  to maintain  health [32]), shared collection  (e.g., tools
to support  group fitness are developed  and deployed  to collect real-
time performance  data for running  groups [33]), or shared action
(e.g., specific  group activities  (walk together  after lunch) were found
in an employer-sponsored  health program  [91]). And this suggests  a
broader  and exciting  space for future design to explore  and facilitate
different  acts of social sharing  throughout  the PI stages. As such, from
our understanding,  shared PI practices  could be described  as follows:

4.2. Potential  opportunities  for shared PI research
From the literature  review,  personal  informatics  is getting more
and more adopted  among the general  public with the development  of
ubiquitous  sensing  techniques.  Research  interests  increasingly  empha-
size the sharing  of diverse  facets of life for various  purposes  via PI
systems.  Hence a trend can be seen that social sharing  has become
an important  property  in many emerging  PI designs.  In addition  to
the findings  discussed  above, here we identify  the remaining  gaps and
design opportunities  with the aim of informing  future research.
4.2.1. Opportunity  1: Leverage  the benefits  and minimize  the risks in social
comparison
Based on our results,  sharing  is mostly driven by exchanging  sup-
port, get information,  and comparison.  And we notice that there is
an increasing  emergence  in comparison-aimed  sharing,  in which users
are enabled  to compare  their own data with others [23]. It rooted
in social comparison  literature  in the psychology  field [135]. On the
one hand, social comparison  in PI context  can be sometimes  stressful
and burdensome,  for example,  if one is left behind by peers. On
the other hand, it can assure users by implying  the ‘‘normal’’  pat-
tern or ‘‘overall’’  distribution  of others, or motivate  users to achieve
more by displaying  others’ progress.  Among  our reviewed  designs,  the
comparison-aimed  cases mostly target generic  users [20], students  [82],
workers  [27], event participants  [54] sharing  with family, friends,  and
peers. Comparison  in patients  and special-care  groups are relatively
rare, which suggests  both challenges  and opportunities  to explore
proper and beneficial  social comparison  for these users. In general,
to leverage  the benefits  and minimize  the risks in comparison  under PI context,  designers  can design the comparison  between  users with
similar conditions.  For example,  in the context  of mobility-impairment
users’ activity  tracking,  participants  show a great desire to compare
with peers with similar mobility  impairments  to discover  new possibili-
ties in rehabilitation  [13]. When the situation  is relatively  comparable,
the experiences  from peers would show more value to learn from.
Meanwhile,  the relatively  fair comparison  would not make users feel
anxious  about being left behind too much by peers. Additionally,  the
privacy  protection  should be embedded  in design to minimize  risks
through  (semi-) anonymous  sharing  [31], social avatar design [14],
or thoughtfully  design nuanced  levels of ambiguity  in the interface,  to
mediate  between  privacy  and social translucence.
4.2.2. Opportunity  2: Tailor shared PI design to specific user group and
ad-hoc scenarios
Our analysis  reveals an increasing  focus on tailoring  shared PI
to specific  user groups and their unique contexts,  moving  beyond
generic  use cases. This trend underscores  a shift from early adopters,
such as quantified-selfers  [2], toward  broader  demographics,  including
less tech-savvy  users and professional  or special-care  domains.  For
instance,  our findings  indicate  that shared PI is being integrated  into
highly contextualized  scenarios  such as clinics [76], workplaces  [88],
classrooms  [14], and family settings  [69], signaling  a growing  need
for domain-specific  solutions.  This evolution  calls for a deeper un-
derstanding  of the nuanced  needs, routines,  and social dynamics  of
targeted  user groups within their ad-hoc environments.  Rather than
applying  generic  design principles,  researchers  and practitioners  should
leverage  insights  from our results to explore  how shared PI can adapt
to the specific  workflows,  cultural  norms, and collaborative  patterns
of distinct  populations.  For example,  shared PI in a classroom  might
emphasize  engagement  through  gamified  activity  tracking  to foster peer
interaction  [14], while workplace  applications  might prioritize  social
negotiations  to combat  sedentary  behavior  [72]. To advance  this area,
designers  should aim to create context-sensitive  solutions  that not only
enhance  users’ engagement  but also mitigate  potential  negative  impacts
on existing  routines  and social structures.  By grounding  the design
process  in the unique attributes  of specific  scenarios,  this approach
paves the way for more impactful  and ethically  mindful  applications
of shared PI.
4.2.3. Opportunity  3: Ask first what sharers expect from sharing before
whom they want to share with
Our characterization  of the four sharing  types identified  the re-
ceivers’  roles by the relationship  with the sharer. Echoing  with [71],
receivers  can either be strangers  or from the user’s existing  social
networks,  and sharers  are expecting  differently  from different  roles
of receivers.  Combined  with our findings,  O2O sharers  expect to get
support,  comments,  or stimuli from family and friends;  O2M sharers
expect to get interactions,  ‘‘views’’,  or ‘‘likes’’ from public media; M2O
sharers  expect professional  comments,  feedback,  or suggestions  from
medical  experts;  M2M sharers  expect community  peers’ to share back. Information  and Software  Technology  185 (2025)  107759

However,  the users’ ‘‘expected  receivers’’  may differ from what they
really shared with. For instance,  when we ask users to whom they
would like to share their personal  data, they are highly likely not going
to say ‘‘strangers’’.  Whereas,  a lot of previous  research  had stated, to
some extent, people tend to share with strangers  with similar conditions
or experiences  rather than family or friends [71]. One example  shows
that sharing  personal  intimate  information  (e.g., infertility)  to strangers
through  the online community  makes infertile  women  feel more com-
fortable  and secured  than sharing  with friends and colleagues  [12].
Therefore,  instead  of asking ‘‘to whom you want to share’’ questions,
designers  should ask (and investigate  in-depth)  ‘‘what do you expect
from sharing’’.  In other words, designers  can turn the way around  by
understanding  sharers’  latent expectations  and desires first, then design
to catch the ‘‘perfect’’  receivers.
4.2.4. Opportunity  4: Further explore shared PI for co-located  groups
Based on the trend of sharing,  current  research  interests  in shared
PI put great attention  on the remote sharing  (e.g., share with peers
from online forums).  However,  the design of shared PI for co-located
groups (e.g., co-workers  [88], classmates  [82], lecture audiences  [64],
wedding  participants  [65], local neighborhood  [74], etc.) is relatively
rare. We see an opportunity  to design shared PI for co-located  user
groups under their specific  co-experienced  context  (e.g., occupational
stress, lecture presentations,  wedding  experiences,  marathon  races,
neighborhood  events, etc.). Co-located  sharing  or physically  sharing  in-
formation  involves  the presence  of others, which can foster immediacy
and intimacy  [136], it creates a sense of interpersonal  connectedness
and has some unique affective  benefits  that are less likely afforded
in remote sharing  [137]. Co-located  shared PI can engage in conver-
sations and co-interpretations,  it can be inspiring,  stimulating,  and
engaging  in sociability.  However,  not all interpersonal  interactions  are
welcoming  and engaging.  Accordingly,  designers  should beware  of the
co-located  interpersonal  dynamics  (e.g. comparisons  [84], social pres-
sures, impression  management  concerns  [138], biased judgments  [31],
awkward  [139]), and the negative  emotional  outcomes  (e.g., stressed,
guilty, embarrassed  [140,141]) in design for the co-located  user groups.
Therefore,  while designing  for co-located  PI suggests  many unad-
dressed  opportunities  for future designers,  it also poses new challenges
regarding  social acceptance  and willingness.  Designers  are tasked to re-
solve the tension  between  these benefits  and the concerns.  For instance,
designers  can emphasize  the benefits  of sharing  to targeted  users by
inviting  their trusted parties (e.g., managers  to employees,  teachers  to
students)  to introduce  or to take part in the design process.  Such as,
inviting  managers  to participate  in the design of mood-sharing  tools
in the workplace  for their staff engage acceptance  of self-tracking  and
benefits  both parties under their existing  structures  (e.g., team cohesion
and empowerment  of employees)  [27]. Additionally,  designers  may
need to caution  the inadvertent,  unintended  self-revelation  and the risk
of automated  disclosure  [79] in co-located  sharing.  Designers  can have
more nuanced  solutions  for privacy  protection  or mediation  by navi-
gating the approaches  that we summarized  in our review:  anonymity,
ambiguity,  and aggregation.  For example,  designing  synchronized  ac-
tivity tracking  using pseudonymous  avatars  for co-located  students  in
the classroom  can afford a more public discussion  without  comprising
their privacy  [14]. Designers  can also leverage  game design elements  in
these non-game  contexts  (gamification  [142]) to ease the sharing  and
address  certain burdensome  facts in a fun and playful way.
4.2.5. Opportunity  5: Addressing  asymmetrical  needs of sharers and re-
ceivers in shared PI systems
Our analysis  highlights  the critical need to design shared PI systems
that address  the asymmetrical  needs of sharers  and receivers.  This
asymmetry  becomes  especially  evident  in scenarios  where stakeholders
have distinct  roles, such as patients  and doctors.  For instance,  while
patients  might require  detailed  tracking  data, including  progress  re-
ports and graphs for self-reflection,  doctors  may find such granular  information  overwhelming  and instead  benefit from succinct  overviews
to support  clinical  decision-making.  Although  previous  work has em-
phasized  sharers’  needs, our findings  reveal a broader  opportunity  to
design systems  that balance  these asymmetrical  requirements  across
diverse  contexts,  not limited  to healthcare.  For example,  shared PI
systems  in workplace  collaborations  or family health tracking  similarly
require  nuanced  understanding  and design to accommodate  differing
priorities  and interaction  patterns  among stakeholders.  Our review
underscores  the importance  of engaging  both sharers  and receivers
during the design process  to uncover  shared and diverging  needs [21].
By incorporating  co-design  practices  and iterative  feedback  loops [4],
researchers  and practitioners  can ensure that shared PI systems  are
better aligned  with the goals of all users. This approach  not only
enhances  system usability  but also ensures  equitable  support  for all
parties,  paving the way for more inclusive  and effective  shared PI
applications  across varied domains.
4.2.6. Opportunity  6: Lower the sharing barriers  by adding options for
fine-gained,  selective  sharing
Users show hesitation  about sharing  because  they may worry their
sharing  spamming  others [71,143,144]. Moreover,  the sharing  content
may disclose  the contextual  information  that users intend to kept
private [42]. These concerns  hold back on users’ sharing  activities
and thus prevent  them from experiencing  the benefits  of sharing  [71].
Therefore,  we see an opportunity  in designing  worriless  sharing  to
lower the sharing  barriers  and make the sharing  more likely to de-
ploy. To not worried  about spamming  others, Epstein  et al. suggest
developing  the system to facilitate  users to avoid oversharing  [42]. To
avoid concerns  about disclosing  contextual  information,  designers  can
empower  sharers  with the ability to selectively  share information  and
control  the level of granularity  [42]. In other words, sharers  should
be able to freely select specific  people for sharing  or able to flexibly
editing data before sharing.  Designers  should keep sharers’  autonomy
in worriless  sharing.  However,  to be noted, while enabling  such fined-
grained,  selective  controls  could enhance  user autonomy  and reduce
their worries,  these controls  should be provided  as additional  options
and should not be overburdening  the users. In other words, users should
still be able to share their data via minimal  steps in an effortless  way.
As a result, here designers  are tasked with the challenge  of balancing
user autonomy  with the system’s  ease of use.
4.3. Limitations
This literature  review has some limitations  that need to be men-
tioned. First, we only reviewed  the scientific  literature  in the three
major databases  for literature  search. Although  they are the predom-
inant sources  for the targeted  research  domain,  and we did involve
references  in these papers to identify  potential  external  papers,  it is still
possible  that we have excluded  some related publications  that are not
covered  by these databases.  Second,  by restricting  our search on shared
PI practices  in scientific  literature,  we may have missed relevant  sys-
tems that are not been published  but are used in practice,  these systems
should be studied  in future research.  Third, given the nature of quali-
tative analysis,  the categorization  and characterization  of the reviewed
design cases might have been influenced  by the coders’ subjective  bias.
Although  cross review and cross revision  between  coders and external
researchers  were conducted  to neutralize  the potential  subjective  bias,
it is still noteworthy  that the categorization  and characterization  in this
work should remain open for future improvement  or debate.
5. Conclusion
This paper reviews  the existing  work on shared PI over the recent
13 years, aimed for analyzing  and characterizing  current  cases to
inform future design and development.  We identified  the different  types
of sharing  and characterized  them by sharing  purposes:  Interpersonal  Information  and Software  Technology  185 (2025)  107759

targeting  (O2O), Public broadcasting  (O2M),  Group monitoring  (M2O),
Community  exchanging  (M2M).  We analyzed  how contextual  factors
differ in different  types. O2O type often describes  a sharing  activity
between  two individuals  with relatively  close relationships.  O2M type
often describes  a mono-directional  sharing  activity  without  necessarily
hoping others to share back. M2O type often describes  a specific  user
group passively  share their high-private  data to limited  numbers  of
professionals.  M2M type often describes  a reciprocal  sharing  activity
within an ad-hoc community  where people have similar goals or inter-
ests. We also examined  the feedback  design and system evaluation  of
the existing  studies.  Based on our review,  we argue that the definition
of shared PI should be evolved  beyond  the sharing  of data. Rather,
it should concern  all stages of PI, including  preparation,  collection,
integration,  reflection,  and action. Along all these stages, we address
design opportunities  for designers  and researchers  to navigate  the
benefits  and risks of design social sharing  in personal  informatics.  In
doing so, we aim to contribute  a set of design-oriented  knowledge  for
the community  to jointly advance  the research  and design of shared PI
systems.
