Mobile application accessibility is crucial for digital inclusion.  This paper presents a systematic literature review (SLR)
following PRISMA guidelines, synthesizing advancements in accessibility issue detection and repair techniques. We
analyze representative mobile accessibility tools and industrial practices to complem ent SLR evidence. Nine major
databases (ACM DL, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, Wiley, Web of Science, Scopus, CNKI, arXiv) were searched, with 76 high -quality studies published u p to July 2025 analyzed. Common accessibility issues are categorized
into four types —perceptibility, operability, understandability, and robustness —aligning with user capability
modeling.  In accessibility issue detection, evolution from static analysis to deep learning and l arge language models
(LLMs) has shifted research from rule -based matching to semantic reasoning, enhancing automation and
generalization. However, detection and repair remain loosely coupled and fragmented, with high false -positive rates
and weak inter -module feedback loops. For repair, we propose a taxonomy of rule- driven, learning -based, and LLM -
assisted approaches, revealing a gap between detection breadth and repair research depth.  LLMs show potential for
semantic understanding, issue reasoning, and repair generation, paving the way toward intelligent agents  for
accessibility. Looking ahead, future research should focus on semantic -enhanced detection, multimodal repair, refined
user capability modeling, and unified evaluation standards —collectively advancing accessibility engineering toward
an accessibility -by-default design paradigm .

ACM Trans. Softw. Eng. Methodol.  1 INTRODUCTION
In recent years, with the rapid development of mobile Internet and intelligent terminal technologies,
mobile applications have been deeply integrated into people's daily lives, becoming core tools for
information access, social participation, work completion, and service consumption [1]. According to
Ericsson ’s Mobility Report 2024, by the end of 2024 the number of global mobile subscriptions had
reached 8.7 billion, of which smartphones accounted for as high as 82%, totaling 7.16 billion devices [2]. Users can download various mobile apps  to access diverse functionalities —such as online shopping, travel
navigation, remote work, and health management— thereby enjoying unprecedented convenience brought
by digital life [3]. However, while digital services are becoming pervasive, a large number of users still
struggle to use mobile apps equally and smoothly due to visual, auditory, motor, or cognitive impairments,
facing systematic exclusion caused by the “digital divide. ” According to statistics from the World Health
Organization, 1.3 billion people worldwide live with signific ant disabilities involving different degrees of
functional impairment, accounting for 16% of the global population [4]. China also has  about  85 million
people with disabilities and 264 million adults aged 60 and above [5], which means hundreds of millions
of users encounter various operational barriers on mobile devices .
Mobile application accessibility  refers to the extent to which a mobile software system, throughout its
design and implementation, enables users with diverse abilities to perceive content, operate the interface,
understand interactions, and complete tasks under robust system support  [6]. The Web Content
Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) [7] articulate four fundamental principles —Perceivable, Operable,
Understandable, and Robust (POUR) —and, in the mobile context, provide a terminology system and
evaluation bas eline via mechanisms and assistive technologies (AT)
1 such as screen readers,
magnification/captions, target size, focus order, and gestures [8]. In addition, the international standard
ISO/IEC 25010 [9] treats accessibility as a sub -characteristic of usability, forming a key component of the
software quality model and interlinking with attributes such as compatibility/interoperability, reliability,
maintainability, and portability. This offers a standards -based rationale for establishing accessibility as a
quality attribute in software engineering (SE) and f or systematically operationalizing it across the SE
lifecycle (requirements –architecture–implementation –testing –operation and maintenance).
Empirical evidence suggests that improvements in accessibility are associated with enhanced user
experience [10][11], as well as improvements in overall product quality and market adoption [12][13][14][15]. Accordingly, accessibility in mobile applications has emerged as an important concern
in software engineering, with both social and technical implications.
As mobile apps increasingly serve as the primary channel through which the public obtains information,
participates in social life, and enjoys services, the existence of standards only lays the conceptual
groundwork. To truly realize mobile accessibility, an engineering pathway that is measurable,
reproducible, and integrable must be established at the SE level. How to incorporate accessibility from the
earliest design stage and sustain it throughout the SE lifecycle [16] [17]  has thus become an important
yardstick for software quality and organizational engineering capability [18][19]. Given platform diversity,
device constraints, and interaction complexity, mobile apps pose higher demands on SE practice [20] .
Accordingly, both academia and industry have conducted extensive research on accessibility issue
detection and repair for mobile apps [21] [22][23][24][25][26][27][28] and have  achiev ed progress in
automated detection, intelligent repair, and standards -based evaluation. In particular, the introduction of
deep learning and LLMs  has pushed accessibility detection from rule -based matching toward semantic
reasoning, enabling a shift from “surface -level visible defects ” to “semantic -level design flaws. ”
Meanwhile, platform specifications (e.g., accessibility APIs on iOS and Android) and developer tooling (e.g.,
assistive testing frameworks and automated checkers) continue to mature. This line of work aligns with SE

Nevertheless, current research still suffers from fragmented detection capabilities, high false- positive
rates, and a weak coupling between detection and repair, and has not yet formed a systematic accessibility
assurance mechanism spanning the full SE lif ecycle. Despite growing domestic and international efforts,
there remains a lack of a systematic and comprehensive survey that synthesizes progress in this field —
especially regarding comparative analyses of issue types, detection and repair pathways, syste m
frameworks, and evaluation metrics. Therefore, this paper aims to systematically review research on mobile accessibility detection and repair, and to construct a reproducible evidence baseline and
methodological framework, providing the SE community with  a verifiable and extensible foundation.
This study conducts a SLR following PRISMA guidelines, covering ACM DL, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect,
SpringerLink, Wiley, Web of Science, Scopus, CNKI, arXiv, and other databases (time window up to July 1,
2025). The process includes de- duplication based on  “title + first author + year (with DOI assistance), ”
dual -reviewer independent staged screening and quality assessment, as well as backward and forward
snowballing. In total, 76 high -quality studies are included.
Based on this corpus, an evidence baseline is established across three main threads —“issues –
detection –repair. ” An accessibility issue taxonomy is then proposed, aligned with WCAG 2.x (POUR), user
abilities, and software engineering concerns, mapping abstract design principles to concrete interaction
barriers and repairable elements. The evolution of detection tech niques is further analyzed along a
trajectory from “static → dynamic → hybrid → model -driven → LLM -assisted, ” with a comparative
examination of th eir trade -offs in coverage, automation level, false- positive cost, and platform adaptation.
Finally, repair strategies in mobile contexts are systematically synthesized, including rule -
driven/optimization -based, machine learning -based, and LLM -assisted app roaches. Their assumptions,
effectiveness, and potential side effects are analyzed, revealing key breakpoints in the “ detection –repair
” chain and possible bridging directions.
These findings provide actionable insights for the development of high -quality, accessibility -oriented
software engineering systems.
(1) We conduct a PRISMA -guided SLR, consolidating mobile accessibility research up to July 1, 2025, and, after de-
duplication, screening, and quality assessment, include 76 high -quality papers as a starting point for future work
on accessibility issue ta xonomy, detection, and repair.
(2) We propose an accessibility issue classification framework compatible with WCAG, user abilities, and SE
perspectives, mapping abstract accessibility principles to specific interaction barriers, thereby providing
theoretical support for issue modeling a nd method selection.
(3) We distill a five -stage evolution pathway for detection techniques —“static –dynamic –hybrid –model -driven–
LLM -assisted ”—and systematically analyze their similarities and differences in terms of coverage, automation,
and platform adaptation.
(4) We complement the underexplored “repair ” dimension of mobile accessibility by summarizing mainstream
repair strategies, discussing their  limitations, and exploring the application potential of emerging techniques
such as LLMs for automated repair.
(5) By integrating accessibility standards and regulations worldwide, we discuss challenges and prospects for
evaluation -system construction and tooling -level engineering deployment, offering references for building
verifiable, transferable, and continuously e volving accessibility -oriented SE frameworks .
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews background and related work;
Section 3 describes the survey methodology. Sections 4 –6 establish an accessibility issue taxonomy for
mobile apps and map it to WCAG (Section 4), summarize detection methods across stages and analyze their
assumptions, inputs/outputs, coverage, and false -positive costs (Section 5), and synthesize
rule/optimiz ation -based, learning -based, and LLM -assisted repair strategies with a discussion of their
background , approaches, scope, strengths, and weaknesses (Section 6). Section 7 discusses challenges and
future directions; Section 8 outlines threats to validity and limitations; and Section 9 concludes the paper.

2.1 Accessibility Standards & Guidelines
As a critical quality attribute, mobile application accessibility  must rely on a clear and operational
standards system to be effectively implemented. On the one hand, accessibility standards provide unified
technical specifications for development, design, and testing, ensuring the systematic integration of
accessibili ty throughout the software lifecycle; on the other hand, they are prerequisites for building
automated detection tools and enabling quantitative evaluation. At the same time, the standards sy stem
also underpins the formulation and supervision of accessibility policies. Therefore, establishing scientific, authoritative, and continuously evolving accessibility standards is a key foundation for promoting fairness
and accessibility in digital envi ronments.
At present, a relatively comprehensive global standards system for accessibility has been established.
Table 1 summarizes representative standards and guidelines, ranging from internationally applicable rules
(e.g., WCAG
2 2.x[5] [31] , EN 301 549), to mainstream platform specifications (e.g., Android
Accessibility[32], iOS Human Interface Guidelines[33]), and further to national -level policy standards (e.g.,
GB/T 37668 -2019 General Technical Requirements for Accessibility Software [34]). Together these form
a multi -layer support system from content principles to platform adaptation. These standards specify
systematic technical requirements in terms of perceivability, operability, understandability, and
robustness, offering broad design references for user groups with different abilities. In particular, at the
mobile -platform level, both iOS and Android integrate rich AT APIs (e.g., VoiceOver, TalkBack, accessibility
labels, focus management), significantly reducing the cost of implementi ng accessibility features.
Moreover, the “four principles ” proposed by WCAG are highly abstract and generalizable, and have been
widely used to guide accessibility design for web content and parts of mobile app semantic structures.

Compared with desktop apps or web content, mobile application accessibility  involves more touch, speech,
and multimodal interaction features, and thus requires targeted design based on platform -specific
development tools and testing mechanisms. For example, Apple iOS provides the VoiceOver speech
feedback system and the Accessibi lity Inspector [35] visualization tool, while Android integrates the
TalkBack screen -reading service and the Accessibility Scanner [36] automated checker. In recent years,
Chinese v endors such as Huawei and Xiaomi have also released their own accessibility guidelines and
capability APIs, further improving native platform support.
Although these standards have played a positive role in advancing accessibility, many objective problems
and technical challenges remain in practice and deserve attention:
1)Insufficient applicability. Current accessibility evaluation still mainly relies on Web- oriented guidelines
such as WCAG, lacking standards dedicated to mobile contexts. Matos et al. [37] point out that mobile accessibility evaluation lacks mobile -specific guidelines, forcing evaluators to refer to documents originally
intended for web pages. As a result, there are no explicit requirements for mobile- specific interactions such
as multi -touch gestures, sensor- based input, or voice commands, making it diffi cult for existing standards
to effectively cover accessibility issues in these functionalities.
2)Poor cross -platform compatibility. Although native Android and iOS each provide accessibility APIs, they
are inconsistent, and cross -platform development frameworks do not fully expose these differentiated
capabilities. Mascetti et al. [38] found that whil e iOS and Android screen -reader APIs overlap substantially
in functionality, mainstream cross -platform frameworks (e.g., React Native, Xamarin) often encapsulate
only part of them, with some basic accessibility interfaces not supported at all. If develop ers need such
missing functions, they must write platform -specific code, which increases development and testing
complexity and requires separate accessibility adaptation for different platforms.
3)Insufficient technical granularity of national standards. China’s GB/T 37668- 2019, as the first domestic
technical standard for accessibility software, carries positive institutional significance, but provides limited fine-grained guidance for real operati onal scenarios. It largely follows WCAG and related standards to
regulate accessibility for web pages and mobile websites/apps, and does not offer specific guidance for
emerging interaction forms such as wearables, VR/AR, or AI (W3C only released the XR Accessibility User
Requirements (XAUR) guideline in 2021 [39]). International studies [40] show that for these new -
technology domains, accessibility recommendations often lack unified standards comparable to WCAG.

accessibility design, weakening the rigor and scientific grounding of the methodology.

apps, and, together with EN 301 549 and platform development guidelines (Android/iOS, etc.), constitutes a reliable baseline [44]. The key challenges now l ie in how to refine abstract clauses into executable
control -/state- level checks and repair constraints, and how to implement and verify them in CI/CD
pipelines. Accordingly, we take WCAG as the benchmark to map guideline clauses to concrete issue types, while specifying the evidence items required for determination, thereby offering a unified reference for
subsequent studies (e.g., dataset construction, benchmark evaluation, and cross -tool alignment).
2.2 Mobile Application  Accessibility Detection
Mobile accessibility detection aims to automatically or semi -automatically identify whether interfaces and
interactions violate WCAG and platform guidelines. It plays a crucial role in supporting developers to
incorporate accessibility design throughout th e entire software lifecycle, and meanwhile provides
technical assurance for systematic accessibility evaluation [45]. Common evidence sources include: the accessibility tree (e.g., value/state, focus order), layouts/resources (XML), pixel -level screenshots
(contrast, touch target size, occlusion), and runtime events (TalkBack/VoiceOver callbacks).
In recent years, both industry and academia have made substantial progress in mobile accessibility
detection. A variety of practically valuable tools and frameworks have emerged, and detection methods and technical routes have shown increasingly diversified and intelligent development trends.
At an early stage, mobile accessibility evaluation mainly relied on manual testing: accessibility experts or real users, under AT conditions, simulated real interactions to assess the accessibility properties and
structural design of UI elements [46] . Such methods can identify design defects that hinder understanding
and operation in depth, but they heavily depend on human judgment, incur high labor costs, and exhibit poor reproducibility; in fast -iterating development processes, it is difficult to balance coverage and
timeliness [45][47] . Especially when mobile app versions are updated frequently, the maintainability of
manual evaluation declines markedly, limiting its long -term usability. To address these challenges,
research and engineering practice gradually shifted toward automated detection to improve efficiency ,
reproducibility, and issue localization capability [48] . Automated approaches typically embed accessibility
checks into development frameworks or testing workflows, enabling developers to perfo rm accessibility
detection alongside functional testing and thus realizing an integrated “testing- as-detection ” paradigm.
Following the traditional view, these approaches are often categorized into manual inspection, automated detection, and user testing [49] . As methodologies evolved, the research community re- examined detection
mechanisms along two axes: (1) the analysis -strategy axis —static analysis (source code, UI layouts, and
resources) versus dynamic analysis (runtime UI states and interaction behavi ors) [23] [50]; and (2) the
development- process axis —development- time detection versus runtime detection [51][52] . The former is
suitable for early testing during the build stage, whereas the latter emphasizes simulating real user usage scenarios.

By jointly considering detection mechanisms, analysis strategies, and levels of intelligence, we propose a
five-category taxonomy: static detection, manual dynamic inspection, dynamic testing framework
integration, GUI automated exploration and behavior an alysis, and AT simulation with intelligent -
augmented detection. This taxonomy is intended to more systematically present current mainstream
methods and their evolutionary trajectory. Compared with prior classifications centered on detection
phases or analy sis strategies, our taxonomy better reveals differences among methods in detection
capability, applicable scenarios, and integration depth, providing a more actionable framework for subsequent research and systematic tool development.
2.3 Mobile Application  Accessibility Repair
Accessibility repair focuses on translating “detected issues ” into implementable interface/code changes.
It aims to preserve accessibility while minimizing disruption to original visuals and interactions. Compared
with detection, repair has a narrower reachable scope, because many strategies depend on prerequisites
(e.g., adjustable style parameters, available text resources, and no mobile app crashes after modification).
With the continuous improvement of mobile accessibility detection capabilities, the research focus has
gradually shifted from merely identifying problems to proposing effective repair strategies. This shift is
not a simple technical iteration, but an inevi table trend driven by real development needs. Although
detection methods can uncover a large number of accessibility issues [23][54][55][56][57][58][59] ,
leaving repair entirely to developers often leads to efficiency bottlenecks and a lack of specialized expertise. This has motivated ongoing research toward automated repair, so as to reduce developer burden
and improve repair efficiency.
Despite many explorations in accessibility repair, systematic surveys in this area remain scarce. Nie et al. [28] offer mainly an overview: although they touch on issues and tools, their organization of
methodological mechanisms and comparative dimensions is still not systematic, making it difficult to
compare, accumulate, and reuse methods across studies. To fill this gap, we, for the first time, propose a repair -technique taxonomy for mobile apps from two dimensions: (1) the degree of automation, and (2)
the way repair is embedded into the development workflow. We summarize three mainstream categories:
rule -driven and optimization -oriented repair, ML -based repair, and LLM -assisted repair. This taxonomy
helps clarify the technical routes, engineering feasib ility, and evolutionary trends of different methods,
providing both theoretical grounding and practical references for future research and system design.

ACM Trans. Softw. Eng. Methodol.  3    SURVEY METHODOLOGY
In this survey, we adopt the SLR paradigm. Specifically, we formulate research questions (RQs), design
search strings and data sources, define inclusion/exclusion criteria, conduct independent manual
screening, perform quality assessment (QA), and finally synthesize and analyze the collected evidence.
3.1 Research Questions
Our objective is to summarize, categorize, and analyze published studies related to accessibility issues,
accessibility detection, and accessibility repair. Centered on issue taxonomy, detection, and repair for
mobile application accessibility , and aligned with the four WCAG 2.x principles, we address the following
three RQs:
RQ1. How can mobile application accessibility  issues be systematically classified and mapped under the
WCAG framework? What are the manifestations and triggering contexts of each category in mobile settings, and how do they relate to user abilities?
RQ2. What is the evolutionary trajectory of mobile application accessibility  detection techniques, and
what coverage, automation level, strengths/limitations, and applicable scenarios does each line of work
exhibit?
RQ3. How can the landscape of mobile application accessibility  repair techniques be categorized, and
what are their repair pipelines, generalizability, and functional performance?
By answering these RQs, we are able to: (i) unify terminology and granularity across studies, creating a
shared issue space for subsequent benchmarking and comparison; (ii) trace the evolution of detection
methods and provide comparable analytical dimensio ns in terms of coverage, automation level,
strengths/limitations, and applicable scenarios, thereby supporting method selection and tool reuse; and
(iii) construct a strategy -oriented synthesis for repair, distilling the use cases, pros/cons, and feasibili ty
gains and limitations of LLMs in repair, helping both researchers and practitioners identify which types of issues are best addressed by which repair approaches.

ACM Trans. Softw. Eng. Methodol.  4.1 From Standards to Engineering: Redefining the Relationship Between Accessibility and the
Software Lifecycle
Accessibility is not a local optimization at the UI layer; rather, it is a systemic engineering requirement
driven by differences in user abilities [65] . Users with visual impairments rely on voice navigation and
linear focus paths [66] ; users with hearing impairments rely on captions and visual cues [67] ; users with
cognitive impairments and older adults rely on consistent [68] , low -burden interaction structures.
Therefore, systems must support diversified perception and operation pathways, rather than merely
satisfying interface -level traits such as “visible ” and “clickable. ” This requirement is especially salient
in mobile app contexts characterized by touch -centric interaction, limited viewport, and strong reliance on
mobile AT.
However, the long -standing lag of accessibility to the end of development is not caused by a lack of
technology, but by process discontinuities. Studies show only 37% of teams incorporate accessibility
requirements at the requirements stage, which increases rework costs by 60%; 58% of teams do not use
accessibility guidelines during design, leading to prototype defect rates as high as 75% [19]. As a result,
late-stage “patch -style repair ” is not only costly and ineffective, but also fails to produce a consi stent
user experience. The root cause is that WCAG and ISO define targets such as perceivability, operability,
understandability, and robustness (the What), but do not specify how to implement them, who is
responsible, or at which stage to enforce them (th e How). The absence of a responsibility mapping from
standards goals to engineering mechanisms leads to accessibility being misconstrued as a UI add -on rather
than a manageable software quality attribute.
To close this gap, we propose a “ framework for mapping accessibility standards to engineering
practice ” (Fig. 4). The framework follows a Why –What–How logic: user -ability differences constitute the
source of requirements (Why); WCAG/ISO are abstracted into standards goals (What); these goals are
further mapped into executable engineering capability domains (How), including information perception,
interface operation, interaction logic, semantic structure, multimodal support, API compatibility, event
managem ent, and system stability. Through a one- way mapping, abstract principles are transformed into
implementable, verifiable, and maintainable engineering responsibility units. This framework strictly
separates standards goals from engineering mechanisms: the four principles are no longer treated as
system capability types, but as normative constraints over engineering capability domains. The capability
domains themselves are measurable, assignable, and integrable, providing structural carriers for detection,
repair, and automated assurance. They are also consistent with the “perception –action–cognition ”
model, offering cognitive plausibility rather than an experience- based taxonomy.
The formation of engineering capability relies on four collaborative mechanisms: (1) user -ability
differences determine system support boundaries; (2) AT provide alternative input/output channels (e.g.,
Accessibility Service); (3) the mobile app layer exposes interpretable semantic structures and focus orders;
and (4) the system layer guarantees cross -device robustness and event pathways. This shows that
accessibility is a cross -layer collaborative outcome rather than a single UI attribute.
Accordingly, Fig. 4 provides an executable path from normative principles to engineering mechanisms,
redefining accessibility as a software quality attribute that is driven by ability differences, constrained by
standards goals, and realized through engineering capabilities. Accessibility is no longer a supplementary
requirement added at the end of development, but a systemic objective that must be designed,
implemented, verified, and maintained.

The framework translates accessibility standards into engineering capability domains that can be
embedded into the software development lifecycle, providing a practical route from principles to

Although the framework is cross -platform in nature, its engineering path is particularly suited to mobile
app contexts, where the ecosystem faces device fragmentation, touch -first operation modes, and heavy
dependence on mobile AT (e.g., TalkBack, VoiceOver). Using the framework only requires identifying user -
ability needs and partitioning engineering responsibilities according to standards goals, without reliance
on specific tools or platforms. It supports requirements modeling, design specifications, usab ility
evaluation, and AT -compatibility validation. The expected benefits manifest at three levels: engineering-
wise, shifting from end -stage patching to early -stage prevention; user -wise, improving information
accessibility and task efficiency; and system -wise, forming transferable and reproducible structural
baselines that enable continuous evaluation and maintenance. Thus, the framework not only provides
structural foundations for engineering accessibility issues on mobile, but also offers a pathway for
mechanistic explanations of typical mobile -accessibility issue categories in the following sections.
In sum, the mapping framework establishes a complete “standards –pathways –verification ” chain for
accessibility, laying the groundwork for lifecycle integration, automated detection, and repair, and signaling a shift of accessibility from an externalized feature toward a core objective of SE.

This framework does not assume that standards can be automatically enacted. Instead, accessibility
truly becomes a software quality attribute only under explicit engineering prerequisites: at the requirements stage, a verifiable model of ability differences must be produced; at the design stage,
traceable semantic and interaction structures must be established; at the implementation stage,
accessibility APIs and alternative pathways must be embedded into the software artifact itself; at the
testing stage, A T-driven and task -oriented verification mechanisms must be adopted; and at the
maintenance stage, interface consistency across devices and versions must be ensured. Without these
mechanisms, accessibility remains at the level of ideals and cannot enter the engineering system. Based on
this, we systematically map accessibility to the SDLC, transforming it from “implementable standards
goals” into “executable process mechanisms ” (Table 4). This mapping clarifies each stage’s core
tasks, inputs/outputs, and re sponsibility boundaries, enabling an engineering leap from “can be
implemented ” to “must be executed. ”
(1) Requirements stage: accessibility as a peer engineering input.
Through ability -difference modeling and task analysis, users ’ perception and operation constraints
are translated into verifiable and traceable requirement specifications, which enter design and testing
alongside functional requirements. Outputs at this stage shift from abstract advocacy to engineering- level
constra ints.
(2) Design stage: structured decisions replace ad -hoc considerations.
Design artifacts must explicitly include semantic structures, focus paths, redundant interactions, and
fault- tolerance mechanisms, defining structural boundaries for subsequent code binding and verification.
Accessibility thus moves from UI ornamentation to an integral part of system structure.
(3) Implementation stage: accessibility enters the software artifact.
By invoking accessibility APIs, exposing semantic information, and constructing alternative operation
pathways, accessibility capabilities are compiled into code and system interfaces rather than attached as
external patches. The output is reusable, deploy able, and integrable accessible components.
(4) Testing stage: accessibility becomes part of quality assurance.
AT-driven, task -oriented, and regression -based verification identifies defects and generates test results
on par with functional defects, giving accessibility quantifiable, regression -ready, and traceable
engineering channels.
(5) Maintenance stage: accessibility as a continuously maintained quality attribute.
Through interface -stability validation and environment -compatibility checks, capability degradation is
prevented, enabling cross -version evolution and turning accessibility from a one- off task into a long- term
maintenance objective.
Through this mapping, accessibility is structurally aligned with the SDLC: architecture provides the
hosting locus, while the lifecycle provides the execution path. Accessibility thereby shifts from an optional
UI-layer feature to a full -process engineerin g responsibility, strictly matching SE ’s core logic —
requirements -driven development, structural decision -making, implementation binding, quality
verification, and version evolution.
Taken together, Fig. 4 and Table 4 demonstrate that accessibility is not an external add -on, but an
internal quality objective. Fig. 4 provides the capability structure, while Table 4 provides the lifecycle
mechanisms. Based on these, our study for the fir st time operationalizes accessibility as a modelable,

4.2 Current Status of Mobile App lication Accessibility Issues
To systematically summarize the state of research on mobile application accessibility  issues, we analyzed
39 relevant studies and, drawing on WCAG ’s four core principles, constructed an issue taxonomy tailored
to mobile contexts. This taxonomy integrates the mapping model above and absorbs recurrent barrier
scenarios from user studies and technical practice, aiming to build an analyzable framework th at is
mappable between theoretical abstractions and real user experience. Large- scale empirical evidence
indi cates that nearly all mobile apps exhibit accessibility defects to varying degrees, and these defects
commonly cause substantial difficulties for users with visual, hearing, motor, and cognitive impairments [69]. Therefore, a scientific and systematic issu e taxonomy is essential not only for locating weak links in
accessibility practice, but also for advancing mobile -accessibility research. Based on the above evidence
and interaction model, we summarize typical accessibility issues into four categories —corr esponding to
information perceivability, interface operability, interaction understandability, and system robustness —
and analyze their impacts on different ability groups.
(1) Perceivable problems (Perceivable).
This category primarily affects users ’ ability to obtain and recognize UI content, including textual
information, images, icons, button states, and so on. The most impacted groups include users with visual impairments, color -vision deficiencies, cognitive impairments, and motor impairments. Fo r example, Chen
et al. [23] focused on assessing accessibility levels of Android apps. They analyzed 1,000 popular apps from Google Play across 33 mobile app categories. Using a combination of automated tools and manua l
inspection, they identified and classified 11 major accessibility issues, such as insufficient text contrast, overly small clickable areas, and missing screen -readable text. Their results show that 46.25% of mobile
apps  have at least one severe accessibility issue, and about 70% lack content descriptions that are critical
for users with visual impairments. This large -scale analysis reveals the severe state of Android accessibility
and underscores the need to pay greater attention to accessibility design a nd testing during mobile app
development. Another study on 12,000 Android apps collected from Google Play found that most Android
apps suffer from serious accessibility issues [54]: 46.25% contain at least one defect related to “screen -
readable text, ” while 60.91% present problems with “ touch target size. ” Even critical government
service apps are not exempt. Mateus et al. [55], investigating the accessibility of Brazilian government
mobile apps (whose usage increased sharply at the time), applied the MATE tool [56] to automatically test
15 mobile apps  in three groups: health, public banking, and social/information services. They categorized
detected issues according to WCAG and BBC mobile accessibility standards. Their findings show that many government apps still have severe accessibility problems, in cluding untagged CAPTCHA images, unlabeled
form fields, insufficient color contrast, and inaccessible buttons and UI activators. In particular, low image
contrast is the most frequent issue, accounting for over  30%. Mateus et al. further observed that many
dynamic content changes cannot be perceived by screen readers, causing users with visual impairments to
miss important information [70]. From nearly 5,000 disability -related reviews extracted from Google Play,
Oliveira et al. found that overly small fonts, indistinguishable icons, and weak color contrast are the most salient complaint types  [71] .
These issues significantly reduce users ’ ability to understand interface structure and content
hierarchy, preventing them from completing basic information -perception tasks. They are the most
common and foundational issue type in current accessibility design. Table 5 details the main mobile-

(4) Robustness problems (Robustness)
This category concerns the sustained availability and compatibility of mobile accessibility across
platforms, versions, devices, and AT, serving as the underlying support that determines whether
accessibility mechanisms can run stably. Typical issues are s hown in Table 8. The primary affected groups
are users who rely on AT (e.g., screen readers, voice control, switch devices), especially users with visual
impairments and those with high dependence on assistive services [97] .
Software updates should ideally create opportunities to improve UX and repair  accessibility defects.
However, a growing body of evidence indicates that updates frequently trigger “accessibility regression
”: previously supported accessibility features are removed, interaction logic becomes disordered, or AT
compatibility breaks, which severely undermines the continuity of use and accessibility guarantees for
disabled users. This phenomenon reflects insuffici ent robustness in current systems under version
evolution, and can further induce cascading degradation in key dimensions such as operability and
understandability.
From a technical -mechanism perspective, Huq et al. [17] emphasize that certain updates, while
optimizing visual design, can damage semantic annotations and focus structures, preventing screen -reader
users from completing previously workable workflows. More over, accessibility behaviors may vary across
visual themes, exposing weaknesses in robustness of development and support tooling. Existing tools often
exhibit interface rendering confusion or assistive -feature failures under dark mode. Ma et al. [82] thus  call
for explicit consideration of dark mode when developing support tools, implying a need for stronger robustness to accommodate different visual themes.
From a user perspective, Oliveira et al. [95]  analyzed 694 Google Play reviews that reported post- update
accessibility issues, and manually coded them under WCAG 2.2. They found that about 52% of comments

In addition, fragmentation and implementation differences in platform standards are another major
contributor to robustness failures. Across platforms (e.g., Android vs. iOS), OS versions, or regional variants, accessibility API coverage, AT response logic , and semantic -labeling mechanisms often diverge.
Mobile a pps may perform well on one platform yet become hard to use on another [98][99] . Compounding
this, mainstream accessibility evaluation tools lack unified standards and transparent scoring, frequentl y
yielding situations where “one platform passes while another fails. ” Such cross -platform inconsistency
and non -uniform standards increase adaptation costs for developers and weaken the sustained usability
and evaluability of accessibility in multi -platform ecosystems, forming a systemic robustness challenge.
Aljedaani et al. [100], using the Chromium project as a case study, conducted an empirical analysis of
mobile -platform accessibility issue reports to examine developers ’ and organizations ’ responses. They
found that although accessibility reports have increased in recent years, their repair  cycles are generally
long, largely because these issues are often assigned low priority. Moreover, Android exhibits significantly more accessibility issues than iOS, with a rising trend over time, whereas iOS -related iss ues decline
annually. Across all issue reports, only 4% were accessibility -related —3% for Android and 1% for iOS —
while the remaining 96% concerned other issue types. The study suggests that Android ’s higher counts
may stem from its more diverse device ecosystem, larger user base, and higher Chromium release
frequency. Rapid iteration can introduce new accessibility issues within short development and evaluation
cycles. As the population of AT users grows and related reports continue to rise, the authors argue that
developers must invest more resources to systematically identify and resolve these issues, improving overall accessibility.
Robustness problems are difficult to uncover through static detection alone; they are often buried in
processes such as version changes, framework replacement, or API shifts, and thus require version -
comparison testing and AT regression testing to capture.  The core challenge is maintaining accessibility
consistency and stability under system evolution —an SE quality -assurance gap that urgently needs
strengthening.

5 ANSWER TO RQ2: WHAT IS THE EVOLUTIONARY TRAJECTORY OF MOBILE APP LICATION
ACCESSIBILITY DETECTION TECHNIQUES, AND WHAT COVERAGE, AUTOMATION LEVEL,
STRENGTHS/LIMITATIONS, AND APPLICABLE SCENARIOS DOES EACH LINE OF WORK EXHIBIT?
Building on the analysis of 20 representative detection studies, this section systematically reviews the
evolution of mobile application accessibility  detection. Prior taxonomies often start from “manual –
automated –user testing” or “static –dynamic,” and/or “development- time–runtime. ” In practice,
however, these boundaries frequently overlap, and thus fail to capture tool -level differences in detection
capability, semantic understanding, and integration depth. By jointly considering detection mechanisms,
anal ysis strategies, and levels of intelligence, we identify five categories: static analysis, manual dynamic
testing, framework- integrated dynamic testing, automated GUI exploration and behavior analysis, and AT
simulation with AI -enhanced testing. This taxon omy more directly reveals the evolutionary path and
suitable scenarios of mobile accessibility detection. Compared with traditional stage - or strategy -centric
classifications, it better exposes differences across methods in coverage, automation, and engineering
integration, providing an actionable reference framework for future research and systematic toolchain
construction.
5.1 Static Analysis
Static analysis is among the earliest and most fundamental approaches to mobile accessibility detection. It
identifies violations of accessibility design guidelines by analyzing source code, layout files, and resource
configurations, without running the ap p, and is therefore suitable for early development stages [45]. Such
methods help developers surface potential issues during coding, reduce late- stage rework cost, and
improve overall code quality and maintainability.



--- Page 22 ---


ACM Trans. Softw. Eng. Methodol.  In industry, Android Lint [104] is one of the earliest static tools with accessibility checks. Introduced by
Google in 2011 and deeply integrated into Android Studio, it performs comprehensive analysis over project
structures, including Java/Kotlin code, X ML layouts, and resource directories. For accessibility, Lint mainly
checks:
 Whether visual components provide appropriate content descriptions (e.g., missing contentDescription);
 Whether input fields are correctly labeled and grouped so that AT can identify their purpose;
 Whether custom view components override AT -required accessibility interfaces and methods[105];
 Other common code- quality issues, such as missing localized strings or layout performance defects that affect
accessibility .
Fig. 5 shows the basic architecture and workflow of static detection.

Fig. 5 Architecture of static accessibility detection
Although static detection provides a fast and efficient early -stage localization mechanism, it has
inherent limitations [23] [49][50][52][56] . First, static analysis only processes properties derivable from
code artifacts, and thus cannot detect runtime- only accessibility problems —for example, whether touch
target size meets minimum accessibility thresholds; whether color contrast remains compliant across screen densities and visual themes; or whether dynamically loaded components are correctly registered
to AT during execution. Second, over- reliance on static detection can create a false sense of safety, because
key issues —such as whether widgets are focusable in real runtime contexts, or whether semantic
information is lost due to contextual changes —only surface through simulated or real user interactions.
Hence, while static detection is indispensable as a starting point, it cannot cover all problems and must be combined with dynamic methods to form a more complete detection system.
Beyond Android Lint, academia has proposed multiple research prototypes for static accessibility
analysis on mobile, expanding rule coverage to dimensions such as UI hierarchy depth, semantic
completeness of labels, and validity of resource references. These advances continue to push static approaches toward stronger semantic understanding and rule generalization, providing a solid foundation
for later integrated automated frameworks.

ACM Trans. Softw. Eng. Methodol.  5.2 Manual Dynamic Testing
Manual dynamic testing is one of the earliest methods used for mobile accessibility evaluation. It typically
requires developers or testers to operate a running mobile app with AT and manually verify the
accessibility properties of UI components [46]. This approach is highly accurate, especially for semantic errors, context -dependent problems, and defects in dynamically generated content, but it is inefficient and
knowledge- intensive, and thus mainly serves as supplementary validation for critical or high -priority
modules.
A typical manual workflow includes: navigating screen by screen, activating a checker on the current
screen, scanning for accessibility issues, and obtaining instant feedback on accessibility properties of UI
elements. Most tools provide screen -level checks and hints; their basic architecture is illustrated in Fig. 6.

Fig. 6 Architecture of manual dynamic testing
With the maturation of mobile development environments, representative manual tools have emerged
in both industry and academia, including:
 Accessibility Scanner (AS) (Android)[36]: a Google -developed mobile app that provides real -time accessibility
improvement suggestions. Users must activate it manually and scan each screen. Checks include missing
alternative text for non- text elements (e.g. , contentDescription), insufficient contrast, too -small touch targets,
duplicate labels, and overlapping operable elements. These checks are implemented via Google’s open -source
Accessibility Test Framework (ATF) [106], which supports multiple accessibility rules on Android.
 Accessibility Inspector (iOS) [35]: Apple ’s native tool that exposes accessibility properties of all UI elements in
an app, including labels, values, and hints. Developers can inspect elements one by one to identify missing labels
or inconsistencies, or enable built- in validation to automatically flag issues such as non -text elements lacking
alternative descriptions.
 IBM AbilityLab Mobile Accessibility Checker (MAC) [107]: supports both iOS and Android and can be integrated
into development environments to detect accessibility issues early. It checks for missing descriptions,
insufficient contrast, improper element sizing, and disordered focus logic. During user interaction, it captures
issues and generates feedback reports, strengthening accessibility awareness during development.
 UI Automator Viewer (Android) [108]: part of the Android SDK that captures UI hierarchies from real devices
or emulators and provides detailed properties such as coordinates, size, class name, resource ID, and content
descriptions. Patil et al. [109] enhanced it into an improved UI Automator Viewer that supports multi- device
parallel checking and integrates touch/key event recording and replay, enabling “semi -automated” dynamic
testing. The added contrast check further broadens its applicability.



--- Page 24 ---


ACM Trans. Softw. Eng. Methodol.  Despite their direct usefulness, manual tools have notable limitations. Studies show that manual testing
is inherently “labor -intensive and time- consuming, ” requiring screen -by-screen operation and repeated
tool activation [23][45][110] . For large, complex, or frequently updated apps, re- evaluating all screens is
impractical, lacking scalability and long -term maintainability.
Moreover, manual tools usually cannot provide global reports; most only generate feedback for the
current activity screen, preventing developers from understanding app -wide issue distributions and
patterns. This “screen -level feedback”  lacks contextual memory  [51] . Huq et al. [17] also note that such
tools may generate redundant error logs, increasing cognitive load for issue triage.
Therefore, although manual testing still plays an important role in specific scenarios —particularly for in -
depth diagnosis of critical flows or as a complementary validation step to other methods —its limitations
have also motivated researchers to explore new directions. On the one hand, some studies extend manual
dynamic testing with runtime instrumentation and crowdsourcing ideas, shifting accessibility checks from
test-device environments to real  end-user devices. For example, Barboza et al. [111] proposed the VisioAux
framework, which integrates an accessibility -testing SDK into mobile apps so that, during end -users’
everyday interactions, ATF rules are automatically invoked on their devices and the resulting findings from multiple devices are aggregated for analysis, providing a new engineering perspective for building
continuous accessibility monitoring mechanisms.
On the other hand, in order to perform accessibility detection in a more controllable and systematic way
during development and testing, and to further improve automation and reusability, the research community has increasingly explored more efficient and scalable techniques such as framework -
integrated dynamic testing and automated GUI exploration. These techniques lay the groundwork for constructing more systematic accessibility testing workflows .
5.3 Framework -Integrated Dynamic Testing
Framework -integrated dynamic testing embeds accessibility checks into existing GUI automation test
pipelines, enabling rules to be triggered and executed automatically. Compared to manual testing, these frameworks can more systematically and efficiently lo calize runtime accessibility problems, and are
particularly suitable for CI environments with rapid iteration demands  [48].
Typically, developers write functional/system test scripts; when the mobile app executes predefined
interaction tasks, the framework automatically evaluates accessibility attributes of involved components.
Once violations are detected (e.g., non- compliance with WCAG), testing is interrupted and exceptions or
reports are generated, pro mpting early repair s. The basic principle is shown in Fig. 7.



--- Page 25 ---


ACM Trans. Softw. Eng. Methodol.
Fig. 7 Principle of framework -integrated dynamic detection
In the Android ecosystem, two representative frameworks are:
 Espresso [112]: Google ’s Android GUI automation framework that supports complex interaction simulation.
Developers can embed accessibility rules in test classes; non -compliant elements are reported automatically.
Espresso integrates ATF under the hood, using the same rules as AS , and checks common issues such as missing
labels, improper target sizing, and insufficient contrast.
 Robolectric [113]: an Android unit- testing framework running on the JVM. It is more efficient than Espresso but
operates in a simulated activity environment rather than real devices, limiting its ability to detect issues that
only appear in real contexts. Its accessibility checks also rely on ATF and are similar to Espresso ’s [53].
On iOS, integrated solutions include:
 EarlGrey [114]: Google ’s open -source “iOS Espresso, ” running inside the mobile app process with strong
synchronization and extensibility. It can locate elements inside scroll containers, improving adaptability to
dynamic UI, and integrates seamlessly with Apple ’s XCTests. After tests, it performs WCAG -based rule analysis
over the UI hierarchy and outputs detailed reports [115].
 KIF (Keep It Functional) [116]: another widely used iOS framework that simulates touch, swipe, and text input.
It integrates into Xcode and calls native interfaces for accessibility checks, reducing middleware dependencies
and improving performance. Its ph ilosophy resembles Robolectric, emphasizing realistic automated inputs and
lightweight execution.
These frameworks significantly improve the systematization and scalability of dynamic detection,
realizing “testing -as-detection. ” Yet they face substantial challenges [23] [50] . First, integration costs are
high: developers must script tests for UI components and insert relevant APIs, increasing workload.
Second, effectiveness depends heavily on the quality and coverage of test suites [117]. If tests cover only
typical paths, ma ny issues are missed. Empirical studies report that only about 14% of mobile app s include
test cases, and only roughly 9% exceed 40% coverage [118][119][120] . Under such conditions, requiring
dedicated accessibility cases for all components is hard to scale, and some frameworks (e.g., Robolectric) may find no accessibility issues in practice [121][122] .
Thus, while framework -integrated testing is conceptually and practically advantageous —especially
when paired with CI —it still suffers from heavy test burdens and low automated coverage, and benefits
from being combined with higher -automation methods such as automated GUI exploration.

ACM Trans. Softw. Eng. Methodol.  5.4 Automated GUI Exploration and Behavior Analysis
Automated GUI exploration represents a major paradigm shift in mobile accessibility detection. Its goal is
to systematically activate mobile app screens via automated traversal, and then capture runtime
accessibility problems by analyzing UI states and response behaviors [23]. Compared with static rule checks or test -case -driven dynamic testing, this approach removes the prerequisite of scripted te sts and
avoids the time and scalability bottlenecks of manual testing. Fig. 8 illustrates its basic framework.


Fig. 8 Framework of automated GUI detection
To enhance flexibility and reuse, PUMA by Hao et al. [123] decouples exploration logic from analysis
logic. Using event generators such as Google Monkey [124][125] , PUMA simulates clicks, swipes, and text
input to explore execution paths, after which the analysis module checks properties of specific UI states (e.g., missing descriptions, contrast violations, non -compliant touch targets). This modularity improves
scalability and cross -app reuse.
To address missing test suites, Eler et al. [56] proposed MATE, emphasizing zero -script automated test
generation. Running on emulators or real devices, MATE uses UI Automator to identify interactive elements and randomly triggers interactions while perfor ming multiple accessibility checks, detecting issues such
as duplicate elements, unlabeled components, and abnormal contrast. It also handles dynamic components and produces detailed reports.
However, MATE ’s exploration is largely heuristic and random, making it hard to reach flows requiring
login, complex inputs, or special permissions [23] [69] . To overcome this, Chen et al. proposed Xbot [23],
which extracts precise Intent parameters needed to launch activities via instrumentation and static
analysis, then performs dynamic accessibility checks on activity pages, achieving up to 80% activity
coverage. Xbot ’s multi -stage pipeline includes APK instrumentation and repackaging, Intent extraction,
and ATF -based detection, substantially improving precision.
Although MATE and Xbot achieve strong coverage for common issues, they often fail to simulate realistic
AT user trajectories, and thus miss experiential problems such as missing focus or assistive- service trigger
failures [50] [110] . Researchers therefore began to simulate AT behaviors. To better approximate real
disabled -user contexts, Salehnamadi et al. [50] proposed Latte, which reuses existing GUI functional test
scripts and replays them with TalkBack, SwitchAccess, and other ass istive services. Latte fo llows three
stages: extracting natural -language test specifications, executing interactions via AT simulation, and
producing reports —reducing developer burden while improving accuracy.



--- Page 27 ---


ACM Trans. Softw. Eng. Methodol.  Latte’s coverage, however, depends on the existence of functional tests, which only about 14% of
mobile apps  have [118][126] . To mitigate this, Alotaibi et al. [127] proposed ATARI, constructing a
TalkBack Swipe Navigation Graph (TSNG) and a Touch Navigation Graph (TNG), then detecting nodes
reachable by touch but inaccessible via TalkBack, thereby identifying TalkBack- specific interaction failures
(TIAFs).
To balance AT realism with higher automation, Salehnamadi et al. [110] introduced A11yPuppetry,
using a “record –translate –replay” pipeline to convert real user interactions into TalkBack- recognizable
operations, supporting multiple navigation modes (linear, jump, search, touch) and producing visualized
reports. Yet it remains sensitive to UI version changes and incurs high maintenan ce cost [117] [120] .
Groundhog [128] removes reliance on tests by crawling apps in a virtual environment, simulating touch
and TalkBack interactions to assess operability and accessibility, and producing video summaries that vividly demonstrate real barriers. While stronger th an Latte and ATARI, Groundhog has limited support
for advanced gestures and dynamic content, and struggles to reproduce some complex UI states  [77] [129] .
In the ad -screen accessibility domain, He et al. [78] extended Groundhog into AdMole, drawing on ad -
request mapping techniques [130][131] . AdMole targets five issue types in ads: linear navigation failures,
touch failures, unlabeled elements, invalid clicks, and excessive interaction, filling an empirical gap in ad
accessibility.
For detecting accessibility issues under text -scaling services, AccessiText [132] runs two rounds of GUI
tests (default vs. enlarged fonts) and combines screenshots with metadata to identify missing/overlapping/clipped/truncated layouts after scaling, achi eving over 88% precision and recall. Su
et al.’s dVermin [133]  models pre -/post -scaling UI differences using connected- component analysis and
SSIM to localize issues more precisely, though at higher time cost.
Notably, most work focuses on insufficient accessibility, whereas Mehralian et al. [89] proposed
OverSight to study over -accessibility risks —where AT users can access hidden functions or sensitive
information that are unavailable to sighted users. OverSight combines static analysis and dynamic
validation to detect Android OAsmells

OverSight validates such smells via TalkBack and SuperAT, revealing structural tension between
accessibility and security: systems must broaden support for diverse abilities while preventing misuse or
abuse of accessibility mechanisms. This suggests that future accessibility design must dynamically balance
“improving accessibility ” and “controlling access boundaries, ” ensuring system robustness and
security while pursuing fairness.
Despite continuous gains in coverage and precision, two bottlenecks remain common. First, most
detection algorithms depend on predefined rules and cannot handle context -dependent issues such as
reading order or semantic tailoring. Second, they lack suffici ent human -like understanding to judge true
usability under AT. Consequently, deep -learning and LLM -based detection is emerging, aiming to inject
semantic modeling and interaction learning into automated accessibility testing and bridge the gap
between auto mation and human cognition.

5.5.1 Breakthroughs in Deep- Learning- Based UI Display Defect Detection
To address limitations of conventional GUI testing in capturing UI display defects —and the cost and
incompleteness of manual labeling —Liu et al. [134] proposed OwlEyes. OwlEyes uses CNNs to classify
defective screenshots and applies Grad -CAM heatmaps to localize problematic regions, detecting occlusion,
text overlap, and missing images. To mitigate data scarcity, OwlEyes synthesizes defective images by
parsing JSON metadata of clean screenshots and perturbing positions/sizes of compon ents such as
TextView and ImageView. However, localization regions are often coarse and thus provide limited
precision for repair.
To further improve precision, Liu et al. [135] proposed Nighthawk, which builds an object -detection
model using Faster -RCNN and ResNet50 to precisely detect and localize UI display defects. It automatically
generates labeled synthetic data heuristically, alleviating training -data shortages. Nighthawk outperforms
OwlEyes in accuracy and cross -platform adaptability, standing as a representative system for UI display
defect detection.
Ali et al. [136] also proposed M -UI-R, leveraging Faster -RCNN to detect issues such as text overlap and
component occlusion, achieving an average precision of 87.7% —about 16% higher than OwlEyes. While
effective for mainstream UI display defects, it still struggles with tiny defect regions and does not yet cover issues like button sizing or layout rationality.
5.5.2 AI-Driven Detection for Specific Accessibility Issues
Accessibility detection is also becoming more specialized. In game GUI testing, Liang et al. [137] proposed
AG3, combining intelligent exploration with image analysis to detect multilingual text overlap and
overflow. AG3 integrates VGG16 feature extraction , YOLOX detection, ViT patch classification, and
multilingual OCR, and uncovered over 2,000 real defects in deployment.
Motor -impairment users have long been understudied. Krishnavajjala et al. [25] proposed MotorEase,
combining dynamic analysis, object detection, and semantic modeling to detect designs unfriendly to motor -impairment users (e.g., hard -to-identify close butt ons, insufficient touch areas). MotorEase uses
GloVe embeddings and Faster -RCNN along with UI metadata and screenshots generated by existing tools,
requiring no test scripts. It achieves about 90% average accuracy and under 9% false positives,
outperformin g AS [36] and Groundhog [128], filling a gap in motor -accessibility tooling.
For low -vision users, Zhang et al. [138] proposed ALVIN, constructing GUI graphs and applying GCN -
based node classification to identify inaccessible components. By avoiding redundancy from GUI
hierarchies, ALVIN improves adaptation for low -vision contexts.  Experiments on 48 mobile apps  show
83.5% precision and 78.9% recall, and ALVIN can model inter -component relations to avoid invisible
redundancy and mishandling of small deviations caused by rule limitations.
5.5.3 Applications of LLMs in Accessibility Detection
The introduction of LLMs adds semantic reasoning and human -experience simulation to accessibility
testing. Maryam et al. [139] proposed AXNav, a multi -agent testing system powered by GPT -4. AXNav
receives natural -language instructions, generates test plans , and executes tests by simulating AT user
operations, supporting checks for VoiceOver, large text, bold text, etc., and outputting annotated testing videos. AXNav substantially increases automation and is regarded as useful by testers. However, it is not
designed to comprehensively scan all issues; rather, it marks a subset of issues during replay to assist
manual testing. Its navigation replay may not fully match real user behavior, and it can fail in some cases
(e.g., deciding when to stop or handling of f-screen elements requiring scrolling).
Rule -based automated detectors have limited coverage on screen -reader -specific errors [59][140], and
their feedback is often insufficiently detailed or explainable, reducing developers ’ motivation and ability
to improve mobile apps . Zhong et al. [52] developed ScreenAudit, focusing on screen -reader interaction.



--- Page 30 ---


ACM Trans. Softw. Eng. Methodol.  By recording TalkBack outputs and using LLMs to analyze their semantics, ScreenAudit automatically
identifies semantic errors and accessibility issues, offering richer context explanations and repair suggestions. Its coverage is substantially higher than traditional tools: average coverage reaches 69.2%,
far above AS [36] and Accessibility Inspector [35] at 31.3%. ScreenAudit can discover issues initially missed by experts and provide concrete repair  suggestions. Its limitation lies in reliance on TalkBack -
focusable elements, which may cause it to miss operable but non -focusable elements.
Overall, with the introduction of CV models and deep learning, perceivability -oriented issues such as
insufficient spacing, abnormal button sizing, and low text– background contrast can now be effectively
detected. The detection paradigm is evolving from the perceivable layer toward a perceivable- semantic co -
evolution, shifting from “detecting whether issues exist” to “understanding issue essence and
predicting UX impact, ” and reshaping the intelligent landscape of accessibility detection. In terms of
covera ge, existing research can collectively detect 26 of 33 typical accessibility problems (78.8%), yet each
single study covers relatively few (usually 1– 9), resulting in a fragmented tool distribution. Fragmentation
increases developers ’ burden to combine tools and may produce redundancy, duplicate reporting, or
omissions, limiting practical value. More importantly, although detection progress is substantial, mainstream work still focuses on identification, with insufficient attention to effective feedback,
optimization, and repair driven by detection results; systematic post- processing and integrated toolchain
support remain lacking. Therefore, the next section turns to mobile accessibility repair techniques,
summarizing key strategies and exploring their linkage with detection mechanisms.
6 ANSWER TO RQ3 : HOW CAN THE LANDSCAPE OF MOBILE APP LICATION  ACCESSIBILITY REPAIR
TECHNIQUES BE CATEGORIZED, AND WHAT ARE THEIR REPAIR PIPELINES, GENERALIZABILITY, AND
FUNCTIONAL PERFORMANCE?
Based on a systematic analysis of 17 representative repair studies, this section summarizes the landscape
and deployment pathways of mobile application accessibility  repair. We construct a three- category
framework —rule -driven and optimization -based repair, ML -based repair, and LLM -assisted repair —and
compare them along repair pipelines, generality, and strengths/limitations. We then conclude with an
overall assessment of the state of mobile accessibility repair research.
6.1 Rule -Driven and Optimization -Based Repair
This line of work treats accessibility repair as a problem of formal modeling and search/optimization. It
relies on manually designed rules, heuristic algorithms, or code mutation strategies to automatically
generate repair patches, and is commonly used in  early research prototypes and lightweight integration
scenarios. The core idea is to satisfy accessibility guidelines while preserving the original layout, visual aesthetics, and functional integrity as much as possible, yielding high interpretability and  strong
engineering fit.
Typically, these methods formulate repair as a multi- objective optimization problem. By automatically
adjusting UI attributes (e.g., size, color, position), they seek to meet accessibility constraints while retaining
the original layout structure and visua l style. This modeling naturally handles multiple —often conflicting —
objectives, such as “increasing contrast ” vs. “preserving the original palette” or “enlarging widgets
” vs. “avoiding layout disruption. ” To solve such problems, meta -heuristic search is wi dely adopted,
with GA being the most common choice. GA iteratively produces high -quality candidate repairs through
selection, crossover, and mutation. Combined with MOO, these approaches output a Pareto frontier
representing different trade- offs among obje ctives. Several systems (e.g., SALEM, ScaleFix) follow this
general recipe; the overall pipeline is shown in Fig. 11.

1) SALEM: Size -Based Multi -Objective Optimization Repair
SALEM (Size -based inAccessibility repair in Mobile apps), proposed by Alotaibi et al. [141], is the first
automatic repair system focusing on size- related accessibility issues in GUIs. Based on genetic algorithms
and multi -objective optimization strategies , this method enhances accessibility while maximizing interface
consistency. SALEM's repair process consists of three stages:
 Element Identification: Grouping UI elements by their logical positions (XPath), sizes, and category types using
the DBSCAN clustering algorithm to ensure visual consistency among components during repair.
 Structure Modeling: Introducing a "Size Relation Graph (SRG)" to characterize size dependencies between components, identifying the minimum necessary adjustment elements and attribute subsets to avoid
unnecessary changes to the entire UI.
 Solution Search: Generating repair patches via a multi- objective genetic algorithm, with a fitness function that
penalizes layout distortion, alignment disruption, or spacing compression caused by repairs. The system can
automatically compile the repaired APK file, successfully repairing approximately 99% of size issues.
Despite its effectiveness, SALEM suffers from excessively long compilation times (accounting for ~98%
of total runtime) and only focuses on size issues, potentially introducing new problems (e.g., abnormal
component spacing) in certain scenarios [22].
2) AGAA: Genetic Algorithm Repair for Undersized Text and Information Redundancy
Xu et al. [142] proposed the AGAA method, expanding the application scope of genetic algorithms to
address visual impairment users' difficulties in perceiving small fonts and redundant information. AGAA introduces a GUI repair framework based on graph modeling, comprising three key technologies:
 Component Relation Graph (CRG) Construction: Integrating XML structure and visual rendering information to
model the GUI as a graph structure, including structural edges (parent- child relationships) and dependency
edges (style and logical associations).
 Issue Capture: Identifying undersized text by comparing component sizes against Google's accessibility
guidelines, while quantifying component clickability and layout occupancy to uncover information redundancy.
 Solution Generation and Consistency Maintenance: Optimizing repair strategies using genetic algorithms and
maintaining consistency among similar components during size adjustment via DBSCAN clustering to maximize
design aesthetics.
AGAA can directly modify source code and automatically deploy repair patches, demonstrating good performance
in practical applications. However, it relies on component identification information, making it difficult to handle
WebView components or non -stan dard designs, thus limiting its applicability.
3) ScaleFix: Multi -Objective Optimization Repair for UI Scaling Accessibility Failures
To address UI failure issues caused by assistive functions (e.g., scaling services), Alotaibi et al. [143] proposed the
ScaleFix system, focusing on repairing UI Scaling Accessibility Failures (USAFs). This method constructs a UI Relation
Graph (UIG) to mo del size, weight, and spatial constraint relationships between UI elements, thereby narrowing the
repair search space. ScaleFix's fitness function integrates five objectives: minimizing text truncation, element missing, and element collision issues, maximizing the preservation of original positional relationships, and reducing repair
interference. It achieves a repair success rate of 90% and significantly improves UI accessibility by 23%. The repaired

and code- defined UI elements.
4) Iris: Context -Aware Color Contrast Repair
Color contrast issues are common barriers in mobile accessibility [144]. Zhang et al. [24] proposed Iris, an
automated repair method that uses context- aware strategies to select alternative color schemes for insufficient text
and image contrast. Iris's key  strategies include:
 Reference Database Construction: Building a reference database with numerous color- compliant UI
components, categorized by component types.
 Alternative Color Selection: Prioritizing color matching from similar components or the same application,

 Repair Implementation: Precisely locating problematic components via static data flow analysis and ensuring

Iris successfully repairs 91.38% of contrast issues without compromising design consistency, effectively improving
user satisfaction and receiving positive developer feedback. However, the current version has limited support for
components without IDs, com plex components like WebView, and compatibility with third -party libraries and new UI
frameworks (e.g., Jetpack).
5) RGNF: Navigation Flow Reconstruction for Visually Impaired Users
Regarding GUI navigation structure accessibility, Zhang et al. proposed the RGNF system [145], which enhances the
usage consistency for visually impaired users by reconstructing the GUI Navigation Flow (GNF). Based on user cognitive models, the system groups UI components using Gestalt psychology's proximity and similarity principles and
redraws navigation orders via the DFS strategy to ensure components are read aloud consecutively in the expected
order. Experimental results show that this method achieves a similarity of 0.921 with users' ideal navigation flows,
and a ccessibility metrics improve to 90.31%. Limitations include incapability of handling GUIs with non- focusable
components, unsuitability for pure text applications, and compatibility issues with some in- app reading tools.

attributes or label data. This enables them to complete repair tasks such as generating missing labels, identifying UI elements, and semantic complementation.  Excelling in processing unstructured data, these
methods feature strong semantic reasoning and cross -interface adaptation capabilities, having

To tackle the challenges of data imbalance and label generation quality, Ma et al. [151] designed the

mechanism. It significantly outperforms previous systems in  multiple metrics such as BLEU and accuracy.

In terms of practical repair capabilities, Zhang et al. [22] proposed the AccessFixer system, innovatively

Additionally, the Screen Recognition method [77] demonstrates how to automatically generate
accessibility metadata by combining visual models and heuristic rules, greatly expanding the scope of
assistive function support. Through multi -stage detection, tex t recognition, and semantic reasoning, this
method has achieved significant improvements in user studies, highlighting the enormous potential of
deep learning in interface semantic understanding [80] [121][152] .
Overall, learning -based repair methods exhibit powerful capabilities in handling complex and diverse
UI scenarios, particularly suitable for tasks such as label generation, semantic completion, and attribute
prediction. However, they rely on large- scale high -quality datasets and still face challenges such as result
interpretability and cross -application consistency. Future research needs to further enhance their
generalization and controllability, combined with rule guidance mechanisms to strengthen system stability and deployment practicality.

7 CURRENT CHALLENGES AND FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS
The rapid development of LLM - and AI -enabled techniques is pushing SE tasks toward more intelligent and
efficient directions [160]. However, constrained by the complexity of mobile app ecosystems and the boundaries of existing techniques, achieving compreh ensive and universal mobile application accessibility
still faces systemic challenges. This section summarizes current limitations around two core stages —issue
detection and issue repair —and then discusses feasible extensions and future opportunities from both the
SE lifecycle perspective and the AI -enablement perspective.
7.1 Challenges in Mobile Accessibility Detection
Mobile application accessibility  detection methods play a critical role in supporting the accessibility -by-
design lifecycle and improving the efficiency of systematic assessments [45]. Existing studies have evolved
from early manual evaluations to static/dynamic testing, automated GUI ex ploration, AT -interaction
simulation, and deep learning/LLM -driven semantic detection, achieving progress in speed, repeatability,
and coverage. Nevertheless, the overall landscape still suffers from the following limitation s:
(1) Fragmented detection capabilities, with insufficient coverage by individual tools. Although existing
methods collectively can identify 28 out of 33 common accessibility issue types (78.8% coverage), a single
study typically covers only 1 –9 types. Such fragmentation forces developers to combine multiple tools,
which often leads to inconsistent results, redundant reports, and coexisting misses, substantially
weakening the practical value of detection in real -world engineering. It also implie s a risk that the reported
overall coverage may be “inflated ” in practice.
(2) Detection precision remains inadequate, with widespread false negatives and false positives
[18] [89][132][138][161] , especially in complex interactions or context -dependent scenarios where
methods struggle to fully interpret context or assess content quality. For instance, static tools (e.g., Lint [104]) cannot detect dynamic properties that only manifest at runtime, e asily creating a false sense of
assurance; manual tools (e.g., AS [36]) can capture runtime issues, but are labor -intensive, non- scalable ,
lack cross -screen memory, and often generate reports with many duplicates, reducing efficiency;
automated GUI exploration tools (e.g., MATE [56] ) are limited by preset rules and often ignore how users
with disabilities actually interact with mobile apps  via AT; AT behavior simulation methods (e.g., Latte



--- Page 36 ---


ACM Trans. Softw. Eng. Methodol.  [50]) better approximate real user experience, but their effectiveness depends on existing test cases or
linear navigation, remains brittle, supports limited complex gestures, and still produces false positives/negatives; deep learning approaches (e.g., Ow lEyes [126] ) sometimes show low precision in
localizing problematic regions, and the realism of synthesized training data is debatable; even advanced
LLM -based methods (e.g., ScreenAudit [52]) may rely on TalkBack- focusable elements, have limited
contextua l understanding, and thus miss elements that are operable but not focusable. Therefore,
improving precision in complex and dynamic scenarios remains the primary bottleneck.
(3) Lack of post -processing mechanisms, making it hard to advance repair. Most studies focus on finding
issues but provide little in terms of executable repair suggestions, prioritization, or automated repair
interfaces. This creates a gap between detectio n and repair. When detection outputs cannot be directly
translated into actionable engineering tasks, their improvement impact is significantly diluted.
(4) Non -unified detection reports hinder downstream repair. Different tools generate reports with
inconsistent formats, granularity, and terminology, and often lack cross -screen aggregation and global
issue -pattern mining capabilities [51] . For example, manual tools usually report results screen by screen
without “memory across scans. ” Huq et al. [17] show that such manual reports may contain many
duplicates. As a result, developers struggle to track, govern, and regress accessibility defects systematically
across the lifecycle, which in turn harms repair efficiency and quality [162][163] .
To address these limitations, future work should focus on the following directions to build more
universal, efficient, and scalable accessibility solutions:
(1) Establish unified mobile accessibility standards and report specifications, providing consistent
detection –repair foundations [51][164]. Future research needs more comprehensive and fine- grained
standards tailored to mobile characteristics, along with uniform reporting norms that supply detailed, explainable issue descriptions and repair guidance to facilitate developer adoption. This can remove the
burden caused by fragmented standards and improve efficiency.
(2) Improve coverage and precision.  1) More intelligent GUI exploration: To overcome limited coverage
on screens requiring specific inputs or involving sensitive data, future exploration strategies can incorporate RL or symbolic execution to reach deeper mobile app states more effectively [53][165][166],
ensuring tools traverse all functional paths . 2) Comprehensive AT behavior simulation: Current
simulations poorly support complex gestures and diverse assistive services. Future methods should simulate richer a ssistive services and complex interactions (e.g., drag, pinch, multi -finger gestures) to
reflect real disability -use scenarios more faithfully.  3) Context understanding and semantic analysis:
Although deep learning and LLMs are already used, they still cannot fully interpret context or assess content quality. Future models must enhance semantic understanding to detect subtle errors such as
reading order or contextual semantics of elements, reducing reliance on human judgment [167][168].  4)
Handling dynamic content and complex UIs: Dynamic loading, navigation loops, or highly customized UI
components cause misses and spurious alarms. More robust detection is needed to accurately capture and
analyze such scenarios [70].
(3) Build end -to-end, full -lifecycle detection –repair pipelines integrated into CI/CD for continuous
assurance.  1) Deep fusion of detection and repair: Given current fragmentation [24][28], future solutions
should translate detection outputs into actionable repair suggestions, or even automate repair, reducing
developer workload and improving efficiency.  2) CI/CD integration: Embedding accessibility detection and
repair into CI/CD enables automated, continuous accessibility assurance, ensuring issues are di scovered
and addressed at every commit or release [169][170].
(4) Introduce user capability -difference modeling for targeted detection and personalized adaptation.
Existing standards/tools are largely static and cannot dynamically adapt UIs to individual users. Future
work should develop capability models that suppor t detection and repair based on disability type,
preferences, and usage habits, enabling true “ accessibility -by-default”  with on -demand adaptation
[171].

Mobile accessibility research is shifting from “detection -centric ” to “proactive repair. ” Existing repair
methods include optimization -based, deep learning– based, and LLM -enabled automation, showing early
success for issues such as label completion and size/contrast adjustment. Yet, several challenges remain:
(1) Insufficient issue coverage is the central challenge. Of 33 issue categories, current repair studies
address only 15 (45.45% coverage), and a single study typically covers only 18.2%. This reveals both the
current focus and the large number of underexp lored issue types. Repair coverage is far below detection
coverage (45.45% vs. 78.8%), highlighting a structural imbalance: many detected issues lack automatic or
semi -automatic repair, leaving developers to manually fix most problems —costly, slow, and err or-prone.
Thus, research must shift toward robust and comprehensive repair mechanisms, especially for still -
unaddressed issue types.
(2) Most approaches target a single issue or specific component attributes, with limited applicability to
cross -screen flows, complex gestures, dynamic UIs, closed- source apps, and cross -file modifications. This
makes them insufficient for the composite an d systemic nature of mobile accessibility. A unified,
comprehensive repair framework is urgently needed.
(3) Repair precision and generalization remain limited. Some methods still mis -handle
complex/dynamic UIs, producing misses or spurious repairs . They also over -rely on datasets; when icons
are rare, error rates rise substantially [75].
(4) Insufficient contextual and semantic understanding is a key bottleneck. Many methods cannot fully
interpret context or evaluate content quality [77][146], and often miss subtle defects requiring semantic
judgment, such as AT reading order or the contex tual meaning of elements.
(5) Lack of unified evaluation criteria and report formats prevents effective comparison across studies
and forces developers to reconcile inconsistent tools. Overall, repair research remains weak in post -
processing and in advancing accessibility improveme nt based on detection outputs [24].
Future repair research can move forward along these directions:
(1) Expand the coverage of repair targets. With only 15/33 categories repaired so far, future work
should prioritize issues affecting cognitive -impaired and multi -disability users, and develop methods for
complex interactions (e.g., drag, pinch) and dynami c UI changes (e.g., looping lists, disappearing menus).
As wearables, VR/AR, and AI -driven interaction proliferate, accessibility repair for these emerging
modalities also becomes crucial [172].
(2) Increase repair intelligence and automation.  1) Multimodal fusion: Explore deep learning and LLMs
for jointly leveraging image, text, structure, and behavior data, enabling deeper UI semantics and intent
understanding, hence more precise repair suggestions.  2) Context -aware repair: Use UI context (e.g., user
habits, scenarios, device configurations) to support personalized, adaptive optimization.  3) End -to-end
automated repair: Develop pipelines from detection to code -level repair with minimal human int ervention,
capable of handling interdependent issues and cross -file changes.  4) RL for repair: Introduce RL to learn
and optimize repair strategies through interaction with mobile app environments, adapting to complex UIs
and user needs.
(3) Strengthen evaluation and validation. Build comprehensive and objective metrics that consider not
only accuracy but also user experience, UI aesthetics, performance, and security impacts. Incorporate real -
user A/B testing and longitudinal studies to validate real -world effectiveness and durability. Simulation
environments that preview repaired UIs can further improve developer efficiency and repair quality.
(4) Promote interdisciplinary collaboration and standards unification. Encourage collaboration among
human factors, SE, cognitive science, and HCI to integrate multi -disciplinary theories into repair
methodologies. Meanwhile, establish finer -grained and mo re universal mobile accessibility standards,
especially considering mobile -specific interactions and emerging technologies. Cross -platform repair
solutions can reduce repeated adaptation costs.
(5) Improve toolchain integration and usability. Develop repair tools with higher integration and more
developer -friendly interfaces, embedding them into IDEs and CI/CD. Provide detailed and explainable

benchmarks will also foster community collaboration and sharing.
7.3 SE perspective: addressing accessibility challenges across lifecycle stages
To situate our findings within SE, we clarify how accessibility issues relate to lifecycle activities and how SE processes can be extended to better support accessibility. Fragmentation and poor deployability of detection/repair are not only technical prob lems but also reflect a structural gap where accessibility is not
stably embedded in the SE lifecycle. Thus, we discuss how to revise SE processes accordingly.
(1) Requirements engineering. Most mobile apps still treat accessibility as optional, causing late- stage
patching and fragmented detection results. SE practice can be extended with accessibility -specific
requirement templates, user capability role models, and acceptance criteria (e.g., AT navigation
constraints, perceptual load thresholds). Elevating accessibility to a first- class requirement helps prevent
downstream report inconsistency and coverage gaps.
(2) Design and modeling. Traditional UI design rarely semantically models user tasks or AT behaviors,
leading to semantic limitations in detection. SE design tools should integrate accessibility -aware static
checks, focus -chain visualization, and cross -device previews. MDE can represent accessibility rules in a
platform -independent manner, alleviating cross -platform inconsistency.
(3) Implementation and toolchain integration. Detection fragmentation partly stems from the absence
of unified IDE/build -time support. SE practice should introduce accessibility -aware static analyzers,
enforceable code annotations, and compile- time constraint checkers. Integrating these into DevOps
pipelines shifts accessibility checks from ad hoc usage to reliable, reproducible quality gates.
(4) Testing and validation. Existing methods struggle with runtime behaviors, gesture interaction, and
dynamic AT use. SE testing frameworks need dedicated AT simulators, multimodal gesture engines, and semantic test oracles beyond structural metrics. Cros s-screen memory and multi -batch consistency checks
can reduce false positives and improve engineering usability.
(5) Maintenance and evolution. Mobile UIs evolve rapidly, causing rule and ML -model drift. SE
configuration management should add accessibility -aware version control, automated accessibility
regression testing, and logs about repair impacts across versions . LLM -based repair agents must meet
reproducibility requirements —pattern -constrained outputs, verification logs, and traceable patch artifacts —
aligning with broader trustworthy AI -for-SE research.
Overall, embedding accessibility throughout the SE lifecycle is the key to transforming today ’s tool -
centric, fragmented approaches into stable, industrializable practices.
7.4 AI enablement: making accessibility a broader reality
AI can not only improve detection/repair accuracy but also transform accessibility from “after -the-fact
remediation ” into “proactive, continuous, user- centered ” engineering, thus enabling real -scale
adoption.
First, LLMs and multimodal models can semantically understand UI layout, user intent, perceptual load,
and AT behaviors, uncovering issues beyond rule violations, such as cognitive overload or gesture conflicts.
Second, AI enables scalable personalization: by modeling user capability profiles (e.g., vision level,
motor patterns, cognitive load), systems can adapt UIs in real time, shifting from a “universal design ”
paradigm to an “accessibility -by-default, adaptive- on-demand ” paradigm.
Third, AI -assisted development tools —accessibility -aware copilots, code -generation agents, and design -
evaluation models —can embed accessibility early in requirements, prototypes, and UI design, reducing
downstream detection/repair burdens.
Fourth, integrating AI into CI/CD enables continuous accessibility regression testing across devices,
themes, scaling factors, and user groups, realizing “continuous accessibility. ”



--- Page 39 ---


ACM Trans. Softw. Eng. Methodol.  However, dataset bias, limited AT simulation, missing semantic modeling, and the lack of verification
standards for AI -generated patches still restrict effectiveness. Breaking these limits requires extending SE
methods, reshaping processes, and incorporati ng accessibility into SE education so developers treat it as a
first -class requirement rather than a downstream patch.
A key takeaway is that accessibility must increasingly “ shift left ”—embedded in requirements,
prototyping, and design rather than relying on post -hoc detection/repair. AI is decisive in this shift -left
process. LLMs and multimodal models can evaluate wireframes and early prototypes, generate
accessibility -aware design alternatives, predict AT interaction conflicts, and automatically produce
accessibility user stories, capability roles, and acceptance criteria. These early interventions greatly reduce
the flow of violations into later lifecycle stages.
Moreover, AI assistants integrated into IDEs can provide real -time, context- sensitive accessibility
guidance during coding, preventing many issues that would otherwise require downstream tools. In this
sense, AI reduces —rather than increases —dependency on post -hoc tooling. Achieving this shift -left requires
SE practice to adapt: requirements must include AI -generated capability models; design tools must open
accessibility -aware feedback loops; CI/CD must support early accessibility gates; and SE education m ust
train developers to realize “accessibility -by-design. ”
Taken together, these mechanisms explain how AI can make accessibility a broader reality while
reducing the cost of late- stage detection and repair. The core insight of this survey is that accessibility must
be treated as a requirement as fundamental as pr oject functionality, and embedded from the earliest
stages. Doing so substantially reduces reliance on costly, fragile, and limited post- hoc tools, enabling a
development process centered on prevention rather than discovery.
8 LIMITATIONS AND THREATS TO VALIDITY
8.1 Literature Acquisition and Screening
Although we designed a multi -stage search process with venue tiering, terminology expansion, multi -
database batch searches, and forward/backward snowballing, and systematically queried nine databases
(ACM DL, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, Wiley, Web of Science, Scopus, CNKI, and arXiv), the
completeness of our literature coverage still cannot be fully guaranteed. (1) The search strings were
manually constructed and iteratively refined based on a domain -specific terminology database, so we may
still have missed studies that use non -standard terminology (e.g., domain -specific descriptions of
disabilities or idiosyncratic ways of describing  accessibility) or that were published in venues outside our
target list. Therefore, this review should be regarded as a representative view of high -quality and traceable
research, rather than an exhaustive enumeration of all existing work. (2) Some HCI -oriented studies that
only marginally involve mobile accessibility may have been excluded during quality assessment. At the
same time, the application of inclusion/exclusion criteria inevitably involves subjective judgment. To mitigate this, we adopted a dual -reviewer independent screening process and resolved disagreements
through discussion, in order to reduce the influence of individual subjectivity as much as possible. (3) Our
search window ends on July 1, 2025. In rapidly evolving areas such as mobile ac cessibility and LLM -
enabled software engineering, new tools, frameworks, and practices are constantly emerging. As a result,
our synthesis of detection and repair technique landscapes reflects a time- bounded snapshot rather than
long -term stable conclusion s. When using this review as an evidence baseline, subsequent research should
partially update and adjust the conclusions in light of newer literature and platform changes, especially in the context of improved LLM capabilities and evolving platform polici es.
8.2 Limited Coverage of Industrial Practice and Grey Literature
In addition to the SLR itself, we selected several representative tools and industrial practices as auxiliary analyses to complement the SLR evidence and strengthen the engineering orientation of our work.
However, for reasons of reproducibility and argume ntative rigor, we can only systematically cover cases

8.3 Task and Platform Adaptability
Our study focuses on native mobile applications and builds an accessibility issue taxonomy, an evolution
path of detection techniques, and a repair -technique taxonomy around three main threads: issues,
detection, and repair. These frameworks are grounded i n the POUR principles of WCAG 2.x, user- ability
models, and the software engineering lifecycle, and emphasize alignment with platform Assistive Technology (AT) behavior and engineering practice. Inevitably, however, they also reflect our modeling
perspecti ve. For example, we place greater emphasis on issues related to visual and motor abilities, while
the coverage of cognitive and auditory impairments and multi -disability scenarios is comparatively
limited. This is partly due to the relative scarcity of rel ated studies in the existing literature, and partly
because accessibility specifications and tools for visual/motor impairments are more mature on native mobile platforms. We also do not provide a systematic survey of Web applications, wearables, XR, or mu lti-
device collaborative interaction. In practice, mobile and Web applications differ fundamentally in terms of technical architecture, interaction modalities, and system integration, and the research community
typically treats them as separate targets rather than merging them.
Therefore, the taxonomies and terminology in this paper are better understood as a WCAG -compatible,
mobile -oriented software engineering baseline, rather than the only possible theoretical partition of the
field; different researchers may reasonably adopt slightly different boundaries and levels of granularity.
How to extend accessibility engineering to additional platforms, richer ability models, and multi -modal
interaction patterns remains an important direction for future work.
9 CONCLUSION
We conducted a SLR on mobile application accessibility, grounded in authoritative databases and a reproducible
search–screening–assessment workflow. We performed multi -round retrieval using credibility -tiered sources and
standard terminology clusters across ACM DL, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, Wiley, Web of Science, Scopus,
and CNKI (with arXiv as a seed source), covering studies up to 2025 -07-01. We then executed deduplication, applied
inclusion/exclusion criteria, conducted forward/backward s nowballing, and performed quality assessment. In total,
76 papers were included. Based on these studies, we systematically synthesized accessibility interaction frameworks,
relevant standards, the issue landscape, the evolution of detection paradigms, and repair techniques, and summarized
common challenges across methodological, technical- mechanism, and system -implementation layers.
The field has shifted from rule -based compliance checking toward user- perception modeling and intelligent repair.
Deep learning and recent LLMs have significantly enhanced automation, especially in generating prompts and
alternative texts, code repair, and  strategy recommendation, showing the potential of “intelligent repair agents.”
Yet these approaches remain constrained by generalization, explainability, robustness, and industrial applicability.
Drawing on the existing literature and technical practice, mobile application accessibility research over the past
decade has evolved from rule- based compliance detection toward user- perception modeling and intelligent repair,
exhibiting a broader migration “from compliance verification to personalized support.”  however, four core gaps
persist:
 Insufficient semantic modeling: Current detection methods primarily rely on static rules and UI structural
features, lacking integrated modeling of UI semantics, task flows, and user intent, resulting in frequent false
positives/negatives and limited robus tness.



--- Page 41 ---


ACM Trans. Softw. Eng. Methodol.   Weak repair mechanisms: Most studies focus on detection, while truly closed -loop “detect –localize –repair –
verify” systems remain largely absent, especially without unified frameworks for multi -disability coordination
or dynamic -interaction repair.
 Missing user capability -difference modeling: Existing methods are built on generic assumptions and do not
capture the concrete interaction patterns and real usage burdens of groups such as visually impaired users, older
adults, and users with cognitive imp airments, preventing genuine adaptive -on-demand support.
 Disconnect between standards and engineering tools: Although WCAG- like standards have become increasingly
fine-grained, tool deployment still faces insufficient adaptation, platform fragmentation, and inconsistent
enforcement, undermining universality.
Overall, although mobile accessibility techniques have achieved substantial progress, there is still a long
way to go before the vision of “accessibility by default ” can be fully realized. To further bridge the gap
between systematic coverage and engineering practicality in real -world development settings, future
research needs to continue deepening several key directions. Specifically, the major avenues for mobile
application accessibility research include: (1) semantics -enhanced detection and interaction modeling —
leveraging semantic graphs and LLMs to enable contextual understanding and cross -screen aggregation,
thereby improving report consolidation and strategy generation; (2) multimodal repair —building coupled
models across vision, language, and code to strengthen generalization and execution efficiency; (3) user -
difference awareness —achieving personalized detection and repair based on capability profiles and
interaction features, operationalizing “accessibility by default, adaptive on demand ”; and (4) standard –
tool integration —unifying reporting specifications and constructing open datasets and evaluation
benchmarks to support the transition from academic prototypes to industrial deployment.
