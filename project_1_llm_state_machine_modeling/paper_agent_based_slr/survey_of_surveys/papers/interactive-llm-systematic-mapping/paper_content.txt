GPTA B S T R A C T
Context: The research volume is continuously increasing. Manual analysis of large topic scopes and continu-
ously updating literature studies with the newest research results is effort intensive and, therefore, difficult to
achieve.
Objective: To discuss possibilities and next steps for using LLMs (e.g., GPT-4) in the mapping study process.
Method: The research can be classified as a solution proposal. The solution was iteratively designed and
discussed among the authors based on their experience with LLMs and literature reviews.
Results: We propose strategies for the mapping process, outlining the use of agents and prompting strategies
for each step.
Conclusion: Given the potential of LLMs in literature studies, we should work on a holistic solutions for
LLM-supported mapping studies.
1. Introduction
The mapping  study methodology  is frequently  used in the context  of
software  engineering.  Two guidelines  for mapping  studies  (MSs) were
proposed  and are, to date, cited more than 7000 times.1The purpose  of
mapping  studies  is to classify  research  and observe  trends.  In contrast,
systematic  literature  reviews  (SLRs)  focus on synthesizing  evidence.
Given the purpose  of mapping  studies,  a more extensive  set of studies
should  be targeted,  as otherwise,  it would  make sense to conduct  an
SLR directly.
This article  discusses  the possibilities  of using LLMs throughout  the
mapping  process,  proposing  strategies  for using LLMs in each research
step of MSs. There are various  motivations  for using LLMs in MSs: (1)
An increased  number  of published  papers  to be analyzed;  (2) Conduct-
ing mapping  studies  on a larger scope; (3) Getting  additional  research
design  ideas by interacting  with the LLM; (4) Reduced  effort allows
updating  mapping  studies  more regularly.  The solution  described  here
is a direction  for researchers  in the software  community  to work on
solutions  as a community,  as a reliable  and intensively  evaluated  solu-
tion will likely result from a community  effort. This article  may serve
as a starting  point for discussing  using LLMs in MSs, working  towards
a holistic  solution,  and evaluating  the solution  in the SE context.
We describe  the solution  and provide  references  to relevant  studies.
Our solution  is inspired  by [1], where the authors  propose  a sys-
tem where testers  interact  with a search-based  software  test system,

focusing  on paper screening  needed  to be trained  based on the users’
labels.
2. LLM-supported  mapping  process
This section  discusses  how to incorporate  LLMs in every step of the
mapping  process,  which is presented  in [4].Fig. 1presents  each step
of the review  process  and briefly  outlines  the input and actions  done
by the user and the output  of the LLM. For all the steps outlined  below
we would  like to highlight  that the researcher  should  have an initial
idea of possible  search  terms,  inclusion  criteria  or extraction  items in
order to validate  the outputs.  Specifically,  research  problems  and goals
should  be driven  by relevant  practical  needs.

2.1. Establishing  a need for the map
At this stage, we provide  the LLM with our research  objectives
and contextual  information  (e.g., paper abstract)  to elicit proposals
for research  questions  and complementary  information  (e.g., additional
objectives).  We edit the questions  as input for the next stage.
2.2. Study identification
2.2.1. Search
Proposed  strategy: Search  aims to identify  relevant  documents  con-
cerning  the research  objective.  Along with defining  research  objectives,
the search  is a complex  and iterative  process.  Creating  a search  strategy
from a search  intent involves  understanding  the searcher’s  underlying
intentions.  The strategy  must be both transparent  and reproducible.
Even though  semantic  searches2are becoming  more common,  the need
for reproducibility  supports  using Boolean  searches.  Boolean  searches
necessitate  formulating  a search  strategy  using Boolean  expressions  and
operators.  The prerequisite  is the identification  and combination  of
relevant  terms.  We advocate  for an autonomous  agent with Human-in-
the-Loop  to meet the demand  for reproducibility,  account  for human
involvement  in the process,  and leverage  the potential  of new techno-
logical  developments.  We propose  an architecture  that places human
intervention  at the center and considers  three agents:
1.Keyword  Identification  Agent: The Keyword  Identification  Agent
identifies  relevant  terms and provides  them for further  research.
Language  models  can support  the search  for semantically  similar
terms.  Semantic  similarities  can, for instance,  help find terms
commonly  used in the past but have largely  been replaced  by
other terms today.  This step also serves to solidify  the focus of

may be to search  at the conceptual  term level, the level of
subtypes  (e.g., Stereolithography),  or supertypes  (e.g., Rapid

2.Semantic  Search  Agent: The Semantic  Search  Agent uses a
Retrieval-Augmented  Generation  (RAG) architecture  to suggest
relevant  documents  based on semantic  similarities.  In this con-
text, it may be helpful  to consider  a graph database  as a storage
component,  allowing  for the incorporation  of links between
documents  (e.g., through  citations).  This step does not select
documents  but adjusts  the search  strategy.  Relevant  search  terms
are then extracted  again from the selected  documents.
3.Search  Strategy  Agent: The Search  Strategy  Agent formulates  the
search  strategy  used to conduct  the research.
The Keyword  Identification  Agent and the Semantic  Search  Agent
thus specifically  support  a citation  pearl growing  strategy .
Relevant  literature: Wang et al. [5] created  prompts  of different  com-
plexities,  the most complex  including  a search  example  and requesting
the use of PICO. Also, prompts  were refined  to request  modifications
to increase  the relevance  of elements.  In addition,  so-called  guided
prompts  were formulated  that separate  the search  construction  process
into sub-steps.  The authors  used existing  SLRs as a basis to assess the
generated  searches.  The results  show the potential  to use ChatGPT  for
search  generation.  The F-Measure  shows better results  for author-based
queries;  however,  GPT-generated  queries  result in less recall, i.e.more
relevant  papers  are missed.  Additional  interesting  findings  were: (1)
The use of PICO harms recall; (2) the use of existing  SLRs as a basis to
assess the generated  searches;  (3) requesting  refinements  reduces  recall
and improves  precision.

process. Therefore, a continual learning system can be superior to hard-
coded prompts. Users’ preferences can be derived from document inclu-
sion and exclusion. Frameworks like DSPy can also be helpful by op-

researchers need to create iteratively.
From a technical perspective, including and excluding documents is
a classification problem. Researchers classify documents as relevant or
not relevant. However, this approach lacks transparency, traceability,
and human oversight. Therefore, language models have to explain the
reasons for inclusion and exclusion. Chain-of-thoughts prompting is a
possible approach to help researchers understand the decision-making
process of the LLM. From our perspective, citations are indispensable.
They allow the verification of arguments and increase traceability.
Relevant literature: Huotala et al. [6] used GPT to simply abstracts
of papers and compare the paper selection done by GPT with human
raters. As found by the authors, a similar performance between humans
and the LLMs is achieved when using One-shot , Few-shot , or Few-shot
with Chain-of-Thought prompting. The authors also found improved
performances when using GPT-4 compared to GPT-3.5 when using
prompt optimization. Zero-shot prompting was not performing well
compared to other prompt optimization approaches. The authors also
highlight the benefit of having seed papers or prior SLRs as input for
prompting. Guo et al. [7] repeated the selection process of existing
SLRs, highlighting that GPT was good at excluding irrelevant papers
but not as good at achieving a high recall. They highlighted the value
of GPT as a support tool for reviewers to save time.
2.3. Data extraction and classification
Before discussing the data extraction and classification strategies,
we would like to highlight that, given the degree of automation used,
it is now possible to go beyond adaptive reading depth during manual
screening and consider the complete papers as input for the analysis for
deductive coding.
1.Inductive coding: In inductive coding, the classification emerges
as a consequence of the coding process. We suggest using
topic modeling with the titles and abstracts as input to identify
the topics and clusters of papers. A typical process is to create
embeddings , reduce dimensions, cluster embeddings, and create
topic representations. Such a process is, for example, supported
by Bertopic , which supports a modular framework, allowing the
exchange of the approaches used, for example, for clustering.
2.Deductive coding: Given is a data extraction scheme (e.g., SWE-
BOK categories). As was learned in the context of screening,
examples are important. Hence, we suggest using One-shot or
Few-shot prompting. As the complete PDF document is analyzed,
a RAG architecture may be helpful, as it first identifies the most
relevant parts of a document after document splitting before
prompting the OpanAI API.
Relevant literature: A solution for inductive coding was proposed
by Wang et al. [8]. They used the aforementioned Bertopic library
to review interdisciplinary research. They compute topic diversity and
cohesion of topics to learn about interdisciplinary integration of publi-
cations. Besides showing hierarchical clusters of topics, feature words ,
and c-TF-IDF scores for terms within topics, they also included a
temporal analysis. Petersen [9] used GPT-4 to judge whether studies are
indeed case studies as claimed by study authors. The result showed that
GPT-4 outperformed the authors (both primary and secondary studies).2.4. Visualization
ChatGPT already provides options for data visualization by gener-
ating source code and visualizing data. However, it is noticeable that
further specialized applications are being developed. An example of this
isLIDA from Microsoft.
For exploratory analysis Bertopic may be used, which allows for the
visualization and exploration of document landscapes.
2.5. Reporting
To support the reporting of studies, we suggest providing GPT
with tabular results and result visualizations and request to highlight
exciting patterns and observations in the data (e.g., with a specific focus
on spotting research gaps).
3. Reflections
Study validity: Former studies have shown that GPT achieved similar
or even better performance than humans. Publication bias and limited
studies currently restrict our understanding of LLMs’ reliability in litera-
ture reviews. The rapid evolution of LLMs, exemplified by Claude.ai and
the upcoming GPT-o1, may quickly render current evaluations obsolete.
This underscores the need for a conceptual framework to guide future
research and applications of LLMs in software engineering (SE). Many
existing studies are from outside SE, highlighting the importance of
developing SE-specific solutions. We aim to inspire further research on
LLMs’ performance in SE literature studies, acknowledging that findings
from current models may not apply to future iterations.
Complementary Tools: Tracing tools such asLangSmith allow for
tracing (debugging, testing) individual steps in LLM-based systems,
which is of use when engineering a systematic mapping tool. WebVoy-
ager [10] can search the entire internet using a visual approach. As
WebVoyager is not limited to research references, it may be of interest
for grey literature reviews.
We suggest two research directions:
•Improving individual steps and evaluating different strategies
(e.g., prompting) to optimize them.
•Build a prototype representing the overall mapping process to
gather further ideas for supporting mapping studies end-to-end.
