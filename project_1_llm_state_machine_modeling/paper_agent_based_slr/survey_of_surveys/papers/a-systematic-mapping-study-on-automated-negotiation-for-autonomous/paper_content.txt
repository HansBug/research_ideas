A systematic mapping study on automated negotiation
for autonomous intelligent systems
Mashal Afzal Memon1 · Gian Luca Scoccia2 · Marco Autili1
Received: 14 June 2024 / Accepted: 31 March 2025 / Published online: 2 May 2025
© The Author(s) 2025
Abstract
Autonomous intelligent systems are known as artificial intelligence software entities
that can act on their own and can take decisions without any human intervention.
The communication between such systems to reach an agreement for problem-solv -
ing is known as automated negotiation. This study aims to systematically identify
and analyze the literature on automated negotiation from four distinct viewpoints:
(1) the existing literature on negotiation with focus on automation, (2) the specific
purpose and application domain of the studies published in the domain of automated
negotiation, (3) the input, and techniques used to model the negotiation process, and
(4) the limitations of the state of the art and future research directions. For this pur -
pose, we performed a systematic mapping study (SMS) starting from 73,760 poten-
tially relevant studies belonging to 24 conference proceedings and 22 journal issues.
Through a precise selection procedure, we identified 50 primary studies, published
from the year 2000 onward, which were analyzed by applying a classification frame-
work. As a result, we provide: (a) a classification framework to analyze the auto-
mated negotiation literature according to several parameters (e.g., focus of the paper,
inputs required to carry on the negotiation process, techniques applied, and type of
agents involved in the negotiation), (b) an up-to-date map of the literature specify -
ing the purpose and application domain of each study, (c) a list of techniques used
to automate the negotiation process and the list of input to carry out the negotiation,
and (d) a discussion about promising challenges and their consequences for future
research. We also provide a replication package to help researchers replicate and
verify our systematic mapping study. The results and findings will benefit research-
ers and practitioners in identifying the research gap and conducting further research
to bring dedicated solutions for automated negotiation.
Keywords Autonomous intelligent systems · Multi-agent systems · Automated
negotiation · Automated decision-making

1 Introduction
The rapid evolution of artificial intelligence (AI) has given rise to autonomous
intelligent systems that can perform actions without human intervention and
make autonomous decisions (Zhang et al. 2017; Buiten 2019; Sharma et al. 2022;
Zhu et al. 2024). Such systems not only function autonomously, but also commu-
nicate on behalf of their users in various domains, e.g., e-commerce, healthcare,
autonomous vehicles, etc.  (Levinson et  al. 2011; Bagga et  al. 2021; Kell et  al.
2022).
When interacting with each other, these systems can be categorized into two
broad categories: cooperative autonomous systems and competitive autonomous
systems. Cooperative autonomous systems communicate and coordinate with
each other to perform a shared task (Chen and Weiss 2019; Rothfuß et al. 2020);
whereas competitive autonomous systems are selfish and only interact to maxi-
mize their own utility  (Tsumura 2021; Gao et  al. 2022). Therefore, how these
systems interact with each other and autonomously resolve conflicts or, more gen-
erally, agree on matters of mutual interest is the focus of interest. One of the pos-
sibilities to resolve conflicts between autonomous systems is negotiation between
these systems, which has gathered the research community’s interest in automated
negotiation, particularly within the domain of e-commerce (Sect. 4.1).
In general, negotiation is a process between multiple systems in which a deci-
sion is made jointly by communication, i.e., through the exchange of dialogues,
bids, and offers to reach an agreement that is further accepted by all parties
involved in the negotiation  (Zuckerman et  al. 2013; Bagga et  al. 2022). Auto-
mated negotiation is a compelling field of research that groups three familiar
research fields into one, namely, game theory, economics, and artificial intel-
ligence  (Baarslag et  al. 2016). The significance of automated negotiation is
receiving great attention in the present age, as intelligent agents who negotiate
with each other and represent human users are likely to be more effective  (An
et al. 2016). Hereafter, the terms “system” and “agent” are used interchangeably
throughout the text as an agent is a type of system that acts autonomously and
makes decisions on behalf of users (Wooldridge and Jennings 1995).
To meet the growing demand for automated systems involving intelligent
agents, the literature reports several reviews/surveys/mapping studies/second-
ary stuff focused on automated negotiation. Specifically, the survey by Kiruthika
et al. (2020) groups the studies in the literature based on stages of the life cycle
of a negotiation agent by considering opponent modeling only. The study presents
a list of techniques used in the literature to model opponents during negotiation.
As detailed below in Sect.  2, similar reviews of the negotiation literature have
been previously proposed also by Lopes et al. (2008), Kraus (2001), Chen and Pu
(2004), Khemakhem et al. (2022), Braun et al. (2006), Baarslag et al. (2016), Kell
et al. (2022), Eshragh et al. (2015), Li et al. (2003)). However, until now, despite
growing interest, the literature has not provided a comprehensive review of stud-
ies that propose practical implementation of approaches or discuss techniques
that can explicitly be employed to automate the negotiation process. In addition,

the literature falls short in clearly reporting the main purpose and the application
domain of the negotiation, which is needed to promote reuse and adoption. In
this paper, we provide the research community with a comprehensive overview
of the recent literature on automated negotiation, focusing on approach-oriented
studies to offer practical insights into how negotiation functions within autono-
mous systems. That is, by narrowing our scope to these studies, we aim to high-
light advances directly relevant to automation, giving a focused perspective that
complements broader theoretical explorations in the field and identifying gaps
where further innovation is needed. Although the selection process led to a rela-
tively small number of studies, by focusing solely on these, we aim to provide the
reader with findings that can directly contribute to the development and applica-
tion of autonomous intelligent systems.
The goal of this systematic mapping study is to differentiate existing research
in the field of automated negotiation from four distinct viewpoints: (1) the exist-
ing literature on negotiation with focus on automation, (2) the specific purpose
and application domain of the studies published in the domain of automated
negotiation, (3) the input, and techniques used to model the negotiation process,
ad (4) the limitations of the state of the art and future research directions.
To attain this goal, the methodology for systematic mapping study from Petersen
et  al. (2015), Wohlin et  al. (2012) is applied, which concentrates on answering
research questions about the state of the art by proposing a reproducible, objective,
and unbiased approach. Summing up, the major contributions of our study are:
1. a classification framework for identifying and evaluating techniques and
approaches for automated negotiation in the literature according to several param-
eters, e.g., focus of the paper, inputs required to carry on the negotiation process,
techniques applied, and type of agents involved in the negotiation—viewpoints
(1)–(4);
2. an up-to-date map of the state of the art proposed in the domain of automated
negotiation, identifying the main purpose, and application domain of each
study—viewpoint (2);
3. a list of techniques used for automating the decision-making process involving
autonomous agents in a negotiation process, and a list of inputs required to carry
out the negotiation process—viewpoint (3);
4. a discussion about promising challenges and their consequences for future
research on automated negotiation—viewpoint (4);
5. a replication package for duplication and verification of our proposed study—
viewpoints (1)–(4).
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section  2 provides background
knowledge of the negotiation process and lays out our study with related work.
Section  3 presents the design of our study from a methodological point of view.
The results of our study are presented in Sects.  4 and  5. Section  6 discusses future
research challenges and threats to validity are discussed in Sect.  7. Section  8 con-
cludes the mapping study.

2  Background and related work
In this section, we briefly discuss the negotiating agent’s life cycle and discuss
related studies in the literature. We focus on literature reviews, reports, and sur -
veys that provide an overview of techniques that can be applied to automate the
negotiation process. Table  1 presents the summary of the existing literature,
comparing the findings of these studies with our work.
2.1  Background
Automated negotiating agents operate through a life cycle comprising three con-
secutive phases (Kiruthika et al. 2020; Braun et al. 2006), namely, pre-negotia-
tion, negotiation, and optional post-negotiation.
During pre-negotiation, the agents focus on finalizing their attributes and per -
sonal preferences before initiating the negotiation. For example, in a negotiation
scenario where the seller and buyer agents interact to carry out a purchase, the
seller defines the value for preferred price that it would like to obtain and the
value for the minimum reserved price below which it cannot accept any offer.
Similarly, the buyer defines its values for the preferred price and the maximum
reserved price. Moreover, the time limit to end the negotiation with no deal is
also defined during this phase.
The negotiation phase comprises offer generation, opponent modeling, offer
evaluation, and an acceptance model for decision-making (Kiruthika et al. 2020).
During this phase, agents follow the negotiation protocol, which is a set of rules
that defines how they interact with opponent agents. Few examples include the
one-shot protocol in which one agent makes an offer that the opponent agent can
either accept or reject (Ji et al. 2014), the alternative offer protocol where every
agent can make an offer alternatively (Aydoğan et  al. 2017), and multi-lateral
negotiation protocol, in which participating agents take a decision cooperatively
by agreeing mutually on a decision (Caillere et al. 2016).
The post-negotiation phase is optional. If the agents involved in the nego-
tiation have found an agreement, its effectiveness can be evaluated during this
phase in terms of agent utility or reward. For instance, in the same seller and
buyer scenario mentioned above, the effectiveness of the decision can be eval-
uated by comparing the final offered value with the preferred value of both
agents. If the final offered value is near the preferred value of both agents, it can
be referred an effective decision (Bagga et al. 2022).
Agents can further be divided into cooperative, when negotiating to find a
mutually beneficial solution, and non-cooperative, when acting independently
and maximizing their own utility. Furthermore, agents can have complete or
incomplete information about the negotiation setting, e.g., other agent’s prefer -
ences (Li et al. 2003).

2.2  Related work
In the following, we present the studies related to our work, positioning our work
within the context, to highlight our contributions. The findings are summarized in
Table 1.
The proposed study by Lopes et al. (2008) highlights the framework of automated
negotiation that encompasses various stages, including pre-negotiation, negotiation,
and re-negotiation, and focuses on techniques used to select negotiation strategies
and choose a negotiation protocol to carry out the process. As presented in Table  1,
similar to our work, the study presents a classification framework that categorizes
the literature as theoretical or computational studies. Moreover, it provides a discus-
sion of the underlying concepts and notion employed in the negotiation process such
as bilateral or multilateral negotiation, single issue or multi-issue negotiation. The
study explains attributes of the negotiation process; however, it is state-of-the-art as
it mostly covers studies from the last decade. Compared to this study, our study cov -
ers the recent literature which focuses on automating the negotiation process using
a systematic approach. Our work offers a comprehensive overview of the negotia-
tion literature by adopting the classification framework outlined in Table  3, which
organizes the literature according to various categories, including the purpose of the
negotiation, the application domain, the techniques and inputs required to carry out
the negotiation and the limitation of each study.
Bilateral negotiation is a scenario in which only two agents are involved in the
negotiation. The literature review by Li et al. (2003) provides a detailed understand-
ing of bilateral negotiation, considering it as a game theory problem. The study dis-
cusses several scenarios of bargaining for price and provides a better understanding
of bilateral negotiation in economics and AI. Still referring to Table  1, the study
underscores its application domain in the Navy detail system, which operates as a
matching market where sailors and commands engage in negotiations similar to bar -
gaining scenarios observed in price-based negotiations. Consequently, the literature
focuses on the literature centered on economics. In contrast, our mapping study cov -
ers the literature that not only focuses on economics but also covers other applica-
tion domains, detailed in Sect.  4.1. In addition, the study lacks the review of the
recent state of the art and is limited only to the techniques used for bilateral negotia-
tion. Our study covers the recent literature with a more general focus on automated
negotiation involving the literature for bilateral as well as multilateral negotiation.
Complementing the previous study, the survey by  Kraus (2001) focuses on
approaches to negotiation in multi-agent environments where agents are considered
self-interested. It also mentions the techniques for cooperative agents. The proposed
study considers negotiation time, efficiency, and simplicity of negotiation strategies
as attributes for evaluating decisions. For example, negotiation time measures the
delay in reaching an agreement, which causes more computational resources com-
pared to the ones that reach an agreement early. Similarly, the efficiency of the deci-
sion can be evaluated by comparing the reward of all agents involved in the nego-
tiation, which should be equal for every agent to reach a successive agreement, and
simplicity can be evaluated by computing the time duration for generating strategies.
The primary focus of the proposed study is on strategic negotiation for price-based

negotiations with in economics. In contrast, our systematic mapping study focuses
on multiple goals to automate the negotiation process focusing on multiple applica-
tion domains.
Preference elicitation is a prerequisite before starting the negotiation process. As
mentioned in Sect. 2.1, the agents can finalize their preferences during the pre-nego-
tiation phase. Besides that, preference elicitation is also useful when an autonomous
agent negotiates on behalf of the human agent, since the goal of the automated deci-
sion system is to assist humans. Hence, it is necessary for autonomous agents to
accurately model user preferences. A survey by Chen and Pu (2004) explains prefer -
ence elicitation techniques, such as a value function that queries the user to rank the
outcome of the value function and clustering that combines and compares the pref-
erences of different users. Although the study provides some techniques for prefer -
ence elicitation, it lacks the techniques presented in the recent literature that focus on
the agent’s history of offers to predict user preferences and represent them in auto-
mated negotiation. Our study not only covers the literature that focus on preference
elicitation but also explores studies that address different purposes of negotiation.
Moreover, as presented in Table  1, the study by Chen and Pu (2004) organizes the
negotiation literature around specific parameters without explicitly mentioning the
classification framework. However, these studies majorly focus on a single aspect of
negotiation. In contrast, the classification framework in our study is designed to be
more comprehensive and generic, addressing multiple parameters.
A recent study by Khemakhem et al. (2022) provides a systematic review of deci-
sion-making systems based on multiple distributed agents. These systems employ
a conflict resolution strategy to overcome those cases in which discording answers
are given by the different agents that compose the system. Similar to our work, as
presented in Table  1, the study follows a systematic approach and presents a clas-
sification framework aimed at analyzing the application of agents. However, unlike
our work, the study focuses on conflict resolution strategies in general and reports
that negotiation is one of the techniques to resolve conflicts. The adopted negotia-
tion techniques and their characteristics are not reported. Our work, introduces a list
of techniques employed in the recent literature to automate the negotiation process.
The study by Braun et al. (2006) provides a review of software agents for elec-
tronic negotiation. This study provides a detailed review of the types of agents, the
tasks these agents can perform, the techniques that negotiator agents can employ
during the negotiation process, and the applications of these agents e.g., planning,
e-commerce, and scheduling, as presented in Table  1. However, similar to most
studies in the literature, the study by Braun et al. (2006) also covers the negotiation
literature of the last decade. Unlike these, our work adopts a systematic approach to
identify and analyze the most relevant literature in the field that focuses on automat-
ing the negotiation process.
Similarly, the authors  (Kiruthika et  al. 2020) propose a new model for the life
cycle of a negotiation agent, categorize the existing negotiation literature based on
the agent’s life cycle, and provides a list of techniques that agents use for modeling
the opponent to predict their preferences during negotiation. As reported in Table  1,
the study also organizes the negotiation literature based on the agent’s life cycle. No
classification framework is adopted. The study focuses primarily on techniques used

to model the opponent; our study covers a broader range of techniques with multiple
purposes, including opponent modeling, offer generation, and other key aspects.
The results of a closely related survey are reported in  Baarslag et  al. (2016),
focusing on techniques that can be used for opponent modeling. The authors pro-

study also presents a classification framework that extracts insights from studies,

A recent study by Kell et al. (2022) follows a systematic approach to provide a
review of machine learning techniques that can be used for bidding strategies in the

broader in scope, covering studies that apply negotiation to a variety of domains (see

learning (see Sect. 4.2).
A survey is presented in the study by Zhu et al. (2024), which offers a detailed
review of autonomous intelligent systems that employ lifelong learning. This
approach enhances the system adaptability, enabling it to continuously learn and
improve their performance in various environments. Although, similarly to us, the
survey focuses on autonomous intelligent systems that operate independently, this
work focuses solely on lifelong learning techniques for their functionality with no
consideration of negotiation. In contrast, our work focuses on multiple techniques
these systems can employ for negotiation with other systems in various application
domains.
Closely to our systematic approach, the study by Grislin-Le Strugeon et al. (2022)
investigates the potential of software agents for data mining. The study also presents
a classification framework to extract the data from studies through certain param-
eters. While the design of the study aligns closely with our approach, the investi-
gation conducted by Grislin-Le Strugeon et al. (2022) concentrates on autonomous
agents that perform data mining, whereas our research emphasizes automated nego-
tiation between intelligent agents.
For what specifically concerns the usage of a classification framework, in the
following we summarize its adoption in existing literature. The study by Baarslag
et al. (2016) introduces a classification framework to extract insights from research,
emphasizing the parameters used to learn about opponents only. Similarly, the work
of Lopes et al. (2008) presents a framework that categorizes studies as either theo-
retical or computational, while Khemakhem et al. (2022) proposes a classification
framework for analyzing the application of agents. The studies by Kiruthika et al.
(2020), Chen and Pu (2004) structure negotiation literature around specific parame-
ters, albeit without explicitly referencing a classification framework. All these works
predominantly focus on individual aspects of negotiation. In contrast, our classifi-
cation framework is designed to be more comprehensive and versatile, addressing

3  Study design
For our systematic mapping study, we structured our research into three main
phases, commonly employed in systematic literature studies  (Kitchenham and
Brereton 2013; Wohlin et al. 2012; Grislin-Le Strugeon et al. 2022): planning, con-
ducting, and documenting.
Planning— During this phase, we realized (1) the need to perform a systematic
mapping study on automated negotiation for agreement, (described in Sect.  2.2), and
(2) we defined the research questions (further discussed in Sect. 3.1).
Conducting— At this stage, we followed the required steps to perform this map-
ping study such as (1) searching for and selecting relevant studies for automated

3.1  Research questions
The aim of this study is to help the research community to summarize the state of
the art on automated negotiation that proposes practical implementation approaches,
while highlighting existing limitations, open challenges, and future research direc-
tions. Specifically, the goal of our mapping study is formulated by using the Goal-
Question-Metric perspectives (i.e., purpose, issue, object, viewpoint (Caldiera and
Rombach 1994)). Table  2 shows the resulting outcome. We further divided our goal
in the following research questions:
RQ1 What is the specific purpose of the negotiation?
With this research question, we aim to identify and categorize the reasons for
which negotiation is employed in the literature, to provide comprehensive list of the
negotiation purposes, and its possible application domains.
RQ2 Which inputs (RQ2.a) and techniques (RQ2.b) are employed to carry out the
negotiation?
This research question explores how the negotiation process is carried out. Spe-
cifically, we investigate (a) what inputs are needed to carry out the negotiation, and
(b) what techniques are applied to automate the negotiation process.
RQ3 What are the limitations of the current approaches, open issues, and future
challenges?
Automated negotiation is a promising research topic. However, to reap its bene-
fits, there are still limitations and deficiencies that need to be overcome to enable its
practical application in a real-world scenarios. Hence, this research question focuses
on assessing and categorizing the limitations and open issues present in the current
state of the art, and addresses the future work to overcome these limitations.

nowadays the research community is focused on embedding ethics into automated
decision making  (Lo Piano 2020; Yu et  al. 2018), we believe that no research is
available that in real immerses the concept of ethics into automated decision mak -
ing, which opens the doors for future research. We further discuss the need for an
ethical autonomous system in Sect. 6.

7  Threats to validity
In this section, we discuss the quality of the data and threats to the validation of
primary studies. At first, we defined a research protocol to verify the high quality
of the acquired data. The research protocol was established before proceeding with
the data collection and was designed by following the guidelines for planning the
systematic mapping study from Keele et al. (2007). As presented in Sect.  3, we rig-
idly complied with the research protocol during the evolution of the proposed study.
In addition, we combined and executed all steps involved in the research protocol
(e.g., study design, search and selection, data extraction, and data analysis) together
in teams to additionally guarantee the quality attributes of the established research
protocol. In the remainder of this section, we further discuss threats to validity in the
context of our study and how we did our best to diminish them.
External validity. This stage deals with validating the risk of quality assurance
(Wohlin et al. 2012), i.e., whether the proposed study correctly identified the state-
of-the-art literature or not. To mitigate this type of risk, we selected top-level venues
for searching publications where we manually applied keyword-based search and
selection processes for conference proceedings and journal publications. One pos-
sible threat to the external validity of our study is the possibility that, despite our
efforts, some relevant venues were not included in the ranking used as a base for
this selection process. However, this threat is mitigated by the subsequent snowball-
ing process, during which forward and backward snowballing have been applied to
extend the set of primary studies in order to have a comprehensive set of studies
representative of the literature of the field. Furthermore, we only extracted peer-
reviewed publications and eliminated short papers, preprints, and editorials. This
process was applied to maintain quality, as peer-reviewed studies are an essential
item for high-grade publications. Subsequently, we applied a set of inclusion and
exclusion criteria, which led to further refinement of the proposed study.
Internal validity. This stage deals with validating the risk related to the study
design (Wohlin et al. 2012). To mitigate this type of risk, we established a research
protocol and a classification framework. The classification framework is designed
and strictly followed as detailed in Sect.  3, where the design of our study is pre-
sented from a methodological point of view. The researchers involved in the pro-
posed study have independently followed the framework to adopt fairly evaluated
descriptive statistics of the research. Subjective biases and interpretations were mit-
igated by closely complying with the selection criteria to evaluate the studies. In
addition, meetings were held regularly during the selection and analysis process to
jointly discuss examples and doubts. Regarding the validity of the synthesis of the
collected data, well-assessed descriptive statistics have been employed for both the
vertical and horizontal analysis, so the threats are minimal. Furthermore, during the
horizontal analysis, we cross-analyzed the values of different concepts and attributes
of the classification framework to perform a sanity test of the extracted data. This
analysis confirmed the consistency of the extracted data.
Construct validity. This stage focuses on ensuring the justification and compat-
ibility of the primary studies with the research questions (Wohlin et al. 2012). In

--- Page 34 ---
 Automated Software Engineering (2025) 32:44
 44 Page 34 of 46
the context of mapping studies, it mainly relates to ensuring that the selected pri-
mary studies were suited to answer the research questions. To ensure that, we manu-
ally extracted the literature from top-level venues related to computer science and
applications, without relying on a single scientific search engine. This allowed us to
avoid potential biases due to publishers’ policies and business concerns. During this
step, a keyword-based search was performed. We are reasonably confident about the
construction of the search query since the terms used are very general and suited to
identify studies related to our research questions. The results of this process were
further expanded by applying forward and backward snowballing. Subsequently, a
screening of identified studies was performed in accordance with the inclusion and
exclusion criteria. Furthermore, as suggested by Wohlin (Wohlin et al. 2012), a ran-
dom set of 10 studies was extracted and analyzed by all authors in order to guarantee
alignment of the analyses.
Conclusion validity. In this stage, the focus is to ensure the validity of the conclu-
sion derived based on primary studies (Wohlin et al. 2012). To do that, we mitigated
potential sources of bias during the data extraction and analysis processes by strictly
adhering to our previously established research protocol, specifically designed to
extract data useful to answer our research questions. Furthermore, to reduce biases,
we followed well-established guidelines for systematic mapping studies  (Kitchen-
ham and Brereton 2013; Petersen et al. 2008a, 2015) to diminish the risk regarding
the validity of the conclusion. Finally, collected data and employed scripts are made
available in the replication package to ensure the transparency of our research.
8  Conclusion
The proposed systematic mapping study (SMS) analyzes relevant studies on auto-
mated negotiation for autonomous systems. For this purpose, we extracted 50 pri-
mary studies from 73,760 relevant studies through a selection process from the top-
ranked publication venues. We applied keyword-based search to extract relevant
studies from 24 conferences and 22 journals, followed by backward and forward
snowballing. We follow the precise inclusion and exclusion criterion to include only
studies that focus on the practical implementation of the techniques proposed for
automated negotiation.
We designed a classification framework to identify, in particular, (1) the specific
purpose of the negotiation, (2) the required inputs and expected outputs of the nego-
tiation process together with the techniques applied to automate the process, (3) the
limitation of the state of the art and the future research directions, and (4) possi-
ble directions for consolidating the existing literature on negotiation with a focus on
automation.
We present the key results of our systematic mapping study by introducing a
blend of content analysis and narrative synthesis (vertical analysis), and correspond-
ence analysis through contingency tables (horizontal analysis). We found that 27

--- Page 35 ---
Automated Software Engineering (2025) 32:44 Page 35 of 46 44
studies in the literature primarily focus on economics, raising questions about poten-
tial applications in other domains; 12 studies involve human agents, raising concerns
about fully automating the negotiation. This emphasizes the importance of build-
ing trust in these systems, as autonomous systems are designed to make decisions
on the user’s behalf. We have also discussed the significance of these aspects for
future research. The growing interest in AI technologies highlights that automated
negotiation can play a central role in future AI-based autonomous systems. Indeed,
future autonomous systems will act on humans’ behalf and make decisions for them
in a fully automated way while interacting with other humans or other autonomous
systems (Matthews et al. 2021). Hence, in the future, these systems can benefit from
negotiation techniques to automatically achieve more equitable and fair interactions
with humans and other systems.
This study further helps the research community find potential studies published
in the domain of automated negotiation that also consider practical implementation
aspects. To the best of our knowledge, no research such as our systematic mapping
study is available that introduces a set of potential studies regarding automated nego-
tiation that provides the practical implementation details of the proposed techniques.
Hence, we believe that our study establishes a valuable asset for both practitioners
and researchers in the wide spectrum of autonomous systems that involve intelligent
agents aiming to reach an agreement to automatically find a solution that satisfies all
involved parties.
