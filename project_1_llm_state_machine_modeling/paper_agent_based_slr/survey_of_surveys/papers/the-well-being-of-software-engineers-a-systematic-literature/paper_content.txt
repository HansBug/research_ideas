The well‑being of software engineers: a systematic literature 
review and a theory
Povilas Godliauskas1  · Darja Šmite2,3 
Accepted: 5 September 2024 / Published online: 3 December 2024 
© The Author(s) 2024
Abstract
For decades, software engineering research and practice has focused primarily on tech-
nological and process-related factors. Today, there is a growing interest in organizational, 
social, and psychological factors, including well-being. Organizational studies show that 
well-being contributes to work outcomes, including creativity, performance, and produc-
tivity. But despite its importance, the predictors and outcomes of software engineers’ well-
being as a multidimensional construct to date are under-researched. This paper reports on 
the multidimensional well-being of professional software engineers and generates insights 
for the future research in this area. 44 quantitative survey studies published between 2000 
and 2023 were selected and synthesized both quantitatively and qualitatively through a sys-
tematic literature review. The results of the review were further analyzed to construct a 
quantitatively-testable theory, detailing the predictors and outcomes of well-being in soft-
ware engineering organizations. The total number of research participants included in the 
selected studies is 16,086 software engineering professionals from at least 42 countries. 
The literature review identified various measures, constructs, and indicators of well-being, 
as well as its predictors and outcomes. The theory, based on cumulative results of carefully 
selected quantitative studies, is an attempt to “correct the record” by establishing well-
being in software engineering as a meta-construct of hedonic, eudaimonic, and integrated 
or hedaimonic qualities predicted by different individual, team and organizational factors 
and impacting the functioning of software engineers and their organizations. The review 
highlighted the under-researched aspects of well-being in software engineering and con-
firmed the need for more advanced quantitative studies. We hope that the theory will ben-
efit researchers in conducting future studies and practitioners in developing nuanced and 
science-based interventions for improving software engineers’ well-being.

1 Introduction
The goal of positive psychology (i.e., the science of well-being) is to understand and 
increase the flourishing of individuals, communities, and societies (Seligman and Csiksze-
ntmihalyi 2000; Seligman 2011). The relationship between positive attitudes and work per -
formance is considered the "Holy Grail" of organizational studies (Weiss and Cropanzano 
1996). Recent systematic literature reviews show that well-being is associated with better 
work outcomes, such as performance, productivity, creativity, citizenship, and customer 
satisfaction (Salas-Vallina et  al. 2018; García-Buades et  al. 2019). In software engineer -
ing research, an increased number of studies are investigating well-being using innovative 
methods, such as sentiment analysis (Mäntylä et  al. 2018a, b). However, software engi-
neers’ well-being as a multidimensional construct is still under-researched (Lenberg et al. 
2015; Sánchez-Gordón and Colomo-Palacios 2019), especially due to a lack of quantitative 
psychological studies.
Many researchers nowadays agree that well-being is a multidimensional construct that 
can include physical, emotional, cognitive, and social aspects of personal, professional, and 
collective functioning (Dodge 2012; Lomas et al. 2014; Linton et al. 2016). However, to 
the best of our knowledge, there are no comprehensive multidimensional models or theo-
ries of well-being that describe and explain what makes software engineers flourish in their 
personal and professional lives (Singh and Junnarkar 2016). In fact, many studies seem 
to reduce the software engineers’ well-being to the absence of negative affective states, 
such as distress and burnout (Maudgalya et al. 2006; Chilton et al. 2010; Kuriakose et al. 
2019; Rezvani and Khosravi 2019; Mellblom et al. 2019; Heshimova et al. 2020; Kuutila 
et al. 2020; Zaidman and Cohen 2020), or the presence of positive affective states, such as 
positive affect or motivation (Graziotin et al. 2015a, b, 2017, 2018; Ke and Zhang 2018). 
Therefore, the bias towards unidimensional affective/motivational states is a serious con-
ceptual issue.
In this paper, we report our results from conducting a systematic literature review on 
well-being of software engineers and synthesize the evidence from existing studies to 
arrive at a theory that explains the relationships between the predictors (antecedents) and 
outcomes (consequences) of software engineers’ well-being. Our research is driven by the 
following research questions:
RQ1.What are the known constructs, indicators, and measures of well-being relevant for 
software engineers?
RQ2.What are the known predictors of well-being of software engineers?
RQ3.What are the known outcomes of well-being of software engineers?
We have addressed these research questions through a systematic literature review and 
formulated a theory of well-being of software engineers based on carefully selected quan-
titative studies. The purpose of the developed theory is to provide a rationale for further 
advanced psychological studies of well-being in software engineering, and to provide 
actionable advice for leaders to increase the well-being of their employees.
The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section  2 outlines related research and 
existing models of well-being in general and in software engineering in particular. Sec-
tion  3 details the steps of the systematic literature review, including the search strategy, 
study selection, data extraction, quality assessment, the methodology behind the data syn-
thesis and theory development, and the deviations from the protocol. Section  4 reports the

2  Background
2.1  Definition of Well‑Being
According to Pollard and Lee (2003), well-being is "a complex, multi-faceted construct 
that has continued to elude researchers’ attempts to define and measure" (p. 60). In positive 
psychology, definitions and measures of general well-being are grounded in at least three 
traditions (Dodge et al. 2012; Ng and Fisher 2013; Huta 2015; Cookie 2016):
• hedonic well-being marked by higher levels of life satisfaction and positive emotion 
and lower levels of negative emotion;
• eudaimonic well-being marked by higher levels of meaning and self-determination;
• integrated well-being marked by constructs sharing both hedonic and eudaimonic quali-
ties, such as higher levels of flow and engagement.
Hedonic well‑being  is one of the oldest and most extensively studied traditions, rooted in 
ancient Greek philosophy. According to Aristippus, a Greek philosopher and the founder 
of the Cyrenaic school of hedonist philosophy (fourth century BCE), pleasure is the only 
good and pain is the only evil in life; therefore, the goal of life is to seek pleasure and avoid 
pain (Ng and Fisher 2013; Huta 2015). Other well-known hedonist thinkers who empha-
sized the importance of pleasure over pain include Thomas Hobbes (psychological ego-
ism), Jeremy Bentham (utilitarianism), and Sigmund Freud (pleasure principle). In psy -
chological terms, hedonic well-being can be defined as the presence of positive affect or 
pleasant emotional arousal (e.g., joy, enthusiasm, and determination) and the absence of 
negative affect or unpleasant emotional arousal (e.g., sadness, distress, and irritation) (Wat-
son et al. 1988; Weiss and Cropanzano 1996). However, contemporary conceptualizations 
of hedonic well-being (rebranded as “subjective well-being” or “SWB”) usually include an 
emotional component marked by high positive affect and low negative affect and a cogni-
tive component marked by high life satisfaction (Diener 1984; Diener et al. 1985; Pavot 
and Diener 2008).
Eudaimonic well‑being  is also rooted in Greek philosophy. In Nicomachean Ethics, Aris-
totle wrote that true happiness is pursued by living a “good life” marked by reason and vir -
tue rather than hedonistic pursuits (Huta 2015). In modern terms, eudaimonia (rebranded 
as “psychological well-being” or “PWB”) is experienced while fulfilling psychological 
needs for autonomy, competence, environmental mastery, growth, self-acceptance, positive 
relationships, and purpose (Ryff 1989; Ryff and Keyes 1995; Ryff and Singer 2006). Simi-
larly, self-determination theory argues that when people fulfill their needs for autonomy,

competence, and relatedness or relationships, they are more intrinsically motivated to pur -
sue eudaimonic activities (Deci and Ryan 2000; Ryan et al. 2006).
Integrated well‑being  is a relatively modern attempt to bring hedonic and eudaimonic 
traditions together (Tulip et al. 2020). It is mostly associated with the PERMA model that 
defines well-being as a composite factor of positive emotions, engagement, positive rela-
tionships, meaning, and accomplishment or achievement (Seligman 2011, 2018). In our 
view, integrated well-being is also closely linked to experiences of flow and engagement. 
According to Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1975, 2008), the developer of flow theory, we 
experience flow when we immerse and lose our selves in tasks a little bit beyond our cur -
rent skills and abilities (marker of eudaimonic well-being). For this reason, the state can be 
highly rewarding and enjoyable (marker of hedonic well-being). At work, we experience 
flow or absorption—one of the markers of engagement—when we are fully focused and 
engrossed in our work (Schaufeli et al. 2002; Bakker et al. 2008).
In the clinical literature, we can find another tradition that conceptualizes well-being 
as a form of health (Jahoda 1958; Engel 1997). For example, the World Health Organiza-
tion1 defines health as “a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not 
merely the absence of disease or infirmity”. In the two-continua model of mental health 
continuum, well-being is marked by the presence of flourishing (i.e., high mental health) 
and absence of languishing (i.e., low mental health or mental illness) (Keyes 2002, 2005). 
We consider the clinical tradition also as a form of integrated well-being that emphasizes 
positive states (marker of hedonic well-being) and positive functioning (marker of eudai-
monic well-being). Furthermore, Linton et  al. (2016) reviewed around 100 measures of 
health and well-being and grouped them into: physical (i.e., bodily functioning), mental 
(i.e., cognitive and emotional quality of life), social (i.e., relationships and social interac-
tions), and spiritual well-being (i.e., meaning and self-transcendence). Linton’s research 
concluded that well-being measures strongly resemble general health measures. However, 
it should be noted that the paper focused mainly on measures used in healthcare rather than 
organizational settings and did not explore existing conceptual or theoretical differences 
between health and well-being.
With respect to organizational settings, the last two decades marked an increased inter -
est in employees’ job satisfaction, happiness, and other positive states (García-Buades et al. 
2019; Bhatia and Mohsin 2020). The growing interest is associated with the radical shift 
from machine bureaucracy to increased adhocracy in the organizational contexts (Mintz-
berg 1984; Lee and Edmondson 2017) and the emergence of new trends in organizational 
research, such as positive organizational behavior (Luthans 2002), positive organizational 
scholarship (Cameron et al. 2003), and positive organizational psychology (Donaldson and 
Ko 2010).
According to the review by Salas-Vallina et  al. (2018), the happiness of knowledge-
intensive workers is indicated by various constructs, including commitment (i.e., connec-
tion with an organization), engagement (i.e., optimal functioning at work), job satisfaction 
(i.e., positive appraisal of one’s work), psychological capital (i.e., optimism, efficacy, resil-
ience, and hope), and well-being (i.e., hedonic and eudaimonic). Furthermore, the review 
also shows that the most prominent theoretical framework of happiness at work is the Job 
Demands-Resources Model. The model holds that happiness is the result of the appraised

2.2  Health and Well‑Being in Software Engineering
For many decades, software engineering research and practice has focused primarily on 
technological and process-related factors with very little attention to organizational, social, 
or psychological factors (Lenberg et al. 2015). However, the shift from linear plan-driven 
waterfall approaches to iterative agile methods that rely on self-organization instead of 
"top-down" direction (Galindo Bello 2018) also fueled the interest in psychological factors. 
Management philosophy in agile environments values individuals and interactions more 
than processes and tools (Fowler and Highsmith 2001), contributing to team performance, 
project outcomes, software quality, and a more balanced workload (Venkatesh et al. 2020). 
Successful use of agile methods highly depends on individual and team behaviors and 
characteristics, such as openness for innovation (Vishnubhotla et  al. 2020) and interper -
sonal skills (Boehm and Turner 2003; Hummel et al. 2013), which ultimately changes the 
professional profile of software engineers.
Software engineering as a profession unites various specialists, both technical (e.g., pro-
grammers and testers) and not so technical (e.g., analysts and product managers).2 Based 
on the data of various systematic literature reviews, software engineers are believed to have 
distinct socioeconomic, motivational, emotional, and temperamental characteristics. Soci-
oeconomically, they are considered intelligent, well-educated, and high-paid (Maudgalya 
et al. 2006). Motivationally, they are driven by technical challenges, problem-solving, and 
benefiting society (Beecham et al. 2008; França et al. 2011). Emotionally, they are more 
prone to negative emotions (fear, sadness, and anger) than positive ones (Sánchez-Gordón 
et al. 2019). Temperamentally, they tend to be more introverted than extroverted and rely 
more on their thoughts, sensations, and judgments than on feelings, intuitions, and percep-
tions (Cruz et al. 2015). Based on the above, software engineers’ interests, motivation, and 
temperament might be at odds with the social nature of modern software development, 
which can be detrimental to their mental health and well-being—an important field of 
study.
Mental health and well-being are important aspects of software engineers’ lived experi-
ence. Related studies demonstrate that software engineers work in a dynamic environment 
marked by tight deadlines, high workload, task interdependence, and uncertainty (Kunda 
1992; Kameo 2017; Zaidman and Cohen 2020), which are especially relevant for agile con-
texts (Venkatesh et al. 2020). Therefore, it is not surprising that software engineers per -
ceive elevated levels of stress and exhaustion (Thong and Yap 2000; Windeler et al. 2017; 
Suárez and Vizcaíno 2023). Second, increasing focus on social skills and teamwork has 
been found to be sources of potential exhaustion and job dissatisfaction, especially when 
team members lack interpersonal skills (Venkatesh et al. 2020), are less agreeable and less 
extroverted (Acuña et al. 2015; Rodrigues and Rebelo 2020). On the contrary, emotionally

One may believe that software engineering profession is associated with particularly 
negative mental health. Yet, proneness to psychological suffering does not necessarily 
mean the absence of well-being. Research suggests that individuals can experience com-
plex states of well-being simultaneously comprising of both positive and negative affect 
(Berrios 2017a, b, 2018). Contrary to Stack Overflow results, Graziotin et al. (2017, 2018) 
demonstrated that professional software engineers experience slightly more positive affect 
than other studied groups, such as students and workers from other areas. There are several 
possible reasons why software engineers may feel happier than other populations. First, 
software engineers have a high potential to experience positive states like engagement 
(Alsunki et al 2020) and flow (Murphy 2014; Pratt et al. 2016), leading to higher levels of 
performance (Graziotin et al. 2014a) and productivity (Graziotin et al. 2014b) and lower 
levels of turnover (Ghapanchi and Aurum 2011). Finally, research shows that time pressure 
and stress are not always detrimental to well-being, as they can be perceived as a positive 
challenge rather than a hindrance (Chong et al. 2011).
The abovementioned findings contribute to the debate about the uniqueness of software 
engineering as a profession. The negative professional characteristics are worrisome and 
deserve further attention. Related research also suggests that the different aspects of soft-
ware engineers’ well-being, due to their complex interplay, cannot be studied in isolation. 
Therefore, there is a need for a more holistic understanding of software engineers’ well-
being and related factors.

3.1  Search Strategy
We performed an automated search in six digital libraries, two indexing services, and one 
professional network (see Table  1). Contrary to the recommended practice (Kitchenham 
2007, 2015), we did not perform a manual search, such as forward snowballing (i.e., search 
via citations) or backward snowballing (i.e., search via reference lists) to retain the repeat-
ability of the search results and avoid the bias that could arise from selective use of refer -
ences and citations. After all, researchers are prone to confirmation bias, i.e., the tendency 
to verify rather than falsify one’s claims (Hergovich et al. 2010; Lilienfeld 2017; Schumm 
2021).
Following various guidelines for performing systematic literature reviews in software 
engineering (Kitchenham 2007, 2015; Wohlin et al 2013; Stapic et al. 2016), we selected 
ACM, IEEE, ScienceDirect, and Scopus as databases that cover the most established jour -
nals and conference proceedings. We added PubMed and EBSCO, since they have appeared 
in relevant systematic reviews of well-being studies outside the software engineering litera-
ture (Linton et al. 2016; Batz-Barbarich et al. 2018; Gonot-Schoupinsky and Garip 2018; 
García-Buades et al 2019). Finally, we added ResearchGate to reduce publication bias, as it 
includes conference papers and papers in print that might not be indexed in digital libraries 
and indexing services. However, due to the limited search engine of ResearchGate, Google 
Search was used as an intermediary search engine to obtain results, e.g., researchgate.net/
publication happiness” “software developers” (refer to section “Search Strings” in our 
Online appendix).
Search terms related to well-being are based on popular constructs and indicators of 
hedonic, eudaimonic, and integrated well-being (see Table  2). The diverse conceptualiza-
tions are discussed in many reviews of well-being (Dodge 2012; Ng and Fisher 2013; Huta 
2015; Cookie 2016). To narrow down the search results to the software engineering field, 
we added terms related to software engineering, including programmers, software engi-
neers, software development, software engineering, and software engineers, as suggested 
in other related reviews. We considered adding the term “programming”, but after piloting 
the search in ACM, Wiley, and EBSCO, we realized that it results in too many irrelevant 
papers, related to educational programs or genetic programming. Finally, the pilot search

3.2  Study Selection
The studies were selected based on justified inclusion and exclusion criteria (see Table  4). 
First, we have included all studies published in academic venues, such as scientific journal 
articles and conference papers, written in English and published between 2000 and 2023. 
We chose to start with 2000s, when the focus shifted from process and tools to individuals 
and interactions (Fowler and Highsmith 2001) and when positive organizational behavior 
(Luthans 2002) and positive organizational scholarship (Cameron et al. 2003) were intro-
duced. Second, we focused only on quantitative studies in order to perform a fair com-
parison among the studies. Also, quantitative methods are the most common in well-being 
research (Helwig et al. 2020). Third, we excluded studies on such aspects as cognitive load, 
stress, and time pressure, since these affective states alone do not constitute well-being. 
Finally, we excluded studies focusing on students of software engineering, unemployed 
software engineers, and workers not related to software engineering.
To ensure the quality of the study selection process, the authors compared their inclu-
sion and exclusion decisions by going through a sample of 100 articles obtained through 
ACM Digital Library using the search strategy (see Section  3.1). The agreement between 
the authors was 100 percent: both authors excluded 99 articles; one article was initially

excluded, and 220 full-text records were assessed for eligibility. The significantly high 
number of irrelevant studies is natural in the automatic search of software development 
studies, especially when many terms are included in the search (Kitchenham 2007). After 
screening the full-text information, 44 studies were included in the review, while 176 were 
excluded. The study was excluded if it used non-correlational or non-experimental meth-
ods (e.g., case or field study, information extraction study); studied irrelevant variables 
(i.e., not constructs or indicators of well-being); surveyed irrelevant samples (e.g., students, 
hobbyists); lacked clear operationalization of relevant variables; lacked clear analytical 
information (e.g., correlation/regression coefficients and their significance values); demon-
strated features of a non-academic study; was non-English.
3.3  Data Extraction
Data extraction was guided by the research questions and performed manually. Data 
extracted includes the following:
• General information on the publication (IDs; title; date; authors; institutions; publica-
tion types; publication venues);
• General information on the methodology (research participants: sample size, sample 
location, gender, professional role; research methods);
• Information on the study of well-being: research measures applied to study well-being; 
constructs/indicators of well-being; predictors and outcomes of well-being; results of 
statistical tests and relationships (correlations and regressions).
Data was extracted by the first author, who is a qualified psychologist with a background 
in quantitative survey studies, to ensure the accuracy of the construct understanding. The 
extracted data is available in our Online appendix.
3.4  Quality Assessment
The quality assessment (QA) was performed by the first author as an additional exclu-
sion step to screen out studies of low quality. The QA criteria were adapted from Yang 
et al. (2021) who synthesized the most common criteria in a recent tertiary study. The QA 
included various stages (i.e., design, execution, analysis, and conclusion) and characteris-
tics (i.e., rationality, rigor, credibility, and contribution). For this study, the stages and char -
acteristics were combined into four final categories, while the statements were transformed 
into questions (see Table 5).
Realizing that simple yes/no answers can be unreliable (Kitchenham 2007), the stud-
ies were evaluated on a scale from 1 to 4, where 1 – strongly disagree, 2 – disagree, 3 
– agree, and 4 – strongly agree. The quality scores were calculated as follows: the sum of 
evaluations divided by the total sum of evaluations (i.e., 56) and multiplied by 100 (see 
section “Quality” in the Online appendix for the quality assessment results). According

In terms of the indicators of well-being, we grouped variables, such as job satisfac-

variables into broader constructs, these theories would guide the clustering process. For 
example, factors like life satisfaction and positive affect would constitute subjective or 
hedonic well-being, whereas experiences of autonomy, competence, relatedness, and 
meaning would constitute psychological or eudaimonic well-being (see Section  2.1). To 
identify whether certain constructs shared either more similarities or differences, we 
would also consult the semantics of the respective survey items and questions that were 
adopted to collect data on a particular indicator of well-being. As a result, we arrived at 
a two-level categorization with three core categories: hedonic, eudaimonic, and hedai-
monic or integrated well-being. The same process was organized to homogenize the pre-
dictors and outcomes of well-being.
In terms of the predictors of well-being, variables that describe personality traits, such 
as extroversion or neuroticism, would constitute the main category of personality predic-
tors, while variables that describe career orientations, such as managerial and technical, 
would constitute the main category of career motivation predictors. Consequently, person-
ality and career motivation would be further subsumed into the core category of individual 
factors, because both main categories describe individual traits and preferences of software 
engineering professionals that they bring to their organizations. Similarly, main catego-
ries, such as job predictors, team predictors, and workplace predictors, would constitute 
the core category of organizational predictors, which are circumstances that are “brought 
onto” software engineering professionals by their teams and organizations.
In the second step, we identified and synthesized the relationships between indicators of 
well-being and their respective predictors and outcomes using a form of inductive reason-
ing, that is, moving from specific observations to general theory. In this case, our observa-
tions were guided by the statistical significance of the relationships between well-being 
and its predictors and outcomes. Due to the differences in the measures of well-being and 
research participants, we were unable to perform a meta-analysis, which prevented us from 
calculating common effect sizes and providing more validity to the theory. Nevertheless, 
the results of the review still empowered us to develop a testable theory and its visualiza-
tion (see Section  4.3) using the guidelines and recommendations proposed by Sjøberg et al. 
(2008).
The statistically significant relationships between the variables were used to formulate 
theoretical propositions. The goal of these propositions is to establish which factors pre-
dict which constructs of well-being, and which constructs of well-being predict which out-
comes. As such, our theory is a type III theory, as defined by Gregor (2006). In contrast to 
theories that guide analysis and description (Type I) and theories that guide understanding 
(Type II), predictive theories (Type III) are expected to predict what will happen if certain 
conditions hold with an approximate or probabilistic degree of certainty (Gregor 2006). 
The theoretical propositions can be used to test the theory through further empirical stud-
ies, whereas primary studies can be consulted to construct explanations and justifications.

3.6  Deviations From the Protocol
The original protocol (see the “Protocol” in our Online appendix) was reviewed by two 
reviewers at the School of Psychology, University of East London, and piloted by the first 
author against the software engineering research literature. Based on the feedback and pilot 
search, some changes were made. First, the review type was changed from an integrative 
review to a systematic literature review of software development studies. Also, the number

increased. Then, the mixed search strategy was replaced with an automated search. Finally, 
the rationale, aims, questions, search strategy and terms, inclusion/exclusion criteria, data 
extraction, quality assessment, and publication plans were expanded or redefined.

4.3.1  Constructs
The proposed theory is constructed by three types of elements as theory’s building blocks: 
well-being, its predictors, and its outcomes. The theory divides well-being into three cat-
egories (see Fig.  6 in the middle): hedonic, eudaimonic, and integrated or hedaimonic 
well-being, which is a neologism that we coined to identify indicators of well-being that 
combine markers that share both hedonic and eudaimonic qualities. The predictors of well-
being (see Fig.  6 on the left) are the factors that have been found to predict changes in 
well-being and are divided into individual factors (including career motivation, health, and 
personality) and organizational factors, including job, team and workplace predictors. The 
outcomes of well-being (see Fig.  6 on the right) denote the changes that well-being pre-
dicts on the two levels: individual outcomes (marked by health and career characteristics) 
and organizational outcomes, marked by job and team characteristics.

4.3.2  Propositions
The relationships between constructs (i.e., groups of deductively and inductively clustered 
variables) are based on statistical regressions extracted from the selected quantitative stud-
ies (see Fig.  4). Arrow means the direction of the regression. Color yellow signifies cat-
egories of well-being, whereas color green signifies that the coefficient of the regression 
is positive and color red means that it is negative. Information in brackets signifies the IDs 
of the selected well-being studies. The relationships can be summarized with the following 
three propositions:
Proposition 1: Software engineers’ well-being is marked by three forms of well-being: 
hedonic, eudaimonic, and integrated or hedaimonic well-being.
Proposition 2: The well-being of software engineers is predicted by two groups of fac-
tors:
a. individual factors (include software engineers’ career motivation, health, and per -
sonality);
b. organizational factors (include software engineers’ job, team, and workplace char -
acteristics).
Proposition 3: The software engineers’ well-being predicts the functioning of software 
engineers (i.e., health and career outcomes) and their organizations (i.e., job and team 
outcomes).
All propositions of the theory are empirically and quantitatively testable. The exact rela-
tionships in the theory should be explored in future studies, using statistical analysis, such 
as regression analysis or structural equation modeling. Below each principle is described in 
greater detail.
Proposition 1: Software engineers well-being is marked by three forms of well-being: 
hedonic, eudaimonic, and integrated or hedaimonic well-being
All three forms of well-being are important when discussing software engineers’ 
well-being.
Notably, satisfaction—the cognitive dimension of hedonic well-being—is considered 
the primary or core component of the model for at least two reasons. Satisfaction-related 
constructs (i.e., job and life satisfaction) were studied more frequently than positive affect, 
which is the emotional dimension of hedonic well-being. For this reason, satisfaction 
encompasses affect and therefore is considered the primary form of software engineers’ 
hedonic well-being. Two, satisfaction was the most frequently studied category of software 
engineers’ well-being in terms of its presence in regression analyses.
Although hedonic well-being is the primary element of the model, that does not mean 
other elements of well-being are less significant. On the contrary, the model hypothesizes 
that eudaimonic and hedaimonic well-being dimensions boost or strengthen the positive 
and well-established relationship between satisfaction and individual and organizational 
outcomes. The hypothesis is based on studies that found positive relationships between 
different forms of well-being (i.e., between hedonic and hedaimonic/eudaimonic). For 
instance, positive affect predicts work engagement (WB8). Similarly, life satisfaction 

--- Page 21 ---
Empirical Software Engineering (2025) 30:35 
 Page 21 of 42 35
is predicted by several forms of eudaimonic well-being, that is, satisfaction of needs for 
autonomy (WB30, WB07), competence (WB30), and relatedness or relationships (WB30).
Proposition 2: The well-being of software engineers is predicted by individual and 
organizational factors
Individual factors, such as career motivation, health, and personality, are the software 
engineers’ internal or psychological characteristics. Individual factors describe the quali-
ties that software engineers bring to their team and organization. First, health describes 
three forms of ill-health: mental (e.g., anxiety stress), physical (e.g., behavioral disen-
gagement, lack of daily routines), and social (e.g., emotional and social loneliness). Vari-
ous forms of psychological dysfunction are negatively associated with hedonic well-being 
(i.e., life satisfaction) (WB30) and hedaimonic well-being (i.e., engagement marked by 
emotional well-being) (WB26). Second, career motivation describes a software engi-
neers’ career-related factors, such as development experience (WB35), deadline orienta-
tion (WB33), career orientation (WB13), eagerness to help colleague and advance one’s 
career (WB17). Finally, personality encompasses various individual differences, such as 
age, emotional intelligence, and three traits of big five personality model: agreeableness 
extroversion, and neuroticism. It is important to note different studies report contradictory 
results regarding the relationship between extroversion and well-being: extroversion has a 
positive impact on life satisfaction but can have both a positive and negative impact on job 
satisfaction (see Fig. 4 ).
Organizational factors related to the job, team, and workplace are software engineers’ 
external or sociotechnical characteristics associated with their work environment. Software 
engineers are exposed to these factors at their jobs, teams, and organizations. First, job 
predictors include several dimensions from the Job Diagnostic Survey: job feedback, job 
significance, skill variety, and task identity (Hackman and Oldham 1974). The remain-
ing characteristics describe circumstances that improve well-being, such as leader-mem-
ber exchange (WB35), agile software development training (WB35), and music listening 
(WB29), and those detrimental to well-being, including complex projects and volatile 
requirements (WB35), time demands of work (WB19), and job burnout (WB27). Second, 
team predictors describe software engineers’ experiences associated with team dynamics 
and communication. These include team size (WB35), teamwork quality (WB10), psycho-
logical safety and team norm clarity (WB03), and two forms of conflict judgment, i.e., 
task-targeted and person-targeted (WB28). Finally, workplace predictors include flexible 
work arrangements with the ability to work from home (WB15, WB39), home office ergo-
nomics (WB26), employability culture (WB38) and onboarding success (WB25), in addi-
tion to workplace characteristics encompassing job security, information sharing, trust in 
senior management, and structural fairness (WB16).
Proposition 3: The software engineers well-being predicts the functioning of software 
engineers (i.e., health and career outcomes) and their organizations (i.e., job, team, and 
workplace outcomes)
This model has two outcome groups: individual and organizational. Individual outcomes 
include career outcomes and health outcomes. Career outcomes describe subjective factors 
that are associated with the career success of software engineers, such as self-assessed per -
formance and self-assessed productivity. Health outcomes are subjective factors that are 
associated with the physical and psychological functioning of software engineers. Some

of these dimensions are found in the definition of health provided by the World Health 
Organization6 (1948): “a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not 
merely the absence of disease or infirmity”. However, our model distinguishes between 
individual health and individual well-being. In the model, individual health is defined as 
the absence of negative mental, physical, and social states, while individual well-being 
indicates the presence of positive states, that is, hedonic, eudaimonic, and integrated or 
hedaimonic well-being.
In addition to the success and functioning of individual software engineers, a technol-
ogy organization is considered successful when software engineers contribute positively 
to certain job and team outcomes. Job outcomes are objective factors associated with the 
work-related success of software engineers. These include various indicators of supervisor-
assessed performance, such as job performance, innovative behavior, and software func-
tionality. Team outcomes are objective factors associated with the work-related success of 
software engineering teams. These include supervisor-assessed team performance, team’s 
emotional capabilities, and teamwork quality.
4.3.3  Scope of the Theory
Theories typically have a limited scope or conditions and circumstances, under which the 
theory propositions are valid (Sjøberg 2008; Stol and Fitzgerald 2015). Our theory is appli-
cable to the well-being of professional software engineers operating in the social context of 
software engineering teams and technology organizations.
Our theory is constructed using empirical evidence from studying diverse software 
engineering roles, including software developers, analysts, product/project managers, test-
ers, designers, people managers, and other technical professionals that were conceived as 
software engineers by other researchers. For this reason, the actual relationships between 
different predictors and outcomes may vary for different roles, genders, professional or 
individual identities, and organizational contexts. The latter is due to the fact that well-
being may be influenced by the culture and values adopted in an organization, which are 
indirectly influenced also by the national contexts. Notably, unlike many other studies 
biased towards empirical research conducted in the Western societies, the evidence behind 
our theory comes from diverse nationalities. The evidence includes the following coun-
tries with the cumulative highest number of study participants from all around the world, 
including India (n = 4,697); Germany (n= 1,918); the US (n = 1,268); South Africa (n 
= 924); China (n = 637); Brazil (n = 470); Russia (n = 464); Pakistan (n = 393); Turkey (n 
= 348); Sri Lanka (n = 232); Italy (n = 189); and Sweden (n = 165).
Furthermore, contrary to many other theories of software engineers’ well-being, our 
theory goes beyond the professional and organizational realm, which is crucial for con-
ceptualizing well-being as a multidimensional construct that includes physical, emo-
tional, cognitive, and social aspects of individual and collective functioning (Dodge 
2012; Lomas et al. 2014; Linton et al. 2016). Therefore, the insights gained from the 
theory can benefit both individual software engineers seeking to improve their well-
being and work performance as well as the managers and leaders of software engineer -
ing teams and organizations working towards establishing a healthy culture and behav -
iors for their employees.

5  Discussion
Our systematic literature review results provide a fertile ground for a more nuanced and up-
to-date conceptualization of software engineers’ well-being (Singh and Junnarkar 2016). 
The developed multidimensional theory of software engineers’ well-being incorporates the 
existing unidimensional models and theories.
In the following, we discuss the emerging model and how it relates to the existing rel-
evant models.
5.1  Well‑Being as a Complex Multi‑Dimensional Construct
The interest in well-being as a core concept in organizational psychology in general and 
in various professional fields in particular is growing. This is evident in the increasing 
number of empirical studies on well-being of software engineers included in our literature 
review. Yet, the results of our literature review once again emphasize the elusive nature of 
well-being as a complex, multi-faceted construct that is hard to define and measure. Our 
mapping of the studied concepts confirms the prevalence of unidimensional constructs and 
a lack of multidimensional conceptualizations of well-being in empirical research in soft-
ware engineering.
Our literature review is not the first review in the field. However, similarly to empiri-
cal studies, the majority of existing literature reviews focus on unidimensional states of 
well-being, such as emotions (Sánchez-Gordón and Colomo-Palacios 2019) and motivation 
(Beecham et al. 2008; França et al. 2011), or negative markers of well-being, such as stress 
(Maudgalya et al. 2006; Suárez and Vizcaíno 2023), time pressure (Kuutila et al. 2020), 
and cognitive load (Gonçales et al. 2021). In contrast, our review relied on and aggregated 
the results from a much more diverse set of quantitative studies that focused not only on 
software engineers’ hedonic well-being (i.e., mainly satisfaction, less so affect) but also 
on eudaimonic well-being (i.e., experiences of autonomy, competence, relationships, and 
meaning and impact) and integrated or hedaimonic well-being (i.e., engagement and flow).
The most related to our work is the literature review by Salas-Vallina et al. (2018), who 
analyzed studies on knowledge workers’ well-being from a multidimensional perspective, 
including hedonic well-being (e.g., job satisfaction and subjective well-being), eudaimonic 
well-being (e.g., psychological well-being and self-determination), and integrated well-
being (i.e., engagement and flow), albeit the review was semi-systematic. Specifically, 
Salas-Vallina et al. (2018) analyzed a diverse number of categories and well-being indica-
tors related to our own. For example, the authors discuss contextual performance marked 
by civic and proactive behaviors. We find contextual performance equally important, as it 
is related to eudaimonic well-being, i.e., one of the three categories of our model described 
as valued and growth-oriented living (Ryan and Martela 2016; Bartels et al. 2019; Unanue 
2021).
Contrary to our review, Salas-Vallina et al. (2018) used somewhat different categoriza-
tion of predictors and outcomes, which can be caused by the differences in the data used 
for abstraction and conceptualization. Another conceptual difference between Salas-Vallina 
et al. (2018) and our review is that the authors conceptualized psychological capital and 
commitment as well-being indicators. In our view, dimensions of psychological capital, 
such as optimism, efficacy, resilience, and hope, are better understood not as indicators of 
well-being but personal resources (in our model, individual factors) that contribute to the 
well-being and performance of employees (Luthans et al. 2007; Grover et al. 2018; Wu and 

--- Page 24 ---
 Empirical Software Engineering (2025) 30:35
 35 Page 24 of 42
Nguyen 2019). Also, we argue that commitment (an indicator of well-being in Salas-Val-
lina et al. 2018) is similar to continuance intention but the opposite of turnover intention 
(outcomes of well-being in our review).
5.2  Relationship with Related Models and Theories of Well‑Being
In the following, we compare our work to existing models and theories in the software engi-
neering literature (Beecham et al. 2008; Sharp et al. 2009; Graziotin 2016; Baltes and Diehl 
2018; Storey et al. 2019; Alsunki et al. 2020). Notably, some authors of relevant models and 
theories do not necessarily refer to the studied concepts as hedonic, eudaimonic, and inte-
grated/hedaimonic, or associate their studies with the boarder concept of well-being explic-
itly. Thus, in some cases, we adapted the models and theories according to our classification.
The results are summarized in Table 9.
5.2.1  Related Theories and Models of Hedonic Well‑Being
Hedonic well-being emerged as the most extensively studied tradition of software engi-
neers’ well-being. Models and theories in software engineering focused on at least three 
markers of hedonic well-being: motivation (Beecham et al. 2008; Sharp et al. 2009), affect 
(Graziotin et al. 2015a, b; Graziotin 2016), and job satisfaction (Storey et al. 2019).
First, the Model of Motivation in Software Engineering was developed based on a sys-
tematic literature review by Beecham et al. (2008) and refined in the Model of Motivators, 
Outcomes, Characteristics and Context by Sharp et al. (2009). Both models focus on soft-
ware engineers’ motivators, and, like many other models of motivation, emphasize various 
organizational factors, such as autonomy, control, equity and career paths, and important 
outcomes, such as retention and productivity. The main advancement in these models is 
related to attention to the software engineers’ personal characteristics (e.g., personality) 
and context (e.g., job characteristics).
Second, the Explanatory Theory of the Impact of Affects on Programming Performance 
proposed by Graziotin et  al. (2015a, b) is based on deep observations and interviewing 
of two computer science students. The theory suggests that programmers’ happiness is 
affected by the events they experience in their personal and professional lives, including 
private events (e.g., memories, reflections, and realizations) and public ones (e.g., situ-
ations observable from the third person). Depending on the situation, events can trigger 
either positive or negative affect (Graziotin et al. 2015a, b; Graziotin 2016). If program-
mers experience higher positive and lower negative affect, they focus better on task and 
project goals (associated with higher levels of flow), consequently increasing their perfor -
mance. In other words, the model shows that hedonic well-being (positive affect) leads to 
hedaimonic well-being (flow). Noteworthy, the authors differentiate affect from satisfaction 
and do not consider affect nor flow as forms of well-being (Graziotin 2016).
Finally, the Theory of Software Developer Job Satisfaction and Perceived Productiv -
ity developed by Storey et al. (2019) is based on productivity-related literature and a 
survey. The theory promotes the social and technical factors, challenges, and work con-
text variables that influence the relationship between job satisfaction and perceived pro-
ductivity. Contrary to our review that considers job satisfaction an indicator of hedonic 
well-being or hedonic happiness, the authors argue that happiness is a broader concept 
than job satisfaction. That said, just because hedonic well-being is a broader category

Evidently, there are many commonalities across the theories and models focusing 
on hedonic aspects of software engineers’ well-being. First, most models study various 
predictors of hedonic well-being. Second, the majority of them focus on very narrow 
aspects of well-being. Finally, some models are generic and identify categories of con-
founding factors, while others are detailed and link specific factors with the impacted 
aspects of well-being. In our work, we depart from the mentioned models that focus on 
hedonic well-being in several ways (see Table  10 for a summary of differences and rel-
evant practical implications).
First, our theory does not speculate about the exact relationships between individual 
and organizational factors. What it does suggest is that software engineers’ well-being is 
affected by various individual and organizational factors, regardless of their exact rela-
tionships. For example, our model shows that higher levels of task or process conflict 
are associated with decreases in hedonic well-being (i.e., affect and satisfaction), which 
is confirmed by independent studies (Acuña et al. 2009; Kuriakose et al. 2019). How -
ever, the model does not speculate whether the conflict is due to a toxic team culture or 
a higher number of disagreeable people in the team. From a practical point of view, as 
long as any of the individual or organizational factors is addressed (e.g., whether it is 
by improving the communication in the team or encouraging individuals to seek profes-
sional support, such as counseling or coaching), the levels of software engineers’ well-
being should go up. Nevertheless, all theoretical models (including ours) are hypotheti-
cal, unless their effectiveness is verified in diverse organizational settings.

1. It is very difficult to judge whether an experience is “positive” or “negative” using only 
cross-sectional data, that is, observations collected at one point in time. According to 
positive psychology 2.0 or the second wave of positive psychology, certain experiences 
may seem stressful or frustrating today (e.g., a very challenging task or project) but, 
upon reflection, turn out to be great sources of growth and meaning in the future (e.g.,

expensive and, therefore, not very common.

or pleasure open witnessing the misfortune of another person), can be experienced as 
both positive and negative affect (Berrios 2017a, b, 2018). For example, a cyberattack 
on company’s core infrastructure can induce stress and anxiety, but also act as a flow-
inducing experience and induce a sense of camaraderie.
Third, in our conceptualization of hedonic well-being, we do not consider satisfac-
tion and affect as theoretically different or distant concepts, as suggested by Graziotin 
et al. (2015a, b). On the one hand, both terms refer to slightly different psychological 
phenomena. For example, affect can be defined as “a neurophysiological state that is 
consciously accessible as a simple, non-reflective feeling that is an integral blend of 
hedonic (valence) and arousal values” (Russel 2003, p. 147), whereas satisfaction is 
defined as a “positive (or negative) evaluative judgment one makes about one’s job or 
job situation” (Weiss 2002, p. 175). In less technical terms, both terms constitute sub-
jective experiences, whereby affect is more felt and “in our body” and satisfaction is 
more rationalized and “in our head.”
On the other hand, when subjects are asked to rate their emotions and moods in inter -
views or questionnaires, they automatically engage in various cognitive and neurolin-
guistic processes that constitute evaluation, including attention (e.g., focusing on the 
question), comprehension (e.g., understanding the question), memory (e.g., remember -
ing situations when a particular affective concept was felt), awareness (e.g., becoming 
aware of one’s recalled affective state), and production (e.g., producing an answer based 
on the recall and awareness). For this reason, when employees are surveyed about their 
feelings, they cannot but think about their affective states, which naturally changes or 
distorts their embodied phenomenological experience, especially when someone lacks 
self-awareness and emotional intelligence. Unless affect is measured by physiological/
behavioral measures or sentiment analysis (i.e., instruments that do not require con-

the Theory of Software Developer Job Satisfaction, and Perceived Productivity devel-
oped by Storey, in which satisfaction is defined as cognitive and/or affective evaluation 
of one’s job (Wright and Cropanzano 2000). Their definition is similar to our concep-
tualization of subjective well-being marked by two evaluative components: cognitive

perspective. This is also supported by related research (Fagerholm and Munch 2012). 
According to Fagerholm and Munch (2012), if we want to understand software engi-

(i.e., cognition), feel (i.e., affect), and want (i.e., conation). Therefore, affect (i.e., the 
affective element of hedonic well-being) and cognition (i.e., the cognitive element of 
hedonic well-being) cannot address the whole picture of well-being, which is marked 
by various factors ranging from motivation and engagement to self-determination and 
meaning (Bakker, and Demerouti 2007; Oades and Dulagil 2016; Lysova et al. 2019; der 
Kinderen and Khapova 2020). Speaking practically, a nuanced understanding of soft-
ware engineers’ experiences entails a combination of approaches ranging from quantita-
tive research and sentiment analysis (e.g., judging states from text or work outputs) to 
human-to-human conversations: authentic, honest, and confidential.
5.2.2  Related Theories and Models of Eudaimonic Well‑Being
Eudaimonic well-being emerged as the second most prominent form of software engineers’ 
well-being.
It can be argued that software engineers lead an eudaimonic life, especially in the Aris-
totelian sense of a “good life”, when they behave responsibly (e.g., ensure systems security, 
avoid code plagiarism) and care about sustainability (e.g., think about the consequences 
of their actions on the users and the environment) (Sojer et al. 2014; Rahanu et al. 2019; 
Schieferdecker 2019; Nazir et  al. 2020). However, it is likely that due to the complex 
nature of the job, software engineers are deemed to demonstrate unethical and irrespon-
sible behavior, which begs the question of whether an Aristotelian-like eudaimonia can be 
achieved in the modern workplace (Stieb 2008). Nevertheless, we managed to find several 
models that focus on various indicators of eudaimonic well-being (Baltes and Diehl 2018; 
Malik et al. 2021), as defined by our theory: autonomy, competence, relationships, in addi-
tion to meaning and impact.
First, the Theory of Software Development Expertise developed by Baltes & Diehl 
(2018) is grounded in related research and empirical data from a mixed-methods survey 
with software developers. The theory focuses on software developers’ expertise (the need 
for competence in our model), which is said to be affected by various individual factors 
(including motivation, and personality) and organizational factors (including task or work 
context). In other words, software engineers may experience eudaimonic well-being when 
their technical expertise and soft skills contribute to their job performance (Baltes and 
Diehl 2018). However, it is important to note that software engineers are more empowered 
to use their expertise and skills when the needs for autonomy or freedom are met (Baltes 
and Diehl 2018; Matturro et al. 2019).
Second, the Conceptual Framework of Psychological Empowerment developed as a 
research model by Malik et al. (2021) draws on related literature and an empirical survey 
of agile project managers and team members and conceptualizes agile practices as a source 
of intrinsic motivation that enables delivery of innovative outcomes. Their work demon-
strated that various team-related factors, such as team communication (associated with 
team predictors in our model), positively impact eudaimonic well-being or psychological

empowerment marked by competence, impact, meaning, and self-determination. However, 
we excluded the model from Table  9 where we compare theoretical models and theories 
only.
As a final note, several models operationalize indicators of eudaimonic well-being as 
organizational factors that positively impact hedonic well-being (Beecham et  al. 2008; 
Sharp et al. 2009). In our model, eudaimonic well-being also positively impacts hedonic 
well-being. Therefore, eudaimonic well-being can also be considered a predictor to 
hedonic well-being, as demonstrated by other quantitative studies (Diedericks and Roth-
mann 2014; Huta 2015; Joshanloo 2019). However, in our theory, we differentiate between 
the experiences of eudaimonic well-being (e.g., autonomy and competence) and techniques 
that contribute to eudaimonic well-being (e.g., less micromanagement and opportunities 
to demonstrate and grow skills and expertise), which, we argue, are better considered as 
predictors of well-being.
Practical recommendations  In order to encourage software engineers to behave ethically 
and take personal responsibility for their behavior at work, avoid management practices 
that induce extrinsic motivation, such as corrective feedback, micromanagement, strict 
deadlines, and other forms of managerial control. Rather, prioritize practices that induce 
intrinsic motivation (marked by experiences of autonomy, competence, relationships, 
meaning and impact) and, consequently, increase job satisfaction.
5.2.3  Related Theories and Models of Integrated or Hedaimonic Well‑Being
Integrated or hedaimonic well-being is a neologism that we created to define a research 
tradition that studies indicators of well-being containing both hedonic (e.g., I am enjoying 
what I do) and eudaimonic qualities (e.g., I am growing in what I do) (Tulip et al. 2020). 
In this tradition, we include flow and engagement (a broader category encompassing emo-
tional well-being and work engagement). Our review demonstrated that flow and engage-
ment were the most under-researched aspects of software engineers’ well-being. That said, 
we managed to find several theoretical models engaging with concepts related to hedai-
monic well-being.
For example, the Explanatory Theory of the Impact of Affects on Programming Per -
formance states that hedonic well-being marked by positive affect increases performance 
when software engineers are focused (Graziotin et al. 2015a, b; Graziotin 2016). Grazio-
tin (2016) connect focus to Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi’s concept of flow, defining it as the 
“attention state of progressing and concentration” (p. 133). However, the authors do not 
specifically conceptualize flow as an indicator of well-being nor suggest that focus and 
flow are the same experiences.
Furthermore, the Framework of Software Developers Engagement Antecedents and 
Productivity developed by Alsunki et al. (2020), is based on the theory of workplace and 
human engagement, adopted for software engineers. The authors explain that software 
engineers’ engagement is affected by four groups of variables: personal characteristics 
(associated with individual predictors in our model), group characteristics (associated with 
team predictors in our model), process/project (associated with job predictors), organiza-
tional factors (associated with work predictors in our model), and development environ-
ment (associated with eudaimonic well-being marked by competence). Similarly, Pratt 
et al. show that curiosity (an indicator of software engineers’ personality) and goal clarity

(an indicator of software engineers’ career motivation) are associated with higher levels of 
flow marked by enjoyment (Pratt et al. 2016).
Practical recommendations  First, in order to improve focus and performance, make sure 
to provide software engineering with flow-inducing tasks that are not too boring or too 
stressful enough, that is, slightly above their experience and capabilities. Also, make sure 
to get software engineers curious and excited about the product or vision of the organiza-
tion to increase enjoyment and passion at work.
5.3  Important Predictors of Software Engineers’ Well‑Being: Individual 
and Organizational Predictors
The software development literature contains a fair number of theoretical models that 
describe various predictors of software engineers’ well-being (Maudgalya et al. 2006; Bee-
cham et al. 2008; Graziotin 2016; Storey et al. 2019).
For example, the Explanatory Theory of the Impact of Affects on Programming Perfor -
mance describes two types of predictors: private events (i.e., memories, reflections, and 
realizations that cannot be observed from the third person) and public events (i.e., situations 
that can be observed from the third person) (Graziotin et al. 2015a, b; Graziotin 2016). In 
our model, private events are related to individual factors that describe the psychology of 
software engineers, including their personality, career motivation, and health, whereas pub-
lic events are related to organizational factors that describe software engineers’ relationship 
with their job, team, and workplace environment. However, our theory does not suggest 
that individual factors are solely private (i.e., never observable) or organizational factors 
are solely public (i.e., always observable). For example, individual factors can be inferred 
from software engineers’ output or observable behaviors (e.g., avoiding social situations, 
such as daily meetings or public speaking, can signify social anxiety, as demonstrated in 
Denizci Nazligul et al. 2019). Similarly, organizational factors can be inferred from soft-
ware engineers’ perceptions and attitudes to their job, team, and workplace.
Other models that discuss various predictors of software engineers’ well-being include 
the Theory of Software Developer Job Satisfaction and Perceived Productivity (Storey et al. 
2019) and the Model of Motivators, Outcomes, Characteristics and Context (Beecham et al. 
2008; Sharp et al. 2009). All of these models share certain similarities with our model. First, 
they focus on satisfaction. Second, they discuss a list of predictors, although using other 
umbrella terms, such as challenges and sociotechnical factors (Storey et al. 2019) and intrin-
sic and extrinsic factors (Beecham et al. 2008; Sharp et al. 2009). Finally, the models include 
a variety of predictors similar to our individual factors (see Table  9 for similarities of factors 
between our model and other theoretical models and theories). However, as noted earlier, 
most of these models study a selected group of predictors in isolation rather than holistically.
Practical Recommendations  First, while hiring, assess the personality traits and career 
motivation of the candidates. Specifically, pay attention to emotionally stable candidates 
who are motivated to help and advance their careers. Second, ensure that software engi-
neers engage in tasks that have fulfilling and satisfying characteristics. These are tasks 
that can be performed from start to finish (i.e., task identity), allowing to make a positive 
impact within or outside the organization (i.e., job significance). Third, ensure satisfying

workplace characteristics, including fair pay and rewards, job security, and flexibility, as 
well as cultivate trusting relationships and transparent information sharing.
5.4  Important Outcomes of Software Engineers’ Well‑Being: Individual 
and Organizational Outcomes
In our theory, we provide a more holistic view of the outcomes of well-being classified 
into individual outcomes (career and health characteristics) and organizational outcomes 
(job and team characteristics). Many models and studies verify the positive relationship 
between various forms of well-being and different individual and organizational outcomes.
Software engineers’ hedonic well-being (i.e., satisfaction and positive affect) contrib-
utes to better individual outcomes marked by career factors and organizational outcomes 
marked by job factors. In terms of career factors, these include higher levels of productivity 
(Storey et al. 2019) and goal achievement (Graziotin et al. 2015a, b; Graziotin 2016) and 
lower levels of absenteeism (Beecham et al. 2008; Sharp et al. 2009) and turnover intention 
(Sharp et al. 2009). Unfortunately, we could not find many studies that verify the predictive 
effect of software engineers’ well-being on health, which we suggest as a prospective direc-
tion for future research. In terms of job factors, these include faster delivery time (Beecham 
et al. 2008; Sharp et al. 2009), quality (Sharp et al. 2009), profitability (França et al. 2011), 
and project success (Beecham et al. 2008; Sharp et al. 2009).
When it comes to eudaimonic well-being, the Theory of Software Development Exper -
tise (Baltes and Diehl 2018) entails that software engineers are productive (e.g., commit 
frequently and resolve issues fast) and produce high-quality code (e.g., structured and 
maintainable) when their behavior is marked by general and task-specific knowledge and 
experience. As we already discussed, expertise is associated with eudaimonic well-being 
marked by competence. However, not only competence but many other aspects of eudai-
monic well-being can positively impact performance, as demonstrated by multiple reviews 
and meta-analytic studies (der Kinderen and Khapova 2020; van den Broeck et al. 2021; 
Good et  al. 2022). In terms of integrated or hedaimonic well-being, the Framework of 
Software Developers Engagement Antecedents and Productivity shows that engagement is 
associated with higher levels of productivity and performance (Alsunki et al. 2020).
Finally, while reviewing and comparing the selected studies with related studies, we 
noticed that not all models of software engineers’ well-being differentiate between indi-
vidual outcomes (i.e., subjective experiences that speak to the functioning and success of 
individual software engineers) and organizational outcomes (i.e., objective experiences that 
speak to the performance of software engineering teams and organizations). In our view, 
there are several reasons why, we believe, it is important to maintain a conceptual bound-
ary between the two. First, it helps practitioners to decide when to use individual interven-
tions like coaching and psychotherapy to target individual outcomes (e.g., career and health) 
as opposed to organizational interventions, such as team coaching and management/human 
resources consulting to target business outcomes. Second, from a software engineer’s point 
of view, the boundary also helps software engineers to realize that achieving individual out-
comes depends a lot on their personal actions and responsibility, whereas achieving organi-
zational outcomes depends both on them and the leaders of their organizations.
Practical recommendations  First, invest in your employees’ future health and perfor -
mance by providing opportunities for company-paid services, such mental health services

(e.g., psychotherapy, guided meditation subscriptions), sports and exercise (e.g., physi-
otherapy, gym subscriptions), and social physical activities (e.g., travelling or hiking 
together). Second, in order to improve team outcomes through eudaimonic well-being, help 
teams establish psychologically safe environments with clear norms, as well as rich and 
frequent communication. Also, support teams in resolving task and process conflicts.
6  Conclusions
In this paper, we presented our overview of research on well-being in software engineering based 
on a systematic literature review of carefully selected empirical studies employing quantitative 
analysis. We summarized the diverse measures of constructs and indicators of well-being, as 
well as its predictors and outcomes. Further, we proposed a Type III theory (a prediction-based 
theory) that describes what well-being is, how it occurs, and how it can be increased or decreased 
in practice.
6.1  Contributions to Research and Practice
Our review is the first literature review that (1) systematically evaluated the quantitative 
studies on software engineers’ well-being in terms of their focus, demography, methodol-
ogy, and quality; (2) provided a detailed, replicable, and quantitatively driven methodol-
ogy for the interdisciplinary and multidimensional study of software engineers’ well-being; 
(3) proposed a model and theory of the predictors and outcomes of software engineers 
well-being; and (4) confirmed the need for more quantitative studies in this field. The 
theory addresses the need for further conceptualization in the field of software engineer -
ing  (Stol and Fitzgerald 2015)  and provides a vocabulary and concept descriptions that 
can be reused by field researchers and harmonize future research studies focusing on well-
being (Sjøberg et al. 2008; Stol and Fitzgerald 2015). Furthermore, the study attempted to 
introduce the goals and trends of positive psychology to software engineering while raising 
awareness of software engineers’ suffering, which can be detrimental to their health and 
technology organizations.
The visual representation of the findings of our review in Fig.  4 and Fig.  5 can serve 
as a map of individual and organizational characteristics that predict well-being as well 
as its important outcomes, including higher levels of performance and productivity and 
lower levels of ill-health and turnover intention. Leaders can navigate this aggregated 
view to answer such questions as who to employ, what type of work to offer employees, 
how to coach and support teams, and how to organize the workplace.
6.2  Limitations
There are several limitations associated with the methodology of the systematic literature 
review.
1. The search strategy was based only on an automatic search to increase the study’s 
replicability and reduce bias. At the same time, we might have omitted the most recent 
studies published in 2024 because we prioritized reproducibility of the search process 
over completeness by including articles published until the end of 2023 (full year).

--- Page 34 ---
 Empirical Software Engineering (2025) 30:35
 35 Page 34 of 42
2. Due to the high number of constructs and indicators of well-being, the review did not 
exhaust the number of terms that might be included in the search string.
3. Due to the study’s focus on quantitative survey studies, qualitative or information extrac-
tion studies were not reviewed.
4. Because the study was initially conducted as a master’s thesis, the data selection and 
quality assessment were conducted mostly by one reviewer, i.e., the paper’s first author. 
However, the potential junior researcher bias in including/excluding the papers was 
assessed on a sample of a hundred papers, which was reviewed by the junior and a senior 
researcher and resulted in a full agreement.
5. Most selected studies used cross-sectional design and regression methods that do not 
offer information on causality between variables.
Notably, most of these limitations are caused by pragmatic reasons; otherwise, it would 
have been almost impossible to conduct the systematic literature review within reasonable 
time constraints. Therefore, replications of this study are recommended to diversify the 
search methods and search terms, and include studies of other methods.
The resulting theory of software engineers’ well-being is also subject to several validity 
threats.
With respect to internal validity, we make no claims that the identified lists of predictors 
and antecedents of well-being of software engineers are exhaustive. In fact, the theory shall 
be viewed as a starting point that maps the specific measures of well-being from diverse 
studies in a multidimensional model. It identifies some studies and helps to evaluate the 
gaps that shall be further extended through future empirical studies. The resulting concep-
tualization of well-being (Fig.  6) is a holistic view of the theory. The identified categories 
of factors that explain well-being of software engineers overlap, extend and in some cases 
differ from a variety of existing models (see an overview in Table  9), because constructing 
theories is not a linear or sequential process with predictable outcome (Stol and Fitzgerald 
2015). Also, different authors may use different grounds for conceptualization.
With respect to external validity, it is not clear whether the theory has a high predictive 
power in all organizational settings and for all software engineering roles. In fact, some 
primary studies included in our literature review suggest that context and even roles matter. 
For example, the authors of WB20 found conflicting evidence with respect to the impact of 
personality traits on well-being dependent on the role. Specifically, extroversion in system 
engineers has a positive impact on their job satisfaction, while in programmers the rela-
tionship between extroversion on job satisfaction is negative. For this reason, the impact 
of personality can differ from role to role and from organization to organization. Neverthe-
less, the building blocks of our theory are based on the self-reported experiences of 16,086 
software engineering professionals from at least 42 countries. Therefore, we consider our 
theory more generalizable to the diverse population of software engineers than any exist-
ing theory or model developed using unidimensional measures of well-being and/or purely 
qualitative data.
6.3  Future Research Directions
We found that, despite the valuable work in the area of software engineers’ well-being, the 
advances are fairly disparate and disjointed, as well as biased toward hedonic constructs, 
such as the absence of negative affectivity or presence of positive affectivity, as dem-
onstrated by some of the reviews performed in software engineering research. With our 

--- Page 35 ---
Empirical Software Engineering (2025) 30:35 
 Page 35 of 42 35
model and theory, we attempt to inform future research about the multidisciplinary nature 
of well-being and the interplay between its three core components: hedonic, eudaimonic, 
and integrated or hedaimonic well-being.
Notably, many existing studies that we reviewed do not specifically use well-being as 
the core phenomenon under study; similarly, different umbrella terms are used to refer to 
the predictors of well-being. One reason for this is the lack of reference vocabulary, which 
we hope we have addressed in our study. Another reason might be that studies of well-
being in software engineering are conducted by software engineering researchers without 
deep knowledge and expertise in psychology. Our research team also faced challenges in 
developing a cross-disciplinary dialogue, which, we believe, was beneficial by contributing 
to mutual compassion and learning. We, therefore, support the emphasis on increasing col-
laboration between software engineering researchers and social scientists or psychologists 
as also pointed out by other scholars (Lenberg et al. 2015; Radant et al. 2016).
Finally, while our theory establishes the relationships between the indicators, predic-
tors, and outcomes of well-being, further research is needed to understand the specific 
nuances of these relationships. Although we hypothesize that well-being could either medi-
ate or moderate the relationship between various predictors and outcomes, it is also fair to 
assume that certain types of well-being could function as moderators (e.g., satisfaction), 
whereas others as mediators (e.g., eudaimonic and hedaimonic well-being). Further work 
shall focus on cross-disciplinary efforts to test the theory and its propositions in industrial 
and professional settings. Such efforts would not only deepen our understanding of the pre-
dictive and casual power of well-being in software engineering but also enrich and add 
more nuance to the current theory.
