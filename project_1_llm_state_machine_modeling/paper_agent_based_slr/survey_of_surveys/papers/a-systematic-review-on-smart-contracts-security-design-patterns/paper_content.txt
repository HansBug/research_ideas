A systematic review on smart contracts security design
patterns

Abstract
Smart contracts have accelerated the adoption of blockchain technology across various
domains by enabling coded agreements between transaction participants. However, increased
software defects and vulnerabilities in smart contracts, driven by developer inexperience with
languages like Solidity and a lack of effective detection tools, pose signiﬁcant risks. Given the
high value of assets managed on blockchain (e.g., cryptocurrencies), these vulnerabilities can
lead to severe consequences. Researchers and practitioners have proposed numerous smart
contract design patterns to mitigate certain faults or vulnerabilities. Despite these efforts, it
remains unclear which types of defects these patterns target and how effectively they address
the wide range of existing smart contract security vulnerabilities. In this paper, we review
the state of the art in smart contract design patterns, categorizing them and analyzing their
effectiveness in mitigating known security vulnerabilities. Our ﬁndings reveal that only ﬁve
patterns directly aim to prevent security vulnerabilities, collectively addressing just 6 out
of 94 security issues identiﬁed by OpenSCV (a state-of-the-art vulnerability taxonomy),
highlighting the need for further research on smart contract security design patterns.

1 Introduction
Blockchain systems are nowadays being used in various domains, including ﬁnance, supply-
chain management, and healthcare, to name a few. The increased adoption of this kind of
system is linked to the advances in smart contract technology, which has become a quite
popular way of developing blockchain applications. As in other types of software, and also
due to being a relatively recent way of developing programs, smart contracts are prone to
carry residual code defects, including software vulnerabilities (Vidal et al. 2024 ).
The presence of a security vulnerability in a smart contract has consequences that are
coupled with the domain of the system and the speciﬁcities of the technology. At ﬁrst, it
is very likely that the activation of a vulnerability brings in ﬁnancial costs due to the usual
context of the use of smart contracts (Dika and Nowostawski 2018 ). In addition to this,
in a blockchain environment, a fault or vulnerability detected in a smart contract cannot
be corrected. The contract must be terminated, and a new one must be deployed instead
(Chen et al. 2022 ), which is an additional cost (also reputational) for the application provider
and emphasizes the importance of following best practices and designs for smart contract
programming to prevent vulnerabilities (Singh et al. 2020 ).
Design patterns are established solutions for known problems, and developers are generally
aware that certain issues are better dealt with in the design phase of the software. Driven by
the increasing use of smart contracts and the identiﬁcation of known problems, including
the creation of insecure code, various types of design patterns started being proposed in the
literature (Wöhrer and Zdun 2018 ; Kannengießer et al. 2022 ; Liu et al. 2018 ). Despite the fact
that smart contract technology is relatively recent, there is already a relatively large number
of works proposing design patterns of various types, including several patterns that represent
the same concept but have different designations and patterns that, despite having the same
name, represent distinct concepts.
In this paper, we review the state of the art in smart contract design patterns with the
goal of consolidating names, pattern meanings, and types of patterns. We place special focus
on security design patterns and aim to determine how well these patterns (that represent
state-of-the-art design practices for creating secure smart contracts) can be mapped to the
avoidance of known vulnerabilities (identiﬁed in OpenSCV Vidal et al. 2024 ) and can, in
this sense, be used to avoid or minimize the risk of creating smart contracts holding security
vulnerabilities.
We identiﬁed 144 smart contract design patterns and categorized them into four main
groups: Control, Maintainability, Performance, and Security, offering a structured framework
for understanding critical aspects of smart contract development. We then speciﬁcally focused
on security design patterns, identifying 36 unique patterns. These patterns were categorized
into 10 subcategories based on three primary concerns: (i) the smart contract vulnerabilities
that can be mitigated by design patterns (according to OpenSCV classiﬁcation Vidal et al.
2024 ), (ii) security measures that can be implemented using these patterns (e.g., integrity),
and (iii) strategies for mitigating security risks through these design patterns.
Our results (available at Sadaf et al. 2024 ) reveal that the majority of patterns (51%) fall
under the Control category, which gathers patterns that mainly address core aspects related to
smart contract functionality. Security and Performance patterns are almost evenly distributed,
each representing about 24% and 20%, respectively, while Maintainability patterns are the
least common (5%). The largest subcategory within Performance is the Gas Consumption
subcategory, with 27 patterns emphasizing the importance of efﬁcient gas management.
Interaction patterns in the Control category are the next most signiﬁcant, followed by Access

2 Methodology
This section presents the procedure used to conduct the systematic literature review on smart
contract security design patterns, according to established guidelines for conducting system-
atic reviews (Kitchenham 2004 ). The procedure includes the following steps:
–Identiﬁcation and analysis of related surveys : The initial phase of this study involves
the identiﬁcation and analysis of secondary studies, including surveys, systematic
reviews, and mapping studies, that address the topic of smart contract security design
patterns. The goal is to identify any gaps or limitations in the existing literature in order
to address them in our own work.
–Deﬁnition of research questions : Building upon the gaps identiﬁed in the previous step,
we formulate a set of research questions that delineate the objectives of our systematic
review.
–Identiﬁcation of primary studies : To identify relevant primary studies that contribute
to the review, we determine the appropriate data sources (e.g., search engines) and deﬁne
a query composed of key terms that are relevant for answering the research questions.
–Study selection : In this step, we apply a set of inclusion and exclusion criteria to the
primary studies identiﬁed, as well as quality assessment criteria. As the search query
may return results that are not relevant to our goals, this step aims to reach a signiﬁcant
set of papers as a way to assure the validity of our study.
–Data extraction and synthesis : During this step, we extract and synthesize data from
the selected primary studies to allow answering research questions.
The following subsections describe each of the procedure’s steps in further detail and
provide an overview of the reviewing process’s outcome.

Six et al. ( 2022b ) built a taxonomy of 120 blockchain patterns that consist of two meta-
categories: “On-chain pattern” and “On/off-chain interaction pattern,” and 15 subcategories.
The On/off-chain interaction meta-category includes 86 patterns within subcategories such
as Data Exchange, Data Management, Wallet and Keys, and Transaction patterns, while the
On-chain meta-category includes 18 patterns within subcategories such as Domain-based
patterns, Smart Contract patterns, and Data Management patterns.
Among these subcategories, Smart contract patterns include 11 security patterns, designed
to address various aspects of smart contract security, such as access restriction, function
execution protection, and risk mitigation. Examples of security patterns included in this
subcategory are the Termination pattern, the Emergency Stop pattern, and the Check-Effects-
Interaction pattern.
The paper also emphasizes the importance of software patterns in ensuring the quality and
efﬁciency of decentralized applications. It suggests that further research could explore the
translation of traditional Gang of Four (GoF) patterns (Hunt and Hunt 2013 ) into blockchain
contexts and the development of architectural patterns and idioms for various blockchain
protocols.
However, the number of security patterns included in the paper is limited, and the paper
does not sufﬁciently address the dynamic nature of security threats in the blockchain domain.
As blockchain technology evolves, new vulnerabilities and attack vectors emerge, necessitat-
ing updates and adaptations to security patterns. The article also notes that the “Smart contract
pattern” subcategory is dedicated to patterns connecting to smart contract implementation
and security. However, our analysis reveals that security design patterns also exist in other
subcategories, such as “Data management” (e.g., Off-chain data storage, Off-chain Signa-
tures). This discrepancy reveals an oversight in the article, suggesting a broader distribution
of security design patterns across the taxonomy than initially presented.
Lu et al. ( 2022 ) reviews design patterns in blockchain-based payment applications. They
present 12 patterns that address essential aspects of state transitions of tokens in blockchain-
based payment applications. These patterns are categorized into two categories: token design

Kannengießer et al. ( 2022 ) conducted an analysis of challenges in smart contract develop-

including Ethereum, EOSIO, and Hyperledger Fabric. These solutions include 20 software
design patterns (SDPs) that aid developers, which are categorized into architectural patterns,
design patterns, and idioms. Among the 20 SDPs, some speciﬁcally address security issues,
such as the Checks-Effects-Interactions Pattern, which mitigates reentrancy attacks, a known
security vulnerability. They also established a taxonomy of challenges and solutions, offering
a structured approach to understanding and applying the SDPs. The main gap in the article
is the absence of a focused discussion on security concerns, such as potential vulnerabilities
and the design patterns that could safeguard against them. Furthermore, the lack of unifor-
mity in smart contract interfaces and the need for synchronization mechanisms to prevent
transaction-ordering vulnerabilities highlight the necessity for more standardized security
practices.
As blockchain continues to advance, new vulnerabilities emerge, requiring not only a
deeper understanding of these new vulnerabilities but also the development of security
patterns preventing them. Current literature, including the analyzed papers, often lacks a
dedicated focus on design patterns with security concerns. Additionally, the lack of a dedi-
cated taxonomy that focuses only on security design patterns for smart contracts would make
it challenging to identify the existing gaps and needs for new design patterns. Addressing
these gaps and providing a mapping between the common security issues in smart contracts
and corresponding design patterns to prevent them is essential not only for advancing the
theoretical framework of blockchain but also for ensuring its practical security in real-world
applications. Thus, in this study, we aim to systematically identify and review the existing
smart contract design patterns, analyze the security design patterns, and provide a mapping
between these patterns and the security vulnerabilities they address.
2.2 Research Questions
Based on the analysis of existing secondary studies, we deﬁned the following set of three
research questions that form the objectives of this systematic literature review:
–RQ-1 : What smart contract design patterns have been proposed in the literature, and
what are their goals and characteristics?
–RQ-2 : Which smart contract design patterns target security, and how do they avoid or
mitigate security threats?
–RQ-3 : Which smart contracts’ known vulnerabilities are directly addressed by the iden-
tiﬁed security design patterns?
RQ-1 aims to establish a catalog of smart contract design patterns, including patterns
belonging to different categories (e.g., performance, security) and with different goals (e.g.,
transaction management, gas consumption).

2.3 Study Identification and Selection
For the identiﬁcation of primary studies, we followed the same process as for secondary
studies and used the same query. The following criteria were explicitly deﬁned to minimize
the erroneous inclusion or exclusion of certain papers and also to foster the reproducibility
of the study selection process.
Inclusion criteria
– The work is centered around the proposal of smart contract design patterns.
– The work contributes to answering our research questions. In practice, one or more pat-
terns should be proposed, a ’security’ label should be included in one or more of the
patterns, and the pattern description should provide sufﬁcient detail to enable under-
standing and a possible connection to an existing vulnerability.
Exclusion criteria
– The paper lies outside the software engineering and blockchain domains.
– The paper has not been peer-reviewed or is not written in English.
As we can see in Fig. 1, our initial search resulted in 977 studies. We began by removing
duplicates, which reduced the papers to 917. To apply the inclusion and exclusion criteria ,w e
then went through three pruning steps, which respectively targeted the title, abstract, and full
text of each study. At each step, the goal is to understand if there is reasonable evidence that
we may ﬁnd descriptions of smart contract security design patterns in the paper. At the title
screening level, we search for such evidence in a relaxed manner, as many times, the titles do
not accurately represent the content of the paper, and we are not very interested in discarding
papers at this step, even if it involves additional work in the following step. At the abstract
screening level, we search for reasonable evidence that the paper will ﬁt our criteria (again,
at this stage, it may not be possible to understand if a given paper is relevant or not just by
examining the abstract). At the full-text screening, we determine if the paper complies with
the criteria in a strict way. This means that the paper must list and describe smart contract
security design patterns. The pattern descriptions must allow us to understand fundamental
aspects, namely how the use of the patterns avoids or mitigates security threats (and what
these threats are). In the end, if the paper complies with these criteria, it is considered part
of our dataset; if it does not comply, it is excluded from the dataset.
The paper screening phase involves some subjectiveness, which should be reduced by
adhering to the practices speciﬁed by the systematic literature review process (Kitchenham
2004 ). In our case, we took additional measures during the screening phase. Speciﬁcally,
a team of three researchers was involved: two early-stage researchers were responsible for
analyzing and labeling the papers at each screening step, and an experienced researcher was
responsible for breaking ties and overseeing the whole screening process.

2.4 Data Extraction and Synthesis
We identiﬁed the following three aspects as key ground data to extract from the studies,
namely the design pattern name, the problem or challenge targeted by the pattern, and a textual
description of the solution to the problem. We also collected the platform and the language
to which each pattern applies. However, in a preliminary pass, we found this information to
be very scarce, and therefore, we discarded it. Based on the collected ground data, we then
proceeded to synthesize information necessary to answer our research questions and began
by consolidating pattern names.
This consolidation generally consisted of identifying patterns that are essentially the same
but have been proposed in the literature with different names. In general, we follow one of

the following three cases: i) We use the original name if it appears in only one paper or if
it is used consistently in multiple papers; ii) We choose the most widely used name when
several alternatives exist (provided one name is used more frequently); and iii) We assign a
new name when name heterogeneity is high. When assigning a new name, we select one that
is clearer, occasionally making adjustments for clarity or simplicity. The mapping between
proposed names in the literature and our new or selected names can be found at Sadaf et al.
(2024 ).
This process was also carried out by two early-stage researchers, who split the work. In
practice, generally accepted terms (or the majority present in the papers) were selected. The
outcome was reviewed by an experienced researcher.
Starting from the different patterns, we then created a structure, bottom-up, to represent the
various types of patterns found. Two early-stage researchers, by consensus, proposed a layer
of categories for the patterns, grouping them by similarity. These layers were then reviewed by
one experienced researcher. To capture the more general intent of the patterns, an additional
higher-level layer of categories was then proposed by consensus by the two early-stage
researchers. These were then reviewed and discussed with one experienced researcher. The
overall structure was reviewed and slightly adjusted by one additional experienced researcher.
By the end of this process, we had sufﬁcient information to answer RQ-1 and RQ-2, which,
in summary, aim at identifying general-purpose design patterns and security design patterns,
respectively.
The process continued and involved mapping to known smart contract security vulner-
abilities. The goal would be to understand how well current design patterns cover known
vulnerabilities. For this purpose, we resort to the information in OpenSCV (Vidal et al.
2024 ), which is a taxonomy of smart contract vulnerabilities that is more up-to-date and
more comprehensive than popular alternatives such as DASP (Wong and Hemmel 2018 )o r
SWC (Songsom et al. 2022 ), or even recent proposals such as Rameder et al. ( 2022 ).

3 Smart Contract Design Patterns
This section presents our ﬁndings with respect to the three research questions identiﬁed in
Section 2. Full details regarding the results are available at Sadaf et al. ( 2024 ). We begin
by presenting basic deﬁnitions regarding smart contracts, design patterns, and security. We
then, in the following subsections, focus on each of the three research questions.
Asmart contract is a digital agreement with its terms encoded directly into an immutable,
decentralized ledger (Khan et al. 2021 ). The presence of a software fault (i.e., a coding
mistake done by a developer) or a vulnerability (i.e., a software fault that can be exploited
from the outside to harm the system) in a smart contract has a high impact since the contract
itself cannot be corrected; it must be terminated, and a new one must be deployed (Sayeed
et al. 2020 ). Security is generally viewed by the composition of conﬁdentiality, integrity,
and availability and is quite an important property in blockchain environments (Zhang et al.
2019 ). This is due to the usual nature of the applications being deployed, which many times
are set in a ﬁnancial context (i.e., the presence of a vulnerability may lead to very high
ﬁnancial and reputation losses) (Dika and Nowostawski 2018 ).
Design patterns are solutions for common problems of various kinds. They can be seen
as best practice solutions for the identiﬁed problems or challenges and help in promoting
properties like code reusability or maintainability. They can also be seen as a means to avoid
some common mistakes made by developers (i.e., software faults), including vulnerabilities
(Wedyan and Abufakher 2020 ).

3.1 Design Patterns for Smart Contracts
We begin by addressing RQ-1 “What smart contract design patterns have been proposed
in the literature, and what are their goals and characteristics?” . During this survey, we
identiﬁed a total of 144 patterns. It is worth noting that, during our analysis of the literature,
we encountered certain design patterns, such as “update by delegates” and “one-off access”
(Liu et al. 2020b ), that were only mentioned by name without any accompanying details
or explanations. Consequently, due to the lack of substantive information, we have omitted
these patterns from our discussion in the article. The identiﬁed design patterns are then placed
bottom-up into 28 subcategories, which in turn ﬁt into four general categories according to
the objective of the design patterns.
–Control: includes design patterns centered on helping manage the control ﬂow, opera-
tional login, and functional aspects of smart contracts (Zheng et al. 2020 ; Wohrer and
Zdun 2018 ).
–Maintainability: dedicated to design patterns that aim at reaching longer-lasting con-
tracts and are either centered on facilitating contract upgrades or centered on reducing
code complexity (Samreen and Alalﬁ 2023 ; Kannengießer et al. 2022 ).
–Performance: design patterns that aid developers in creating smart contracts that are
efﬁcient in terms of storage and gas consumption, thus being centered on how storage
resources can be effectively used or on how gas is handled by the contract in a way to
avoid extraneous costs imposed by the contract execution (Marchesi et al. 2020 ).
–Security: includes design patterns that aim at avoiding the introduction of known vul-
nerabilities in the code or have a speciﬁc security concern as a goal (e.g., authentication,
authorization) (Wohrer and Zdun 2018 ; Dika and Nowostawski 2018 ).
Figure 2illustrates the distribution of identiﬁed design patterns across the four general cate-
gories. As shown, the majority of design patterns fall under the control category (51%), which
addresses functional and operational concerns. These concerns are primary in the develop-
ment of any software system, including smart contracts. The remaining design patterns are
roughly divided between security (24%) and performance (20%) patterns, highlighting the
equal importance of both aspects in smart contract development. Maintainability (5%) pat-
terns constitute the smallest category, reﬂecting the less frequent need for maintenance in
smart contracts due to the immutability of blockchain technology.

4 Smart Contract Security Design Patterns
This section presents a detailed view of the security design patterns identiﬁed in the literature.
The goal is to contribute to answering RQ-2: Which smart contract design patterns target
security, and how do they avoid or mitigate security threats? . It is worth mentioning that
the speciﬁcities of the blockchain context result in security vulnerabilities (e.g., Reentrancy
and Unsafe Non-Blockchain External Call Vidal et al. 2024 ) and, consequently, security
patterns (e.g., Secure ether transfer and Tokenized Exchange) that are distinct from other
systems (Vidal et al. 2024 ). In the following subsections, we go through each subcategory
and describe all security patterns in detail.
4.1 Secure External Calls
Secure External Calls is a subcategory of security patterns that includes a set of four patterns
found across 15 papers. This subcategory focuses on the security of either inter-contract
communication or interaction with entities external to the blockchain. In this subcategory,
we ﬁnd patterns that aim to avoid unsafe external calls (Vidal et al. 2024 ). The goals are
achieved by explicitly checking and ordering calls, concurrency mechanisms like mutexes and
decoupling, modifying transfer operations, and limiting external calls as a way of minimizing
risks. A well-known issue addressed by these patterns is the reentrancy vulnerability, which
occurs when a malicious contract, after initiating a call, is allowed to make new calls to
the vulnerable contract before the initial call has been completed. Due to this vulnerability,
unexpected state changes may occur, including the depletion of credit (Vidal et al. 2024 ).
Checks effects interaction This pattern (Rajasekar et al. 2020 ; Argañaraz et al. 2020 ) guides
smart contract function development and mitigates the risk of introducing reentrancy vulner-
abilities by enforcing the order in which operations are performed. A reentrancy vulnerability
exists when an external contract maliciously reenters the calling contract (i.e., the contract ini-
tially executing) during execution, potentially altering its state or control ﬂow in unintended
ways. This pattern speciﬁes that functions should ﬁrst check all preconditions to validate
inputs, ensure authorization, and verify the current state of the contract, protecting against
invalid operations. Next, functions should apply effects by securely updating their internal
123Page18of40 95

--- Page 19 ---
Empirical Software Engineering (2025) 30:95
state, such as adjusting balances or recording changes. Finally, the function should perform
interactions with external contracts, such as transferring Ether or invoking external functions,
only after ensuring that the contract’s state is fully updated and consistent. This order, along
with the fulﬁllment of the preconditions, prevents the external contract from re-entering and
altering the state of the calling contract during execution, enforcing the correct ﬂow (Wohrer
and Zdun 2018 ; Britten et al. 2021 ).
The importance of this pattern is quite evident as the corresponding vulnerability has
been exploited in historical incidents, such as the DAO hack, where the lack of proper order
enforcement in operations allowed an attacker to exploit the system state through recursive
calls, leading to $60 million worth of Ether at the time (Britten et al. 2021 ). By ensuring that
all debits and credits are recorded before any further transactions begin, the Checks Effects
Interaction pattern provides a defense against such vulnerabilities (Wohrer and Zdun 2018 ).
Mutex The main idea behind this pattern is to address the threat of reentrancy attacks by
employing a mechanism of mutual exclusion (i.e., a mutex). It ensures that a contract function
cannot be re-entered or exploited while still executing by utilizing a boolean variable as a
lock. This lock is activated before an external call is made and deactivated once the call is
completed, effectively aborting any reentrant calls attempting to execute during the locked
state (Wohrer and Zdun 2018 ). This pattern mirrors the traditional concept of mutexes, where
mutual exclusion restricts concurrent access to shared resources, thereby maintaining the
integrity of the contract’s state. This approach may help in scenarios where recursive calls
or callbacks could modify the state of the parent contract before the original function is
completed, a vulnerability exploited in reentrancy attacks (William 2008 ). As a security
measure, the Mutex pattern is a straightforward means to protect control ﬂow with minimal
negative consequences, such as increased gas costs, complexity, or development overhead.
Alternative strategies exist and can also be considered (Rajasekar et al. 2020 ).
Pull Payment This is an approach to mitigate the risks associated with direct fund transfers
in smart contracts, particularly the possibility of failure or vulnerability to reentrancy attacks
when a contract sends funds to another party. In traditional “push” payment models, the
sending contract initiates a transfer, which involves making an external call to the receiving
contract. Such calls inherently hand over control to the receiving contract, potentially allowing
malicious reentrant calls that could exploit incomplete state updates or disrupt the contract’s
execution (Wöhrer and Zdun 2018 ). To address these challenges, the Pull Payment pattern
decouples the transfer action from the contract’s core logic by enabling recipients to withdraw
funds at their convenience rather than the sender pushing the funds. This approach minimizes
the attack surface by isolating each external call and shifting the responsibility for initiating
withdrawals to the user, aiming to minimize scenarios where the contract might be forced
into unintended states due to failed operations or malicious interactions (Wöhrer and Zdun
2020 ; Hornyák and Alkhoury 2020 ).
This pattern may be helpful in scenarios such as micropayments or bidirectional payment
channels. For instance, on a blockchain-based crowdfunding platform, contributors’ funds
could be stored in a contract until the project creator withdraws them. This ensures that if
an external interaction fails, the funds remain safely within the contract, preventing loss or
unintended behavior.
Limit External Calls Refers to the practice of minimizing the frequency and/or total number
of calls to external smart contracts due to inherent costs and associated security risks. Each
call to an external contract introduces overhead in the form of gas fees and exposes the system
123Page19of4095

--- Page 20 ---
Empirical Software Engineering (2025) 30:95
to potential vulnerabilities, such as reentrancy attacks or unexpected execution failures. This
pattern advocates for the use of single, multi-purpose functions that can handle multiple
tasks or return various pieces of data in one call, rather than having multiple functions, each
performing a separate action or returning a single piece of data (Marchesi et al. 2020 ; Moura
2020 ). As an example, a decentralized ﬁnance (DeFi) platform might use this pattern to
minimize the number of external calls required to execute complex ﬁnancial transactions,
thereby reducing gas costs and potential attack vectors.
In summary, the implementation of the aforementioned set of design patterns improves
the security of smart contracts by establishing a framework for secure external calls. These
patterns collectively ensure that smart contract functions check preconditions and apply state
changes before making external calls, employ mutual exclusion to prevent reentrancy attacks,
decouple the action of fund transfers to mitigate risks, and minimize the attack surface by
reducing the number and frequency of external calls.
4.2 Secure Credit Transfer
Secure Credit Transfer refers to a subcategory that includes one design pattern discussed
in ﬁve of the reviewed works. The identiﬁed pattern concerns safeguarding transactions
involving ﬁnancial assets. In the context of smart contracts, the transfer of ﬁnancial assets
usually refers to the transfer of cryptocurrencies or tokenized assets between parties. The
following pattern was identiﬁed with this explicit goal in mind.
Secure ether transfer The Secure Ether Transfer pattern is designed to ensure safe Ether
transfer within the Ethereum blockchain, addressing the risks of excessive gas forwarding
and reentrancy attacks. It offers three methods for transferring Ether: send, transfer, and
call. Send and transfer are higher-level functions with built-in gas limits that protect against
malicious contract activity by restricting the amount of gas to be used, whereas call is a
low-level function that forwards all remaining gas, ensuring that the gas forwarded does not
exceed safe thresholds and preventing reentrancy attacks or other unintended behaviors that
could result from forwarding excessive gas to an invoked contract (Rajasekar et al. 2020 ;
Guida and Daniel 2019 ). As an example, in a smart contract used for crowdfunding, the
Secure Ether Transfer pattern ensures that refunds or payouts are executed without exposing
the contract to the aforementioned typical risks.
4.3 Secure Programming Practices
In this subcategory, we include patterns that aim at avoiding writing vulnerable code. We
identiﬁed a single pattern present in six of the analyzed works. This type of pattern may take
the form of adhering to a set of coding standards or using speciﬁc language mechanisms
(e.g., prepared statements for writing SQL). The main idea is that the resultant code either
avoids a certain vulnerability (e.g., SQL injection) or is less prone to hold vulnerabilities.
Secure programming practices take into account the features of the programming language
being used or speciﬁc security concerns of the operating environment, such as the Ethereum
Virtual Machine (EVM) for Ethereum smart contracts.
Randomness This pattern addresses the challenge of generating random numbers securely
within blockchain-based systems that operate in a deterministic environment, i.e., where the
output of a function must be the same for all nodes given the same input to maintain consensus

--- Page 21 ---
Empirical Software Engineering (2025) 30:95
(Bartoletti and Pompianu 2017 ; Argañaraz et al. 2020 ). This deterministic nature poses
obstacles to achieving true randomness, as predictable or insufﬁciently random values can
lead to security vulnerabilities that can be exploited to manipulate outcomes in applications
such as lotteries, games, and auctions (Lopez Marin 2022 ;C h e ne ta l . 2020 ).
To mitigate the insufﬁcient randomness risks, the Randomness pattern often relies on
oracles, which provide random values generated off-chain or on-chain techniques like lever-
aging block hashes from not-yet-mined blocks. For example, a lottery smart contract might
query an off-chain oracle service to ensure secure and unbiased randomness. Although on-
chain methods, such as using future block hashes, are more convenient, they are generally
considered less secure due to potential manipulation by miners or attackers with substantial
computational power (Chen et al. 2020 ). The implementation of the Randomness pattern
ensures that the generation of random numbers is reusable and modular (Lopez Marin 2022 ).
4.4 Flow Control
Design patterns that are categorized under Flow Control are found across three of the ana-
lyzed papers. These patterns aim to prevent an adversary from being able to manipulate the
execution ﬂow of the program and, consequently, the execution order of statements or state
transitions within a program. The goal is to ensure that the program behaves as intended,
even in the presence of unexpected or malicious inputs that could try to alter the program’s
ﬂow to exploit vulnerabilities. The following paragraphs present the patterns that ﬁt in this
subcategory.
Guard Check Regulates control ﬂow to enhance security by ensuring that all predeﬁned
conditions on the contract’s state and function inputs are veriﬁed before execution proceeds
so that expected behavior is assured. In practice, this allows the blocking of unauthorized
actions or reduces the likelihood of activating residual faults in the code with consequences
in the control ﬂow of the code (N’Da et al. 2020 ; Moura 2020 ).
Guard checks can include mechanisms such as user authentication, validation of input data,
or compliance with conditions that must hold for the function to execute correctly (N’Da
et al. 2020 ; Moura 2020 ). In practice, they can validate user inputs, verify the contract’s
state, enforce invariants, and ensure compliance with logical constraints. They may also
discard unacceptable conditions, thereby avoiding vulnerabilities or unintended outcomes.
For example, these checks could prevent an unauthorized user from accessing privileged
functions or stop a transaction if the contract state does not meet predeﬁned criteria. The
utility of this pattern extends to various scenarios, such as user authentication, data validation,
or verifying the integrity of responses from other contracts (V olland 2019 ).
Escrow The Escrow design pattern is focused on transaction management by mediating
the transfer of tokens between two parties through a neutral third-party contract called the
escrow. In this pattern, tokens are ﬁrst transferred to the escrow smart contract, which serves
as a trusted intermediary to hold the funds until predetermined payment conditions are met
(e.g., coupons, insurance payouts, social welfare beneﬁts) (Lu et al. 2022 ). This mechanism
ensures that the seller delivers the promised product or service and that the buyer completes
the payment accordingly, mitigating the risk of failure to comply with the agreement. The
escrow contract’s logic includes veriﬁcation steps to conﬁrm that all transaction requirements
have been fulﬁlled before releasing the funds to the seller. This pattern is helpful, especially
in high-value transactions where the impact of a failure or malicious behavior is high and,

as a consequence, the need to protect the interests of all parties is strong (Lu et al. 2022 ).
This pattern is helpful, especially in high-value transactions where the impact of a failure or
malicious behavior is high and, as a consequence, the need to protect the interests of all parties
is strong, such as insurance payouts, social welfare beneﬁts, or e-commerce settlements
(Goldfeder et al. 2017 ; Amani et al. 2018 ).
4.5 Safe Arithmetic
Safe Arithmetic patterns, found across four papers, refer to techniques that ensure arithmetic
operations are carried out with correctness and, as a consequence, do not compromise the
intended functionality (Hickey et al. 2001 ). In the particular case of smart contracts, there
are numerous cases of calculations resulting in either a number larger than the maximum
value the data type can hold (i.e., overﬂows) or a number that is smaller than the smallest
value the data type can represent (i.e., underﬂows) (Sun et al. 2022 ). Such cases have been
many times reported as security vulnerabilities, allowing an attacker to manipulate balances
or other important state variables (Sayeed et al. 2020 ;C h e ne ta l . 2020 ). Safe arithmetic is
essential to avoid this kind of issue.
SafeMath This pattern, which is available in a few libraries (Chen et al. 2020 ), allows
avoiding overﬂow or underﬂow vulnerabilities. Overﬂow occurs when an arithmetic operation
exceeds the maximum value of a data type, while underﬂow arises when an operation results in
a value below its minimum range (Vidal et al. 2024 ). Such vulnerabilities have historically led
to severe exploits, as demonstrated in the attack on BEC tokens, where improper validation
allowed unauthorized manipulation of balances and state variables (Tan et al. 2022 ). The
main idea is to provide safe versions of the four arithmetic operations by including checks
against problematic outcomes. Whenever a calculation reaches an overﬂow or underﬂow
state, SafeMath reverts the transaction to ensure the integrity of the state of the contract
(Moura 2020 ; Bartoletti and Pompianu 2017 ). While this approach incurs some runtime
overhead and gas costs, the beneﬁts of preventing critical arithmetic vulnerabilities outweigh
the costs (Ren et al. 2021 ; Bartoletti and Pompianu 2017 ).
4.6 Access Control
The Access Control subcategory includes a set of patterns, found across 30 of the analyzed
papers, that mostly target authentication and authorization, which are respectively related to
identity veriﬁcation and checking if a certain entity is allowed to execute a certain operation
(Samarati and De Vimercati 2000 ; Penelova 2021 ).
Authorization This pattern restricts the execution of certain functions that are sensitive or
somehow critical (e.g., sending ether, invoking the self-destruct() function (Mavridou and
Laszka 2018 )) to speciﬁc user addresses. It is commonly used to ensure that only authorized
users, such as the contract owner or those with speciﬁc roles, can invoke certain functions that
may alter the contract’s state. In practical terms, implementing this pattern typically involves
using access control lists or mappings of addresses to track permissions and incorporating
modiﬁers in the contract’s functions to check these permissions before allowing the execution
of the code (Bartoletti and Pompianu 2017 ; Argañaraz et al. 2020 ).
Real-world applications of the Authorization pattern are obviously widespread. For
instance, in notary contracts, the pattern may ensure that only the document owner can
add or modify data, thereby preventing external tampering. In gambling games, the pattern

may allow only the contract owner to manage participants’ fees and rewards, for instance,
ensuring that only the winner can withdraw their prize. Some other decentralized applica-
tions may also generally use this pattern to assign different roles to different user addresses
(Bartoletti and Pompianu 2017 ).
Time Constraint This pattern is used to deﬁne the time window during which certain actions
are allowed or to specify deadlines after which certain functions can no longer be used. In
practice, the developer controls the execution of contract functions by using timestamps or
block numbers as references for measuring time intervals and durations, thus adding temporal
logic to contract operations. These mechanisms will ensure that contract actions are bound
by the temporal rules encoded within the contract (Bartoletti and Pompianu 2017 ;X ue ta l .
2021 ). This pattern may be particularly useful in scenarios where the timing of actions is
critical, such as ﬁnancial transactions, identity veriﬁcation, and gaming events (Bartoletti
and Pompianu 2017 ). For example, a certiﬁcate holder might share a link to a veriﬁer that
grants access to credentials only within a deﬁned period, preventing access after the period
has expired (Xu et al. 2021 ; Hornyák and Alkhoury 2020 ).
Commit and Reveal This pattern consists of two phases: a commit phase and a reveal phase.
In the Commit Phase, a user submits a hashed version of its information to the blockchain,
keeping the original information secret for a period of time. In the Reveal Phase, the user
discloses the original information, and its integrity can be veriﬁed by the network by hashing
it again and comparing it to the initially committed hash (Wöhrer and Zdun 2018 ; Liu et al.
2018 ).
The commit-reveal scheme is crucial in some blockchain-based applications because it
ensures that no one can access the information before its original content is revealed, and it
prevents users from altering their submissions after committing them (Rajasekar et al. 2020 ).
For example, in a blockchain-based auction, bidders can commit their bids without revealing
the corresponding details to other participants until the auction ends. Once it ends, the details
will be veriﬁable. Thus, this pattern actually addresses both access control and integrity.
Ownership This is an access control pattern used in smart contract development to ensure
that critical functions are only callable by a speciﬁc, authorized entity - the owner of the
contract. The owner is set at the moment the contract is deployed, with the deployer’s address
being then registered as the owner and this address being used to verify authorization for
executing sensitive methods (Töberg et al. 2022 ; Zecchini et al. 2019 ; Six et al. 2022a ). For
example, maintenance tasks or functions that manage contract funds are typically restricted to
the owner to prevent potential misuse (Wöhrer and Zdun 2018 ,2020 ). Real-world examples
of the Ownership pattern include its use in decentralized ﬁnance platforms, where the contract
owner might need to update interest rates or manage liquidity pools.
Access Restriction The Access Restriction pattern ensures that certain functions can only
be executed when speciﬁc restrictive conditions are met. The pattern aims to control func-
tion access based on predeﬁned requirements, which can include temporal conditions, caller
information, transaction details, or other necessary prerequisites. These requirements are
often implemented using reusable modiﬁers that enforce conditions across multiple func-
tions within the same contract. Additionally, Access Restriction can extend permissions to
a broader set of users, such as those approved by other members, thus providing more ﬂex-
ible access control (Wöhrer and Zdun 2018 ; Hornyák and Alkhoury 2020 ). For example, in
a decentralized ﬁnance application, an Access Restriction pattern might be used to ensure

that only users who have staked a certain amount of tokens can access premium features or
execute high-value transactions (Wöhrer and Zdun 2018 ; Hornyák and Alkhoury 2020 ).
Blocklist The Blocklist pattern addresses the need to restrict access to certain entities regard-
ing an off-chain asset. This is achieved by implementing a contract that maintains a set of
Bloom ﬁlter coefﬁcients. These coefﬁcients enable efﬁcient off-chain computation, allowing
clients to determine if an entity is included in the blocklist without explicitly storing the
list within the contract. The main components of this pattern include a contract that holds
the Bloom ﬁlter coefﬁcients and the off-chain clients that utilize these coefﬁcients to per-
form the necessary calculations, ensuring that disallowed entities are blocked from accessing
the speciﬁed asset (Worley and Skjellum 2019 ). This pattern is useful in scenarios where
maintaining a comprehensive list of disallowed entities on-chain would be impractical due
to storage constraints (Wei et al. 2022 ). For example, a contract could use this pattern to
track certiﬁcate revocations or the Ethereum addresses of stolen cryptocurrency, ensuring
that these entities are blocked from accessing the speciﬁed asset.
Dynamic Binding The goal of this pattern is to authorize actions by participants whose
addresses are not known in advance. The pattern works through the association of an authority
with a contract action through an off-chain secret rather than predeﬁning the user’s address
within the contract. The secret, when provided by the user, is veriﬁed by comparing its hash
with a hash stored in the smart contract. This approach allows the smart contract to be updated
to include this veriﬁcation mechanism without needing a special protocol to exchange the
secret. A third-party contract can also facilitate the authorization by conﬁrming the secret
key and then allowing interaction with the target contract (Thirulokachander 2021 ).
Embedded Permission This pattern enforces access restrictions directly within the con-
tract’s code for selected functions. By specifying authorized addresses within the contract,
this pattern allows only those pre-approved participants to invoke critical functions, such as
self-destruct() in Solidity. It embeds a check within the function itself that veriﬁes whether the
caller’s address matches the list of authorized addresses before any function logic is executed
(Lu et al. 2018 ; Rajasekar et al. 2020 ). The presence of embedded permissions could help in
avoid the Parity multi-sig wallet incident, which ended up in freezing 500K Ether (Xu et al.
2018 ; Rajasekar et al. 2020 ).
Selective Content Generation This pattern enables the creation of customized credentials
tailored to a holder’s speciﬁc requirements regarding the credential contents. In this pattern,
an issuer generates a credential that includes only the necessary data as requested by the
holder, ensuring that only the essential information is shared with a veriﬁer, who needs
this information to validate the holder’s identity (Liu et al. 2020b ). This approach allows
data owners to specify access control rules, thereby generating responses that avoid leaking
unnecessary data (Xu et al. 2021 ; Liu et al. 2020a ). As an example, this pattern can be applied
in contexts such as Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI), where a certiﬁcate issuer might generate a
customized credential to show only the grade earned for a module rather than exposing the
entire transcript (Xu et al. 2021 ; Liu et al. 2020a ).
Tokenized Exchange This pattern allows authorizing and tracking access to off-chain data
storage through the use of security tokens. It allows for a veriﬁable history of data access,
which is useful for auditability, and also encapsulates the complexities of interacting with
various off-chain data sources via a standardized interface that allows encoding a set of

et al. ( 2020 ). A real-world application of this pattern could be in healthcare, where patient
data stored off-chain needs to be accessed by various stakeholders such as doctors, insurance
companies, and researchers.
Single Authorization The Single Authorization design pattern in smart contract security
is a protocol that designates a speciﬁc blockchain account as the sole authority to sign and
authorize transactions (Xu et al. 2021 ). This pattern enforces function-level authorization to
mitigate the risks associated with the open accessibility of smart contracts on blockchain plat-
forms, where, typically, any participant could invoke any function. It basically requires that
each function invocation is preceded by a veriﬁcation step to conﬁrm the caller’s authoriza-
tion by the predeﬁned single authority, offering a centralized method to manage permissions
in contrast to more complex multi-signature authorization mechanisms. For example, in a
decentralized ﬁnance application, this pattern could be used to ensure that only a desig-
nated account can authorize high-value transactions, thereby protecting the contract from
unauthorized access and other potential risks (Xu et al. 2021 ).
Stealth Address This pattern ensures that only the designated recipient can detect and spend
the funds associated with a speciﬁc transaction in the blockchain. When a sender initiates
a transfer, it generates a one-time, computationally random-looking address known as a
stealth address. This address is unique to the transaction and can only be recognized by the
recipient, who can then claim and spend the funds. This pattern prevents any third party from
determining the actual recipient of the transaction. Stealth addresses obscure the economic
relationship between the sender and recipient while ensuring that only the intended recipient
has access to the transferred assets (Lu et al. 2022 ; Kovács and Seres 2024 ).
Authorized Spender The Authorized Spender design pattern in smart contract development
enables token owners to grant a third party, such as another user or a smart contract, permission
to spend a predeﬁned amount of their tokens. The pattern is implemented through an ’approve’
function within the token contract, where the token owner speciﬁes the spender’s address and
the token amount they are authorized to use. By doing so, the contract records an allowance
that is veriﬁed each time the authorized spender attempts to initiate a transfer, ensuring that the
spender cannot exceed the set limit and providing a secure method for delegated transactions.
This is particularly useful in blockchain ecosystems where buyers want to delegate payment
responsibilities (Lu et al. 2022 ). As an example, in a decentralized marketplace, a buyer can
authorize a smart contract to handle payments on their behalf, streamlining the transaction
process and enhancing user convenience. The approve function can be added to the token
template contract, with parameters for the spender’s address and the amount, allowing, in
this way, the occurrence of delegated transactions.
4.7 Integrity
The Integrity subcategory of the security category, which includes a set of three patterns found
across 11 papers, ensures data is kept from unauthorized changes throughout its lifecycle. It
includes mechanisms that verify data integrity, protect against unauthorized modiﬁcations,
and ensure the authenticity of both on-chain and off-chain information.

Hash Integrity This pattern aims to inform about the integrity of data that is stored outside
the blockchain. A hash of the original data is generated and stored on the blockchain, which
allows users to check the integrity of the off-chain data by checking the on-chain hash value
(Lu et al. 2018 ; Ordoñez-Guerrero et al. 2022 ). This pattern is essential for the case of
applications in which data integrity is critical, such as ﬁnancial records, legal documents,
and other integrity-sensitive information (Xu et al. 2021 ; Rajasekar et al. 2020 ).
File Comparison File comparison involves verifying the integrity of a ﬁle by comparing
its cryptographic hash value with the corresponding hash stored on the blockchain. This
service allows users to check if a ﬁle has been altered without authorization, ensuring that the
ﬁle remains unchanged and original. By utilizing a cryptographic hash value like SHA-256,
users can select a ﬁle and verify its authenticity. If the hashes match, the ﬁle is conﬁrmed
to be authentic. This approach emphasizes veriﬁcation at the ﬁle level rather than handling
extensive data that may not be easily stored on the blockchain (Lu et al. 2018 ; Parmar and
Kaur 2021 ). As an example, in auditing processes, the hash of an agreement stored on-chain
allows auditors to verify the integrity of an off-chain ﬁle by ensuring its hash matches the
blockchain-stored value (Lu et al. 2018 ).
Reverse Veriﬁer The Reverse V eriﬁer pattern ensures the integrity of blockchain data when
accessed by external sources. When an off-chain entity, like a traditional database application
or a banking API, requests blockchain data, this pattern veriﬁes it by checking the stored hash
against the data, detecting any tampering. Reverse V eriﬁer speciﬁcally ensures the integrity
of blockchain data as it is accessed by external systems. This is especially useful for scenarios
like retrieving check data from the blockchain (Rajasekar et al. 2020 ).
4.8 Privacy
This subcategory includes three design patterns found across four of the analyzed papers.
These patterns focus on ensuring conﬁdentiality across interactions and transactions, e.g., by
hiding identities or preventing identity correlation.
Multiple Registrations The Multiple Registration pattern allows an entity to register and
use distinct identiﬁers for each of its transacting relationships, minimizing the risk of identity
correlation and privacy breaches. By using separate identiﬁers for different applications or
roles, interactions with one party can be isolated from another, thus enhancing privacy. This
approach involves registering multiple identiﬁers in an identiﬁer registry, where each identi-
ﬁer uniquely corresponds to a different identity relationship. Consequently, each transaction
is associated with a unique identiﬁer, preventing the linkage of identities and protecting the
entity’s privacy by avoiding the exposure of all identities held by that entity through a single
identiﬁer (Xu et al. 2021 ; Liu et al. 2020a ). This approach leverages decentralized identi-
ﬁers, a widely adopted standard in Self-Sovereign Identity frameworks, to manage unique,
persistent identiﬁers associated with blockchain accounts (Liu et al. 2020b ;X ue ta l . 2021 ).
As an example, a person could register separate identiﬁers for interactions with a hospital
and a university, preventing either entity from linking his/her activities (Xu et al. 2021 ;L i u
et al. 2020a ).
Zero Knowledge Proofs Zero-knowledge proof allows users to prove the validity of trans-
actions or data without revealing the underlying details. This pattern allows one party (the

prover) to inform another party (the veriﬁer) that they possess certain secret information,
which is veriﬁable, without revealing the information itself or the identities of either party.
This is achieved through an interactive veriﬁcation protocol consisting of a sequence of
predeﬁned actions that ensure the veriﬁer is convinced of the prover’s knowledge without
gaining any additional data. The protocol does not require complex public key infrastructure
and is resistant to malicious attempts to extract further information (Blum et al. 2020 ;S u n
et al. 2021 ;W a n ge ta l . 2019a ). A practical application may be the encoding of a vulnerability
and corresponding exploit of a real system. This would be veriﬁable by a target entity but,
at the same time, would not reveal the details, which otherwise could allow other entities to
exploit the vulnerable system.
Key Sharding In this pattern, a master cryptographic key is split into multiple shards, which
alone are incomplete but collectively can be used to reconstruct the original key. This tech-
nique prevents a single key’s overexposure across multiple transactions, protecting identity
privacy. It enables an entity to manage distinct subkeys for various identity accounts under
one master key, mitigating risks associated with key compromise. The main idea is that enti-
ties can conduct transactions with the assurance that their various identities remain unlinked
and secure (Liu et al. 2020a ;W a n ge ta l . 2019a ). In practical applications, users can store
these key shards securely in diverse ways, such as distributing them among trusted family
members or storing them in physical safes. If the original key is lost-due to events like device
failure or theft-users can recover it by collecting the required number of shards that meet the
regrouping threshold (Huang et al. 2024 ; Liu et al. 2020a ).
4.9 Risk Mitigation
This subcategory includes ﬁve design patterns found across 16 of the analyzed papers. These
patterns aim to reduce the impact of possible existing security threats in smart contracts.
Emergency Stop This pattern is a measure to rapidly stop the impact of a major vulnerability
by allowing to securely stop contract execution (Thirulokachander 2021 ). In practice, it can
be implemented as a function within the contract that an authenticated party can activate
so that the problematic operations can be disabled (Wohrer and Zdun 2018 ). A real-world
example of the need for this pattern is illustrated by the DAO attack, where participants on
the Ethereum network could only watch as their funds were withdrawn due to a lack of an
emergency stop mechanism (Rajasekar et al. 2020 ).
Speed Bump This pattern delays the execution of sensitive operations to diminish the risk
of abrupt, potentially damaging activities (e.g., an attacker exploiting a vulnerability). The
implementation of this pattern may take several forms, such as limiting the amount of funds
a user can withdraw per day and instituting a mandatory waiting period before processing
withdrawals, among others. This provides additional time for stakeholders to detect and
respond to irregularities (Wohrer and Zdun 2018 ;F a h m i d e he ta l . 2022 ). Analogous to real-
world banking scenarios, such as limiting withdrawals during a bank run, this pattern prevents
sudden, unrestrained actions that could harm the integrity of a contract. For example, in
decentralized ﬁnance (DeFi), slowing down high-value transactions allows additional time
for validators or stakeholders to monitor irregularities (Thirulokachander 2021 ).

Rate Limit Is a pattern that allows contracts to manage and cap the frequency of task exe-
cutions over a speciﬁed time period, with the aim of mitigating the effect of a high number
of messages being sent to a smart contract (e.g., a surge of requests) and controlling its com-
putational demand (Wohrer and Zdun 2018 ). By setting a rate limit, the pattern ensures that
functions, such as withdrawals or other sensitive operations, are not called too frequently,
thereby avoiding the risk associated with scenarios like rapid fund drainage (Fahmideh et al.
2022 ; Moura 2020 ).
Balance Limit This design pattern refers to setting the maximum amount of funds that can
be held within a contract at any given time, thereby managing the amount of money at risk.
When the contract’s balance reaches this predeﬁned quota, any further payments that attempt
to exceed this limit are rejected. However, it is important to note that this approach cannot
prevent Ether from being forcibly sent to the contract. This limits the potential damage in the
event of a contract breach by ensuring that only a manageable amount of funds is ever at risk
(Wohrer and Zdun 2018 ; Thirulokachander 2021 ). By capping the funds at risk, this pattern
minimizes the attack surface, as contracts holding excessive capital become attractive targets
for malicious actors (Wohrer and Zdun 2018 ).
Multi-signature The goal of this pattern is to make a certain transaction require the approval
of multiple authorized parties before it can be validated and executed. The main idea is that
no single entity should be able to unilaterally carry out a transaction. It serves as an additional
protective layer, particularly in scenarios where the loss of a private key cannot be remedied
by current blockchain technology. Multi-signature setups can vary in conﬁguration, from
requiring all parties to sign a transaction to requiring a subset of signatures. This allows for
some ﬂexibility in the mechanism, e.g., to better ﬁt speciﬁc scenarios (Liu et al. 2018 ;L u
et al. 2018 ).
4.10 Auxiliary
The Auxiliary category includes a set of patterns found across three papers. They aim to
facilitate the implementation of some security-related speciﬁc function within a system, thus
not being part of the core functionality but instead supporting the security measures.
Key Generation This pattern refers to the creation and distribution of encryption keys to
secure transactions on the blockchain. The overall idea is to generate keys (using asymmetric
encryption) that are shared among parties prior to executing transactions on the blockchain.
Such keys should be properly managed so that security is not compromised (Lu et al. 2018 ).
Database Proxy The Database Proxy pattern is a specialized adaptation of the traditional
Proxy design pattern tailored for blockchain-based systems. Its primary purpose is to add an
extra layer of security and efﬁciency by performing lightweight tasks, such as basic security
checks or auditing, before allowing access to database connectors. It minimizes computational
costs on-chain by deferring the retrieval and unpackaging or decryption of sensitive data until
it is required off-chain. The contract code should be organized to ﬁrst invoke the proxy for
preliminary operations and then proceed with more detailed data handling off-chain (Zhang
et al. 2020 ).

4.11 Summary
Within the Security category, we have identiﬁed 10 distinct subcategories, each representing
a critical aspect of securing smart contracts and blockchain applications. Among these, the
Access Control subcategory stands out as the most signiﬁcant, comprising 13 design patterns.
This is reﬂective of the crucial role that access control plays in ensuring that only authorized
entities can execute speciﬁc functions or access sensitive data within a smart contract. The
large number of design patterns in this subcategory also highlights the diverse approaches
available to developers for implementing strong access control, from simple ownership checks
to more complex permission management systems.
Following Access Control, the Risk Mitigation and Secure External Calls subcategories
are the next largest, respectively, with 5 and 4 patterns.
Risk Mitigation includes design patterns aimed at reducing the potential risk of security
threats and vulnerabilities. These patterns help developers anticipate and address potential
risks that could compromise the contract’s security and functionality. The presence of 5
patterns in this subcategory highlights the importance of proactively managing risks in smart
contract development.
Secure External Calls includes design patterns focused on safely interacting with external
contracts or systems. Smart contracts often need to communicate with other contracts or
external data sources, which introduces a signiﬁcant security risk if not handled properly.
Vulnerabilities such as reentrancy attacks can arise when external calls are not securely
managed. The four design patterns in this subcategory provide developers with some best
practices for securely handling these interactions.
These subcategories, together with others in the security category, form a more complete
framework for addressing the various security challenges inherent in smart contract develop-
ment. The emphasis on Access Control, Risk Mitigation, and Secure External Calls reﬂects
the broader priorities in blockchain security: mitigating security threats, controlling access,
and ensuring safe interactions with external entities.

6 Discussion
We have identiﬁed 144 smart contract design patterns and categorized them into four main
groups: Control, Maintainability, Performance, and Security. This categorization provides a
structured framework for understanding various critical aspects of smart contract develop-
ment. We then highlighted several design patterns, with a particular emphasis on security.
Here, we highlight the key ﬁndings and gaps identiﬁed from our analysis:
–General distribution of design patterns across the main categories: The majority of
design patterns fall under the Control category (51%), addressing functional and opera-
tional concerns that are fundamental to software development, including smart contract
development. The remaining design patterns are nearly evenly distributed between Secu-
rity (24%) and Performance (20%), underscoring the importance of both security and
performance aspects in smart contract development. Patterns related to Maintainability
make up the smallest category (5%), reﬂecting the reduced need for maintenance in smart
contracts due to the immutable nature of blockchain technology.
–Distribution of design patterns across the subcategories: The gas consumption sub-
category within the performance category is the largest, comprising 27 design patterns.
This underscores the critical importance of gas management in blockchain and smart con-
tracts. The next most signiﬁcant subcategory is interaction, within the control category,
highlighting the complexity of developing interactions among contracts and among con-
tracts and third parties. The third most signiﬁcant subcategory is access control within the
security category, emphasizing the crucial need for secure access to smart contracts and
blockchain. Together, these subcategories, Gas Consumption, Interaction, and Access
Control, highlight the need for careful attention to performance optimization, interaction
management, and security controls for smart contract development, each of which is
crucial for building reliable, efﬁcient, and secure blockchain applications.
–Categorization of security design patterns: We have identiﬁed a total of 36 security
design patterns, categorized into 10 subcategories. For each security pattern, we dis-
cussed the general concept, the intention behind it, and the security measures it enables.
Whenever possible, we connected the descriptions to real-world examples or provided a
more practical perspective on each pattern.
–Distribution of security design patterns across security subcategories: Within the
Security category, we identiﬁed 10 key subcategories critical to securing smart contracts
and blockchain applications. Access Control is the most signiﬁcant, with 13 design pat-
terns, highlighting its important role in ensuring that only authorized entities can access
or modify smart contract functions and data. Following this, Risk Mitigation and Secure
External Calls are the next largest subcategories, respectively, with 5 and 4 patterns. Risk

–Completeness of security design patterns in terms of smart contract vulnerability

Together, these patterns address only 6 out of the 94 known vulnerabilities presented
in OpenSCV . In addition, it is worthwhile mentioning that two OpenSCV families of
vulnerabilities, namely “#3 Mishandled Events” and “#4 Gas Depletion” (Vidal et al.
2024 ), are not considered by any pattern. While this is understandable due to the overall
low number of vulnerabilities addressed, it further highlights a signiﬁcant gap in the
availability of design solutions that can aid developers in preventing vulnerabilities. It
is likely that families of related vulnerabilities may partially share solutions or that at
least the insights gained from designing a solution for a certain vulnerability can help
in designing solutions for related vulnerabilities. This is an aspect for which awareness
must be raised.
In summary, the dynamic and evolving nature of blockchain technology means that new
vulnerabilities continuously emerge, posing ongoing challenges in smart contract develop-
ment. Despite the progress made, the ever-changing landscape of security threats necessitates
ongoing research of security design patterns. Addressing these challenges is essential not only
for advancing the theoretical framework of blockchain technology but also for ensuring the
practical security and reliability of smart contract applications in real-world scenarios.
This work highlighted signiﬁcant gaps where further research is needed across various
dimensions . Here, we outline several potential directions for future research:
– The ﬁrst research direction may be to target vulnerability detection tools. Such tools may
help collect the different forms of security vulnerabilities and associated information
to formalize the corresponding design patterns. Thus, establishing the appropriate links
between vulnerability detection tools and the vulnerability information that they collect
may be necessary for acknowledging a recurrent problem, collecting its characteristics,
and contributing information that is essential for deﬁning new design patterns.
– While our identiﬁcation and categorization of security design patterns may serve as a
foundation that allows for evolution, such evolution will only occur if research on security
design patterns with direct connections to security vulnerabilities is carried out. Thus,
a second research direction should be set on further extending the current set of known
security design patterns for smart contracts .
– A third research direction may be directed towards the process of deﬁning smart contract
design patterns and may involve, for instance, the creation of a collaborative smart con-
tract patterns repository . This would include researchers and practitioners collaborating
in gathering information, identifying and proposing solutions, voting or selecting the best
candidates for patterns, and iteratively reviewing design patterns. An up-to-date catalog
of security design patterns is a valuable asset when the end goal is to improve the security
of smart contracts.
– One of the main gaps identiﬁed in this work is indeed the very limited coverage of current
design patterns regarding current smart contract vulnerabilities. While many current
design patterns target more common risks such as reentrancy or integer overﬂows, other
less widespread or more recent vulnerabilities are not addressed. Obviously, there may be
cases of vulnerabilities for which a design pattern may not help, such as a wrong compiler
version set on a smart contract (i.e., 5.8.2 Outdated Compiler V ersion in OpenSCV).
Despite this, the large number of unaddressed vulnerabilities leads us to conclude that

7 Conclusion
This paper reviewed the state of the art in smart contract design patterns, with particular
emphasis on security design patterns. We categorized the various design patterns and analyzed
how well current security patterns avoid known security vulnerabilities. Results have shown
that the security design patterns identiﬁed address a small number of security vulnerabilities
(6 out of 94 in OpenSCV). In future work, we intend to understand how the design patterns
catalog presented in this paper can be extended to avoid a broader range of vulnerabilities.
