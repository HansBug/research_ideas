A data catalog enables an organization  to maintain  an inventory  of its data assets by collecting  and managing  
the relevant  metadata.  We conducted  a systematic  multi-vocal  literature  review on data catalogs  to understand  
their features  and usage. We systematically  selected  and analyzed  86 literature  sources and 39 catalog tools. 
We first utilized the findings  from the literature  to develop  a classification  framework  comprising  24 fine-
grained and five high-level  features,  along with three maturity  levels. Next, we analyzed  39 tools based on 
the classification  framework.  Organizations  typically  include a data catalog as a component  in their big data 
platforms  and use it to support the various phases of the metadata  management  lifecycle.  Hence, we also 
mapped  the catalog features  to the requirements  of metadata-driven  big data architectures,  namely data mesh, 
data lake, and data lakehouse.  Moreover,  the mappings  of the features  to the phases in a metadata  management  
lifecycle  were developed.  Our findings  shall aid organizations  in making informed  decisions  when choosing  
data catalog tools and help researchers  identify the critical research  issues in data cataloging  and metadata  
management.

1. Introduction
Metadata,  often described  as data about data, are critical in manag-
ing and understanding  large and complex  data assets (Furner , 2019 ; 
Sen, 2004 ). Metadata  include  information  such as schemas,  origin, 
history,  location,  ownership,  permissions,  and versions  of data (Furner , 
2019 ; Gartner , 0000 ). Such information  enables  discovery,  understand-
ing, and governance  of data assets within and across organizations.  A 
lack of metadata  is a significant  bottleneck  to turning  data into valuable  
assets in data-driven  organizations  (Graetsch  et al., 2023 ).
Metadata  are also pivotal in big data architectures  like data mesh, 
data lake, and data lakehouse.  In the data mesh, which is a decen-
tralized  approach  where each data domain  is responsible  for its data 
products,  metadata  facilitates  the interoperability  and discoverability  
of data products  across domains  (Dehghani  and Fowler , 2022 ; Goedege-
buure et al., 2024 ). In the data lakehouse,  which combines  data lakes 
and data warehouse  functionalities,  metadata  support  efficient  data 
storage,  querying,  and analytics  (Zaharia  et al., 2021 ). In the data lake 
architecture,  which stores vast amounts  of structured,  semi-structured,

Data catalogs  are emerging  as an essential  component  in big data 
architectures  to acquire,  store, and manage  metadata  (Gröger , 2021 ). 
A data catalog  is a repository  of metadata  that has been acquired  from 
various  data sources  in an organization  (Olesen-Bagneux , 2023 ). For 
example,  data scientists  in a manufacturing  company  need to forecast  
the demand  for products  for the next quarter.  With a data catalog,  
they can quickly  find relevant  data sets like sales figures,  inventory  
levels, and supply chain metrics  through  the company’s  data catalog  by 
searching  data sets based on their metadata.  Without  a catalog,  they 
would spend significant  time manually  searching  across departments  
for this information,  risking the use of outdated  or incomplete  data. 
Data catalogs  also promote  collaboration  and data sharing  among 
teams, fostering  a data-driven  culture (Olesen-Bagneux , 2023 ) within 
organizations.

While many data catalog  tools exist, such as Alation,1 Atlan,2 In-
formatica  Data Marketplace,3 DataHub4 and CKAN,5 there is a lack of 
systematic  study focusing  on their features,  usage in metadata  man-
agement,  and integration  with data architectures  such as data mesh, 
data lake, and data lakehouse.  Organizations  can use such information  
to select a suitable  data catalog  tool and successfully  implement  their 
metadata  management  use cases. Researchers  can use the results from 
this study to understand  the gaps between  industry  and academia  and 
identify  the features  of data catalogs  that require  further investigation.
In this research,  we aim to study data catalog  tools to understand  
their characteristics  and formulate  guidelines  for using them to manage  
metadata  in big data architectures.  For this purpose,  we conducted  a 
Systematic  Multivocal  Literature  Review  (MLR) (Garousi  et al., 2019 ) 
on data catalog  and metadata  management  tools. An MLR enables  us 
to conduct  a wide range of research  across various  resources,  allowing  
us to cover the latest academic  results and the most recent industry  
findings.
We analyzed  86 sources,  from white and grey literature,  along 
with 39 data catalogs  available  on the market and their respective  
documentation.  We identified  24 fine-grained  features  and 5 high-level  
ones for data catalogs,  and we mapped  them to the metadata  life-
cycle management  steps, 3 maturity  levels for data catalogs,  and the 
requirements  of modern  metadata-driven  big-data  architectures,  such 
as data mesh.
The paper is organized  as follows.  Section  2 provides  an overview  
of existing  research  on data catalog  and metadata  management  tools. 
Section  3 defines  the research  questions  and describes  the methodology  
adopted.  In Section  4, we describe  the classification  framework  we 
derived  from our MLR, and we identify  the features  of data catalogs.  In 
Section  5, we classify  data catalogs  available  on the market according  to 
the classification  framework.  Section  6 maps the features  to metadata  
life-cycle  management  steps, and Section  7 maps the features  to big 
data architecture  requirements.  Section  8 discusses  our results.  Finally,  
we describe  the threats to the validity  of our study in Section  9 and 
draw our conclusions  in Section  10.
2. Background  and related  work
In this section,  we introduce  the key types of metadata,  including  
business,  technical,  and operational,  as they are fundamental  con-
cepts underlying  data catalogs.  We also review related work on data 
marketplaces,  integration  with modern  data architectures,  essential  
functionalities  of data catalog  tools, and the role of data catalogs  in 
managing  metadata.  The section concludes  by identifying  research  gaps 
and motivating  the need for a comprehensive  analysis  of data catalog  
capabilities  and their support  for the metadata  lifecycle.
2.1. Metadata
The metadata  of an artifact  (e.g., a data point, data set, or machine  
learning  model) provide  information  about one or more aspects  of the 
artifact.  A data catalog  ingests and manages  various  types of metadata  
from different  data sources,  including  structured,  semi-structured,  and 
unstructured  data. While many metadata  classifications  exist (Baca , 
2008 ; Zeng and Qin, 2020 ; Sawadogo  and Darmont , 2021a ; Ravat and 
Zhao , 2019 ), which include  administrative , descriptive , preservation , tech-
nical and use metadata,  for brevity  we will describe  them according  to 
the Data Management  Body of Knowledge  (DAMA-DMBOK)  (DAMA In-
ternational , 2017 ), which identifies  three primary  categories:  business  
metadata,  technical  metadata,  and operational  metadata.

regulatory  compliance  labels, and data quality rules. This type of 
metadata  helps ensure that data is aligned  with business  processes  
and regulatory  requirements  within organizations.  For example,  a Cus-
tomer_ID  field in a customer  relationship  management  (CRM) system 
may be defined  as a unique identifier  for each customer  in the business  
glossary.  Additionally,  it may be classified  as personally  identifiable  
information  (PII) and governed  by the General  Data Protection  Regu-
lation (GDPR)  policies,  restricting  access to authorized  personnel  only. 
Business  metadata  also include  data classifications  (e.g., ‘‘Confidential,’’  
‘‘Public,’’  ‘‘Restricted’’),  which are essential  for security  and compliance  
purposes.
Technical  metadata.  Technical  metadata  describe  the structural  aspects  
of data and thus helps data engineers  and data scientists  to correctly  
store, manage,  and use data. They encompass  schema  definitions,  data 
types, constraints,  indexing  strategies,  and storage  details.  These meta-
data enable systems  to understand  how data is stored, processed,  and 
integrated  across platforms.  For instance,  in a cloud data lake, a dataset  
might be stored in a storage  bucket as partitioned  Parquet6 files, with 
schema  details specifying  column  names, data types, and constraints  
such as primary  and foreign  keys.
Operational  metadata.  Operational  metadata  provide  insights  into pro-
cessing  aspects  of data at run time. They include  query execution  logs, 
data freshness  indicators,  access control  logs, and data lineage.  This 
type of metadata  is critical for monitoring  data quality,  optimizing  
system performance,  and ensuring  compliance  with security  policies.  
For example,  a dataset  used in a financial  reporting  dashboard  may 
contain  metadata  on last update timestamps,  query response  times, and 
user access logs, helping  organizations  ensure that reports are based on 
the most recent and accurate  data.
2.2. Related work
Several  studies have attempted  to review and analyze  the tools used 
in data cataloging  and metadata  management.
2.2.1. Data marketplaces,  integration  with data architectures,  and metadata-
driven technologies
A concept  related to the data catalog  is the data marketplace,  a 
platform  that provides  the infrastructure  and services  for exchang-
ing data products  between  providers  and consumers  across diverse  
environments,  incorporating  features  for dataset  organization,  access, 
sharing,  and pricing (Fernandez  et al., 2020 ; Niu et al., 2018 ; Barns , 
2018 ). Driessen  et al. (2022 ) systematically  review data market plat-
forms, identifying  key application  domains  where data markets  are 
actively  used and introducing  taxonomies  of design challenges  and 
proposed  solutions.  Palviainen  and Suksi (2023 ) conduct  a large-scale  
survey of data marketplaces,  analyzing  over 190 commercial  platforms.  
They identify  key metadata  challenges  in data trading  ecosystems,  par-
ticularly  the lack of interoperability  standards  for metadata  structuring  
and pricing mechanisms.  Sharma  et al. (2020 ) propose  a blockchain-
based data marketplace  architecture,  leveraging  smart contracts  and 
decentralized  data storage  to enable secure metadata  exchange.
Zhang et al. (2023 ) focus on data pricing models,  demonstrating  
how metadata  influence  pricing in data exchange  platforms.  The role 
of data marketplaces  in metadata  lifecycle  management  is becoming  
increasingly  relevant.  The study on trusted data marketplaces  for credit 
scoring  (Azcoitia  and Laoutaris , 2022 ) outlines  a reference  architecture  
that integrates  data catalogs  with privacy-enhancing  technologies,  such 
as homomorphic  encryption  and multi-party  computation.  Similarly,

Data catalogs  do not operate  in isolation:  they must be integrated  
with enterprise  data architectures  and frameworks  to support  efficient  
data discovery  and exchange  in organizations.  Recent research  empha-
sizes the pivotal role of data catalogs  across data architectures  such 
as data lakes, data lakehouses,  and data mesh. For example,  Narge-
sian et al. (2019 ) argue that without  metadata  management  systems,  
data lakes risk becoming  unmanageable  data swamps . They show how 
metadata  can improve  data discovery  and reuse in data lakes (using 
catalogs).  In the context  of time-series  data scenarios,  Pohl et al. (2024 ) 
identify  the metadata  management  layer as a key component  in the 
data lakehouse  architecture  to support  data governance  and transac-
tion management.   Nambiar  and Mundra  (2022 ) discuss the roles of 
metadata  in both data warehouses  and data lakes. They advocate  for
knowledge  catalogs  that enhance  semantic  understanding,  data lineage,  
and governance  across structured  and unstructured  data environments.  
Karkošková  (2024 ) considers  the data catalog  to be the backbone  of 
the data mesh, enabling  decentralized  governance  and cross-domain  
collaboration  through  features  such as data shops, knowledge  graphs,  
and lineage  tracking.  Other studies also suggest  using the data catalog  
to support  data product  discovery  in data mesh (Van Eijk et al., 2024 ; 
Goedegebuure  et al., 2024 ).
2.2.2. Functionalities  and requirements  of data catalogs
A fundamental  challenge  in data cataloging  is identifying  the key 
functionalities  required  for effective  metadata  management.  Jahnke  
and Otto (2023b ) provide  an in-depth  overview  of modern  data cat-
alog architectures,  outlining  essential  features  for metadata  manage-
ment, data discovery,  and governance.  However,  they do not offer 
a general  framework  for classifying  tools, leaving  room for further 
exploration.  Petrik et al. (2023 ) take a more structured  approach  by 
identifying  13 functional  requirements  for enterprise  data catalogs,  
categorizing  them into metadata  management,  data governance,  inter-
operability,  and intelligent  automation.  Beyond  catalog  functionalities,  
metadata  interoperability  and automation  are crucial in ensuring  effi-
cient data exchange.  Labadie  et al. (2020 ) discuss how data catalogs  can 
operationalize  the FAIR (Findable,  Accessible,  Interoperable,  Reusable)  
principles,  particularly  within enterprise  settings.  Similarly,  Ehrlinger  
et al. (2021 ) present  4 functional  components  of data catalogs  and an 
8-step guideline  for their implementation.
Data catalogs  could also support  complex  metadata  governance  
and lifecycle  management.  Sawadogo  and Darmont  (2021a ) provide  
an overview  of the state-of-the-art  regarding  different  approaches  for 
data lake design,  focusing  on architectures  and metadata  manage-
ment. Highlighting  the importance  of metadata  management  in data 
lakes, Megdiche  et a. Megdiche  et al. (2021 ) propose  and validate  a 
metadata  model compliant  with the FAIR (Findable,  Accessible,  In-
teroperable,  and Reusable)  principles.  The study by Thamjaroenporn  
et al. on big data analytics  in digital government  introduces  a frame-
work for data governance  and cataloging,  emphasizing  data exchange  
and metadata  standardization  (Thamjaroenporn  and Achalakul , 2020 ). 
Martinez-Gil’s  work on quantifying  Open Data Catalog  (ODC) similar-
ity explores  methods  for comparing  metadata  structures  and ensuring  
interoperability  across different  catalogs  (Martinez-Gil , 2023 ).
2.3. Research  gaps and goals
Despite  the growing  importance  of data catalogs  and metadata  
management,  several key issues remain unresolved.  First, there is a 
limited  understanding  of the core features  of data catalogs  and the tools 
available  to support  them. Additionally,  the integration  of data catalogs  
with metadata-centric  architectures,  such as data mesh, data lake, and 
data lakehouse,  is not well-defined.  Another  gap is a systematic  analysis  
of the extent to which current  data catalog  tools support  the entire metadata  life cycle. Existing  studies tend to focus on individual  aspects  
of metadata  or catalogs  without  providing  a comprehensive  view that 
addresses  all these elements  together.
We aim to bridge these research  gaps by systematically  reviewing  
data catalog  tools using both academic  and grey literature  sources.  
This study analyzes  the core features  of data catalogs,  reviews  existing  
tools, and explores  their alignment  with metadata-driven  architec-
tures. We also evaluate  how data catalogs  support  the different  phases 
in the metadata  lifecycle,  providing  a comprehensive  perspective  on 
data cataloging  to guide future research  and practical  applications  by 
practitioners.
3. Research  methodology
We conducted  a Systematic  Multivocal  Literature  Review  (MLR)
(Garousi  et al., 2019 ) to identify  and classify  a list of data catalog  
tools and their features  and capabilities.  We chose MLR because  most 
data catalog  tools are from the industry,  and many related grey litera-
ture sources  exist. Our MLR primarily  follows  the guidelines  proposed  
by Garousi  et al. (2019 ) and MLR studies (Islam et al., 2019a ; Recupito  
et al., 2022 ; Islam et al., 2019b ; Edison et al., 2021 ; Islam et al., 2019b ). 
We also drew inspiration  and guidance  from literature  reviews  and 
comparative  studies of tools (Giamattei  et al., 2024 ; Yussupov  et al., 
2021 ).
Fig.  1 shows our MLR methodology.  We followed  a structured  
process,  starting  with a pilot study to assess feasibility  and define an 
MLR approach  covering  both white and grey literature.  Four research  
questions  guided the study, focusing  on data catalog  capabilities,  tools, 
metadata  support,  and relevance  to modern  architectures.  We ap-
plied inclusion/exclusion  criteria  and designed  two queries  to retrieve  
sources,  refining  them through  filters and time constraints.  This yielded  
86 sources:  54 academic  papers,  23 grey literature  sources,  and nine 
sources  identified  through  snowballing.  Of the 65 identified  tools, 39 
were selected  after the filtering  process.  A classification  framework  was 
built from extracted  features  and mapped  to metadata  lifecycle  stages 
and big data architecture  needs.
3.1. Pilot study and planning:  Background,  research  questions  and litera-
ture selection  criteria
This paper aims to investigate  the state of the art of data catalog  
tools, assess their main capabilities  and features,  and guide organiza-
tions in choosing  the most suitable  tool for building  a data catalog.  We 
also consider  the use of such tools within emerging  data architectures,  
such as data lakehouses,  data meshes,  and data lakes.
The pilot study was the first step, used to test methods  and define 
the scope of our research.  We reviewed  the studies that analyze  the 
academic  literature  on data catalogs  and metadata  management,  and 
found limited  coverage  of the dimensions  we aimed to explore.  As a 
result, we included  the relevant  grey literature,  leading  to an MLR 
approach.
We consider  the following  four research  questions  in our MLR:
1.RQ1. What are the key capabilities  of data catalogs?  This question  
aims to identify  and synthesize  key features  provided  by data 
catalogs,  providing  a taxonomy  for systematically  classifying  and 
comparing  the cataloging  tools.
2.RQ2. What tools can be used to build a data catalog?  We want 
to provide  an overview  of the current  state of the art regarding  
tools that enable enterprises  to build data catalogs.  We also aim 
to analyze  the selected  tools based on the features  identified  in 
RQ1.
3.RQ3. To what extent can the data catalog tools support the phases 
in the metadata  management  life-cycle?  We want to understand  
how the various  features  of cataloging  tools can be employed  
to support  various  metadata  management  activities.The Journal  of Systems  & Software  230 (2025)  112584

and Klas, 2010 ). The realization  of this feature can employ  the fine-
grained  features  that support  connections  to data sources,  building  
a data dictionary,  gathering  data quality information,  and harvesting  
metadata  from a sample of the complete  dataset  (Yang , 2023 ; Eichler 
et al., 2022 , 2021 ; Helal et al., 2021 ; Cheng-Wei  and Bing-Bing , 2018 ;

relate data objects semantically.

tions to choose databases  that align with their infrastructure.  They 
manage  metadata  schemas,  relationships,  and lineage,  often stored as 
SQL tables, JSON, or RDF. In some cases, it is possible  to find hybrid 
architectures,  combining  databases  for scalability  and flexibility.

4.2. Metadata  augmentation
The metadata  collected  by catalogs  might be incomplete  or require  
to be augmented  with additional  information.  A data source might 
not provide  quality metrics,  such as integrity,  accuracy,  authenticity,  
and uniqueness  (Yang , 2023 ). A data asset might lack the documenta-
tion of its data models,  data semantics,  and business  context  (Zitoun 
et al., 2021 ; Sawadogo  and Darmont , 2021b ; Abolhassani  et al., 2018 ; 
Batwada  et al., 2020 ). Metadata  augmentation  allows the enrichment  
of metadata  with additional  information,  such as semantics,  labels, 
annotations,  and quality information  (Hai et al., 2021 ; Bianchini  et al., 
2023 ; Kumar et al., 2024 ; Papenbrock  et al., 2015 ; Bratt et al., 2017 ; 
Maccioni  and Torlone , 2017 ; Hai et al., 2016 ; de Oliveira  et al., 2022 ; 
Eichler et al., 2022 ; Sawadogo  and Darmont , 2021b ; Grunzke  et al., 
2019 ; Abolhassani  et al., 2018 ; Boukhers  et al., 2023 ). Catalog  features,  
such as business  glossaries  and classification,  enable enriching  data 
assets by incorporating  tags and labels based on criteria  such as asset 
type, sensitivity  level, business-criticality,  quality score, and business  
terms adopted  by an organization  (e.g., business  domain,  data domain,  
and department)  (Eichler et al., 2022 ).
Data documentation  (Shraga and Miller , 2023 ; Eichler et al., 2022 ; 
Zitoun et al., 2021 ; Eichler et al., 2021 ). The documentation  of a 
data asset typically  includes  brief descriptions  of data, links to other 
resources,  such as glossary  terms, dashboards  and related datasets,  and 
references  to documentation  outside  the catalog,  such as APIs documen-
tation, ETL pipelines  documentation  and reports.  Data catalogs  may 
employ  AI in managing  documentation,  for example,  generating  doc-
umentation  by automatically  summarizing  and interpreting  metadata  
attributes  (Holl and Gossling , 2019 ; Kobayashi  et al., 2018 ; Bratt et al., 
2017 ; Hai et al., 2016 ; MiliĆ et al., 2021 ). Many catalogs  use built-
in Markdown  or Rich Text editors to create and store documentation  
or employ  Wiki-based  and graph-based  technologies,  e.g., knowledge  
graphs and graph databases  (such as Neo4j15).
Classification  (Eichler et al., 2022 ; Beheshti  et al., 2018 ; Vries et al., 
2022 ; Grunzke  et al., 2019 ; Cherradi  et al., 2023 ; Li and Sugimoto , 2017 ; 
Aggour et al., 2017 ; Bratt et al., 2017 ; Hai et al., 2016 ; Cano et al., 
2023 ; Kobayashi  et al., 2018 ; MiliĆ et al., 2021 ; Shanmugam  and Seshadri , 
2016 ). This feature refers to the process  of organizing  and classifying  
data assets based on some criteria  that span from data type and sensi-
tivity to usage and quality.  This feature helps users identify  data assets 
based on their characteristics  (e.g., specific  data class or business  term). 
Catalogs  may offer ML-based  or rule-based  classification  capabilities  
that scan and categorize  assets based on their content  (Sousa et al., 
2023 ; Boukhers  et al., 2023 ; Stansberry  et al., 2020 ). The classification  
process  typically  adds tags and labels to data sets, enabling  advanced  
search capabilities.  Catalog  users can also personalize  their experience  
by assigning  personal  labels to data assets.
15https://neo4j.com/Business  glossary  (Bianchini  et al., 2023 ; Shanmugam  and Seshadri , 
2016 ; Eichler et al., 2021 , 2022 ). The business  glossary  is a central-

terms. It represents  a bridge between  business  and technical  terms 
and can ensure that everyone  in an organization  understands  and

4.3. Metadata  usage
Once metadata  are collected,  stored, and augmented,  they can be 
used. Metadata  users can be either a software  application  (e.g., the 
data discovery  component  of a data catalog)  or a human (Pingos and 
Andreou , 2023 ; Vries et al., 2022 ). A common  use case for metadata  
is developing  dashboards  for data quality visualization,  data asset 
usage monitoring,  or data lineage  visualization  (Cherradi  et al., 2023 ; 
Sousa et al., 2023 ; Shraga and Miller , 2023 ; Huang , 2020 ; Li and 
Sugimoto , 2017 ; Cronholm  et al., 2017 ; Seabolt  et al., 2018 ; Armbrust  
et al., 2020 ; Shastri et al., 2020 ; Abolhassani  et al., 2018 ; Shanmugam  
and Seshadri , 2016 ). Catalogs  might offer the feature to define data 
products  directly  in the catalog  by collecting  and organizing  metadata  
accordingly  (Eichler et al., 2021 , 2022 ).
Search (Eichler et al., 2022 , 2021 ; Helal et al., 2021 ; Holl and Gossling , 
2019 ). The search feature enables  the discovery  of data assets. Data 
catalogs  implement  this feature with different  technologies:  full-text  
search engines,  like Elasticsearch16; Natural  Language  Processing  (NLP); 
search ranking  algorithms;  knowledge  graphs,  which are at the basis of 
the data discovery  platform  proposed  by Helal et al. (2021 ), enable to 
find relationships  between  datasets  and efficiently  integrate  them. The 
type of technology  that forms the basis for the search engine used by a 
data catalog  has a huge impact on data discovery  and its impact once 
adopted,  especially  if enhanced  by AI, capable  of providing  insights  and 
personalized  recommendations  suggest  related datasets  based on user 
activity  and metadata  context  (Beheshti  et al., 2018 ; Cherradi  et al., 
2023 ).
Dashboards  (Sousa et al., 2023 ). Dashboards  provide  visual insights  
into data usage, governance  status, lineage,  and quality metrics.  Meta-
data also power data lineage  and impact analysis  by tracking  depen-
dencies  across datasets,  and supports  data quality monitoring  through  
automated  checks and completeness  or accuracy  scoring.  Visualizing  
metrics,  insights,  and any information  related to data assets is a feature 
that can be found in catalogs.
Data profiling  (Liu and Zsu, 2009 ; Zitoun et al., 2021 ; Helal et al., 2021 ; 
Maccioni  and Torlone , 2017 ; Papenbrock  et al., 2015 ; Grunzke  et al., 
2019 ). Data profiling  generates  statistics  or representative  summaries  
of a data asset to understand  its structure  and quality.  Data catalogs  
generally  can perform  this activity  when extracting  metadata  from a 
dataset.
Data product  (Dehghani  and Fowler , 2022 ; Goedegebuure  et al., 2024 ). 
Dehghani  (Dehghani  and Fowler , 2022 ) introduces  data as a product  as 
the second principle  of the data mesh architecture.
[It] applies product  thinking  to domain-oriented  data to remove usability  
frictions  and truly delight the experience  of data users .
The desired  attributes  of data products  are discoverability,  addressabil-
ity, understandability,  trustworthiness,  native accessibility,  interoper-
ability, independent  value, and security.  Some catalogs  provide  built-in

4.4. Governance
Abraham  et al. (2019 ) analyzed  the existing  literature  on data 
governance,  including  various  definitions  for data governance,  and 
created  a common  definition.
‘‘Data governance  specifies  a cross-functional  framework  for managing  
data as a strategic  enterprise  asset. In doing so, data governance  spec-
ifies decision  rights and accountabilities  for an organization’s  decision-
making about its data. Furthermore,  data governance  formalizes  data 
policies,  standards,  and procedures  and monitors  compliance’’.
Metadata  are vital inputs for governance  operations,  as they provide  
observability  and monitoring  of the operations  on data happening  
across the organization  (Yang , 2023 ; Pingos and Andreou , 2023 ; Zitoun 
et al., 2021 ; Sawadogo  and Darmont , 2021b ; Cronholm  et al., 2017 ; 
Armbrust  et al., 2020 ; Amorim  et al., 2017 ). However,  they can also 
be the outputs  of governance  operations,  such as data policies  and the 
roles and responsibilities  of stakeholders  (Sousa et al., 2023 ; de Oliveira  
et al., 2022 ; Eichler et al., 2022 ; Seabolt  et al., 2018 ; Shastri et al., 
2020 ; Shanmugam  and Seshadri , 2016 ; Dong et al., 2016 ; Casanovas  
et al., 2016 ). Monitoring  and recording  how data assets are accessed,  
used, and modified  is a feature that some catalogs  offer. This can be 
implemented  at different  levels, such as log monitoring,  audit trails, 
and access patterns.  Information  collected  from the consumers  who 
use data assets can be exploited  to understand  their value and impact 
on an organization.  For instance,  if a data catalog  can collect data 
quality metrics,  it might also automatically  monitor  if metric values are 
inconsistent  and thus trigger alerts or initiate  workflows  for handling  
or reporting  quality issues. Another  automated  element  is to assign 
tasks to individuals  with specific  roles, such as data stewards,  to 
investigate  and resolve  issues. For instance,  a catalog  might track how 
a data asset is used in multiple  downstream  dashboards  and machine  
learning  models,  allowing  users to assess the impact of changes  to the 
dataset  on business  processes  (Hai et al., 2021 ; Sousa et al., 2023 ; 
Shastri et al., 2020 ; Tang et al., 2019 ; Abolhassani  et al., 2018 ). We 
identified  several fine-grained  features  that describe  the various  aspects  
of governance:  data lineage,  change  tracking,  sensitive  data detection,  
roles and responsibilities,  task automation  and standardization,  rules 
and policies,  data quality assessment,  and data risk (Dolhopolov  et al., 
2023 ; Cherradi  et al., 2023 ; Muñoz et al., 2021 ; Batwada  et al., 2020 ; 
Stansberry  et al., 2020 ).
Data lineage  (Hai et al., 2021 ). Data lineage  is the process  of col-
lecting,  recording,  and visualizing  the flow of data from its sources  to 
destinations.  Data catalogs  offer this feature,  sometimes  enhanced  by 
AI, for example,  augmenting  lineage  by completing  missing  information  
or adding tags and semantic  descriptions  to lineage  steps (Schrott  et al., 
2023 ; de Oliveira  et al., 2022 ; Eichler et al., 2022 ; Huang , 2020 ; Holl 
and Gossling , 2019 ; Maccioni  and Torlone , 2017 ; Li and Sugimoto , 
2017 ; Aggour  et al., 2017 ; Hai et al., 2016 ; Shastri et al., 2020 ; Tang 
et al., 2019 ; Abolhassani  et al., 2018 ; Shanmugam  and Seshadri , 2016 ; 
Guedes  et al., 2020 ; Interlandi  et al., 2018 ; Cherradi  et al., 2023 ; Dang 
and Anh, 2021 ).
Changes  tracking  (Shraga and Miller , 2023 ; de Oliveira  et al., 2022 ; 
Eichler et al., 2022 , 2021 ; Huang , 2020 ; Armbrust  et al., 2020 ). This 
feature refers to the capability  to monitor  and record modifications  
made to data assets and their metadata  over time. It helps an organiza-
tion maintain  transparency  and control  over its data assets. Capabilities  
such as version  control,  logging,  and visualizations  support  realizing  
this feature.Sensitive  data detection . The ease of identifying  sensitive  data in data 
sets can help organizations  to accurately  implement  the procedures  for 
protecting  personal  data. Cataloging  tools provide  built-in  capabilities  
to find and label sensitive  data explicitly,  for example,  by assigning  a 
specific  tag or flag to data fields containing  sensitive  information,  such 
as social security  numbers  or health information  (Aggour  et al., 2017 ; 
Shastri et al., 2020 ; Cherradi  et al., 2023 ).
Roles and responsibilities  (Pingos and Andreou , 2023 ; Sawadogo  and 
Darmont , 2021b ; Cronholm  et al., 2017 ; Seabolt et al., 2018 ; Shastri et al., 
2020 ; Shanmugam  and Seshadri , 2016 ; Dong et al., 2016 ; Dolhopolov  
et al., 2023 ; Stansberry  et al., 2020 ). A data governance  structure  
typically  consisted  of a set of roles such as data stewards,  data owners,  
domain  owners,  data scientists,  and data platform  administrators  (Eich-
ler et al., 2022 ). Each role may be given specific  permissions  for 
viewing,  editing,  and managing  assets. Data catalog  tools should sup-
port defining  these roles and their responsibilities.  This feature ensures  
that data assets and metadata  are properly  governed  and maintained  by 
the right individuals.  In data governance  workflows,  such as review-
ing and approving  data, appropriate  responsibilities  can be assigned  
to specific  roles to ensure the right stakeholders  are notified.  Also, 
governance  policies  (e.g., data protection  and retention)  can be defined  
and enforced  so that only specific  roles can perform  particular  tasks 
(e.g., accessing  sensitive  data).
Task automation  and standardization  (Yang , 2023 ; Shraga and Miller , 
2023 ; Eichler et al., 2022 ; Cronholm  et al., 2017 ; Seabolt et al., 2018 ; 
Shastri et al., 2020 ; Vries et al., 2022 ; Dong et al., 2016 ; Dolhopolov  et al., 
2023 ; Stansberry  et al., 2020 ). Data catalogs  may support  automating  
tasks such as creating  and reviewing  data governance  policies,  granting  
data access, and creating  triggers  and templates  in a standardized  way. 
Enforcing  consistent  and standardized  processes  for managing  data 
assets can reduce manual  errors, improve  compliance,  and enhance  
efficiency.
Rules and policies  (Eichler et al., 2022 ; Cronholm  et al., 2017 ; Sawadogo  
and Darmont , 2021b ; Hai et al., 2016 ; Seabolt et al., 2018 ; MiliĆ et al., 
2021 ). Some tools provide  features  to define rules and policies  to 
enforce  data governance  and compliance.  For instance,  data quality 
rules, data integrity,  retention  (e.g., triggering  deletion  workflows  once 
data reaches  the end of its life-cycle),  usage of data (Shanmugam  
and Seshadri , 2016 ; Dolhopolov  et al., 2023 ; Cherradi  et al., 2023 ; 
Stansberry  et al., 2020 ) and compliance  rules (e.g., GDPR compli-
ance) (Shastri et al., 2020 ; Dong et al., 2016 ) can be enforced  and 
automated  within the data catalog.  Data assets that violate these rules 
are flagged,  facilitating  the identification  and review of the problematic  
data.
Data quality  assessment  (Yang , 2023 ; Sousa et al., 2023 ; Hai et al., 
2016 ; Eichler et al., 2021 , 2022 ; Cherradi  et al., 2023 ). Assessing  the 
quality of a dataset  involves  measuring  a set of quality dimensions  such 
as accuracy  (a measure  of the degree to which data correctly  reflects  
real-world  events or objects),  uniqueness  (a measure  of a fraction  of 
duplicate  data), and completeness  (a measure  of a fraction  of missing  
data) (Ehrlinger  and Wöß , 2022 ; Ustunboyacioglu  et al., 2024 ). Data 
catalog  tools should be able to compute  data quality metrics  on the 
data they manage  and store the calculated  metrics  as additional  meta-
data (Hai et al., 2021 ; Kumar et al., 2024 ; Sawadogo  and Darmont , 
2021b ; Sinthong  et al., 2021 ; Abolhassani  et al., 2018 ; Boukhers  et al., 
2023 ; Wang et al., 2016 ). This evaluation  is performed  by applying  
quality checks and rules, and automatically  flagging  quality issues 
if found. For instance,  completeness  checks can be performed  when 
crawling  data sources  to ensure no duplicate  rows exist.
Data risk (Hai et al., 2021 ; Yang , 2023 ; Aggour et al., 2017 ). This 
feature refers to analyzing  and assessing  the risks associated  with data 
assets, including  compliance  risks, regulation  risks, security  risks, and 
quality issues (Hai et al., 2021 ; Yang , 2023 ; Aggour  et al., 2017 ; The Journal  of Systems  & Software  230 (2025)  112584

Shastri et al., 2020 ; Abolhassani  et al., 2018 ; Wang et al., 2016 ). Data 
catalogs  may offer components  that help identify  such risks, generate  
reports,  and remediate  risks, usually  by integrating  them with data 
risk management  tools. For instance,  a catalog  could provide  insights  
on who accessed  a database  containing  sensitive  data by monitoring  
access patterns,  or a lineage  graph could show that sensitive  data are 
shared with a third-party  organization,  highlighting  potential  risks with 
third-party  data sharing.  Also, data catalogs  can help ensure data are 
compliant  with regulations  such as GDPR by enforcing  governance  
rules (Cronholm  et al., 2017 ; Eichler et al., 2022 ).
4.5. Collaboration
Many data catalogs  allow the various  stakeholders  of metadata  
to interact  with each other and provide  feedback  on the data assets 
available  on the catalog  (Eichler et al., 2022 ; Shanmugam  and Seshadri , 
2016 ; Stansberry  et al., 2020 ; Eichler et al., 2021 ). For instance,  a data 
consumer  can comment  on a data asset’s quality,  and the data provider  
can check the comment  and act accordingly.  Stakeholders,  such as data 
users and business  analysts,  can make the data catalog  and stored assets 
more valuable  and reliable  by rating and commenting  on data assets, 
actively  engaging  with data assets owners,  data stewards,  and other 
actors via integrated  chat services,  and sharing  documentation  about 
data assets (Bratt et al., 2017 ; Anon , 2024d ). This process  allows all the 
users to contribute  to managing  the data catalog  and providing  proof of 
the benefits  of its adoption  (de Oliveira  et al., 2022 ; Iury et al., 2016 ; 
Parra-Moyano  et al., 2024 ).
Texting  (Eichler et al., 2022 ; Shanmugam  and Seshadri , 2016 ). The tex-
ting feature of a data catalog  allows users to send text-based  messages  
within a data catalog  environment.  It can enhance  real-time  interaction  
between  catalog  users.
Reviewing  (Eichler et al., 2022 ; Iury et al., 2016 ; Shanmugam  and 
Seshadri , 2016 ). This feature allows users to write detailed  evaluations,  
comments,  or critiques  about data assets. Compared  with rating, it 
enables  users to provide  qualitative  feedback.
Rating (Eichler et al., 2022 ; Shanmugam  and Seshadri , 2016 ). The 
rating feature allows users to rank data assets within a catalog  quanti-
tatively,  typically  using a 5-point  scale. It is a community-driven  metric 
for assessing  a data asset’s perceived  value and quality.
Sharing  (Eichler et al., 2022 , 2021 ; Stansberry  et al., 2020 ). Some 
data catalogs  allow users to share datasets  via directly  accessible  links, 
making  it easy to provide  access to data quickly.  These links can often 
be set with different  levels of access permissions.  Users can collaborate  
more effectively  with colleagues  on data projects  by enabling  direct 
data sharing.
4.6. Maturity  levels
To better understand  the evolution  of metadata  management  capa-
bilities in contemporary  data ecosystems,  we propose  a model with 
three maturity  levels for data catalogs.  These levels are designed  to 
reflect the progressive  enrichment  of features,  aligned  with emerg-
ing use cases in data mesh, data lakehouse,  and data lake architec-
tures (Travkin , 2024 ; Corcho  et al., 2024 ). Importantly,  these levels 
are not intended  to measure  tool reliability  or adoption,  but rather 
to categorize  tools based on functionality  and alignment  with modern  
metadata  needs.
As illustrated  in Fig.  6, each level corresponds  to a set of capabilities  
that build upon one another.  Level 1 represents  foundational  metadata  
harvesting  and visibility.  At this stage, data catalogs  focus on indexing  
and exposing  datasets,  typically  through  basic features  like connectivity  
to data sources,  a standard  data dictionary,  and simple search func-
tionality.  These capabilities  enable organizations  to take initial steps towards  centralizing  metadata  and making  it discoverable,  especially  
for technical  users.
Level 2 introduces  more advanced  metadata  management  capabili-
ties focused  on data governance  and collaborative  use cases. Catalogs  
at this level provide  richer context  around  data assets by incorporating  
business  glossaries,  profiling  capabilities,  and data quality indicators.  
They also address  organizational  governance  requirements  such as 
role-based  permissions,  lineage  tracking,  and standardized  definitions.  
This maturity  level also facilitates  improved  communication  and trust 
between  business  and technical  stakeholders.
Level 3 marks the transition  to strategic  business  value creation  
from enterprise  data, where metadata  becomes  the foundation  for data 
productization  and advanced  data governance.  Catalogs  at this level 
allow organizations  to treat data as a product,  offering  features  such 
as curated  and versioned  data packages,  collaborative  tools for users to 
annotate  or comment,  and mechanisms  to assess data quality or privacy  
risks. Integration  with internal  or external  data marketplaces  becomes  
possible,  enabling  governed  data exchange  and monetization.  This ma-
turity level supports  scalable,  decentralized  architectures  and reflects  a 
high level of automation  and user engagement:  it is well-aligned  with 
the principles  of data mesh, as it supports  federated  governance  and 
promotes  shared understanding  of data across domains  (Dehghani  and 
Fowler , 2022 ).
The proposed  maturity  levels resonate  with broader  maturity  frame-
works found in the literature.  For instance,  Immuta’s  Data Marketplace  
Maturity  Model (Immuta , 2025 ) identifies  a trajectory  from basic access 
control  towards  optimized,  monetizable  data services.  Similarly,  Cor-
cho et al. (2024 ) propose  a maturity  model for semantic  artifact  cat-
alogs and provide  a detailed  breakdown  of 12 dimensions,  similar 
to our classification  framework.  Their model aims to help evaluate  
catalog  readiness  for supporting  semantic  interoperability  in complex  
research  infrastructures.  Their structured,  multi-dimensional  approach  
aligns conceptually  with our level-based  perspective.

6. RQ3: Mapping  the data catalog  features  to metadata  life-cycle  
management  steps
Metadata  life-cycle  management  is the process  of creating,  storing,  
updating,  and retiring  metadata.  Academic  and grey literature  provides  
several models  for data and metadata  life-cycle  management  (Sinaeep-
ourfard  et al., 2016a ; Holom et al., 2020 ; Potvin and Thompson , 2016 ; 
Government  of Canada , 2024 ; Anon , 2025 ; Barton and Robertson , 
2005 ; Sinaeepourfard  et al., 2016b ; Eichler et al., 2022 , 2021 ). In this 
section,  we consider  the model proposed  in Anon (2025 ), Government  
of Canada  (2024 ), which reflects  the findings  from academic  litera-
ture (Potvin and Thompson , 2016 ; Eichler et al., 2021 ; Holom et al., 
2020 ; Sinaeepourfard  et al., 2016a ). We use it to assess the extent to 
which data catalogs  support  metadata  life-cycle  activities.
Fig.  10 summarizes  the steps in the adopted  metadata  lifecycle  man-
agement  workflow  (Anon , 2025 ; Government  of Canada , 2024 ; Potvin 
and Thompson , 2016 ; Sinaeepourfard  et al., 2016a ). This process  aims 
to optimize  data/information  understanding,  maximize  its usage, and 
reduce time to manage  it. There are eight phases in the process.
Plan . The planning  phase is crucial for understanding  metadata  
requirements  and identifying  key stakeholders.  This involves  analyzing  
and documenting  metadata-related  challenges  within the organization,  
reviewing  existing  data governance  roles and processes,  and formulat-
ing a strategy  for implementing  a metadata  management  solution.  Ad-
ditionally,  this stage involves  reviewing  established  metadata  schemas  
to ensure their compatibility  with business  needs.
Construct,  Create,  Acquire . This phase involves  selecting  metadata  
schemas  and defining  technical  requirements.  It is important  to acquire  
a data dictionary  and business  glossary  to standardize  metadata  termi-
nology.  A key objective  is to establish  agreements  on how metadata  will 
be created,  maintained,  integrated,  and accessed.  Identifying  potential  
risks and issues is also part of this phase.
Commission,  Organize,  Store . Metadata  architecture  and gover-
nance frameworks  are established  in this stage, defining  organiza-
tional roles, responsibilities,  and quality assurance  processes.  Meta-
data are collected  from various  sources,  classified  based on sensitivity  
and criticality,  and stored in a structured  manner.  This stage also 
involves  documenting  policies  and procedures  related to metadata  gov-
ernance.  Collaboration  with relevant  business  units ensures  alignment  
with organizational  objectives.
Access . This stage ensures  that authorized  users and applications  
can access metadata  efficiently.  It includes  providing  tools for har-
vesting,  publishing,  interpreting,  and sharing  metadata.  The primary

platform  (Zaharia  et al., 2021 ). Similar  to the data lake architecture,  
the data lakehouse  architecture  uses low-cost  storage  (e.g., Hadoop  file

the lakehouse  architecture  is a data catalog  (called metastore)  to store 
table names, schemas,  and references  to each table’s specific  metadata.
7.3. Data mesh architecture
Dehghani  and Fowler (2022 ) defines  data mesh as:
‘‘a decentralized  sociotechnical  approach  to share, access, and manage  
analytical  data in complex  and large-scale  environments  within or across 
organizations’’
Data mesh decentralizes  data ownership,  assigning  it to individual  
business  domains  to enhance  flexibility  and scalability.  It treats data as 
a product,  with each domain  responsible  for the quality,  reliability,  and 
usability  of its data, including  defining  data access interfaces  and ensur-
ing data meets user needs. Data Mesh employs  a federated  governance  
model, striking  a balance  between  central policies  and domain-specific  
requirements  to achieve  tailored  governance  strategies.  This architec-
ture is organized  around  business  domains,  aligning  data management  
practices  with business  processes  and objectives  (Dehghani  and Fowler ,

7.4. Data catalog positioning  within big data architectures
A data catalog  is a key component  in modern  data architectures  for 
metadata  management,  discovery,  and, partly, data governance,  which 
are essential  capabilities  for organizations  handling  large-scale  data 
across data lakes, data lakehouses,  and data mesh environments.  Fig. 
11 visualizes  the positioning  of the data catalog  within these big data 
architectures.
In data lakes, where raw and semi-structured  data is ingested  from 
multiple  sources,  a catalog  harvests  metadata,  enriches  them with 
business  context,  and ensures  discoverability,  preventing  the system 
from turning  into a data swamp.  It classifies  and structures  datasets,  en-
abling analysts  and data engineers  to locate relevant  data easily while 
enforcing  access control,  data lineage,  and compliance  policies  (Jahnke  
and Otto, 2023a ).
In data lakehouses,  a data catalog  enhances  functionalities  beyond  
basic metadata  storage.  A global e-commerce  company,  for instance,  
can utilize the Data Source Connectivity  feature in a catalog  to access 
real-time  inventory  data from warehouses  worldwide,  ensuring  consis-
tent and reliable  reporting  across regions.  The catalog  also plays a role 
in automated  governance,  defining  roles and responsibilities  for differ-
ent teams, enforcing  data integrity  constraints,  and enabling  versioning  and auditing  of transactional  changes.  Additionally,  it facilitates  self-
service access, as seen in marketing  teams that use the collaboration,  
search, and data dictionary  features  of the catalog  to retrieve  campaign  
performance  data without  requiring  IT intervention.
In data mesh architectures,  a data catalog  serves as a centralized  
registry  for distributed  data products,  ensuring  discoverability,  inter-
operability,  and compliance  with governance  across domains.  Each 
business  unit can maintain  its own glossary,  classifications,  and poli-
cies while still adhering  to enterprise-wide  governance  frameworks.  
Features  such as automated  metadata  standardization,  policy-driven  
access controls,  and lineage  tracking  enable teams to govern their data 
products  autonomously  while maintaining  consistency  throughout  the 
organization.
The diagram  (Fig.  11) further highlights  how a data catalog  inte-
grates with data sources,  governance  frameworks,  analytics  tools, and 
security  policies,  creating  a structured  data management  ecosystem  
across data architectures.

8.1. Implications  for practitioners
Our findings  can help organizations  systematically  choose a data 
catalog  tool based on their big data architectures  and metadata  man-
agement  use cases. They also help tool developers  identify  features  that 
enable them to gain competitive  advantages.
8.1.1. Selecting  the right data catalog tool
We identified  24 fine-grained  and 5 high-level  features  that can be 
used to compare  and assess the maturity  of data catalog  tools (see 
Sections  4, 5, and 4.6). Out of the 39 tools we analyzed,  only one 
provides  all the features  we identified,  while only five contain  more 
than 90% of the fine-grained  features.  Considering  the vast number  
of data catalog  tools available  on the market,  our findings  enable 
practitioners  to make informed  decisions  when choosing  a particular  
tool for their metadata  use cases.
The tools we studied  vary in their capabilities  and maturity.  Most 
tools offer the core capabilities  necessary  to build a data inventory,  
including  data source connectivity,  dataset  search, dataset  documen-
tation, data lineage,  classification,  and detection  of sensitive  data. 
However,  limited  tools support  key data governance  features  (e.g., data 
quality assessment  and data dictionary)  and collaboration  features  
(e.g., commenting,  reviewing,  and texting).  Moreover,  the maturity  of 
a given feature can also vary, for example,  the number  and type of data 
sources  supported  in the data source connectivity  feature and natural  
language-based  search queries  in the search feature.  In this respect,  
practitioners’  work in harnessing  our results should probably  start from 
feasibility  studies and sufficiently  limited  proof-of-concept  pilots that 
determine  baseline  values for the above.
8.1.2. Integrating  data catalogs  into organizational  big-data  architectures
Our findings  include  the mappings  between  data architectures  and 
catalog  features  (see Section  7). They can help organizations  that 
need to choose a cataloging  tool to implement  the metadata-related  
requirements  of their data architectures.  However,  organizations  must 
carefully  consider  the potential  mismatches  between  the quality re-
quirements  expected  by a particular  data architecture  and what a 
specific  deployment  of a cataloging  tool can offer. For instance,  the data 
mesh architecture,  which revolves  around  data products,  defines  new 
organizational  roles and responsibilities,  shifting  data ownership  to the 
data producer.  In such a scenario,  an all-in-one  data catalog,  such as a 
data marketplace,  is not necessarily  the best solution,  as it undermines  
decentralization,  one of the pillars of data mesh (Dehghani  and Fowler , 
2022 ; Goedegebuure  et al., 2024 ).
8.1.3. Guidelines  for effectively  managing  organizational  metadata
Our results demonstrate  the complexity  and interdependence  of 
data catalog  capabilities  required  for effective  metadata  lifecycle  man-
agement  within an organization  (see Section  6). The distribution  of 
high-level  and fine-grained  features  at each lifecycle  stage underscores  
the need for a holistic  approach  to metadata  management,  rather 
than relying on isolated  tools. For example,  the governance  feature 
is present  in nearly every stage, emphasizing  that effective  oversight,  
compliance,  and risk management  must be integrated  throughout  the 
entire lifecycle.  The consistent  appearance  of the metadata  augmen-
tation as a feature across stages suggests  a need for adaptive  and 
evolving  metadata,  which are continuously  enriched  to meet changing  
organizational  needs. We also observed  that the collaboration  features  
are notably  concentrated  in the access and use metadata  lifecycle  
stages. However,  the effectiveness  of these features  depends  on their 
integration  with other lifecycle  stages. Automation  and standardization  
also emerge  as crucial capabilities  that support  the scalability  and 
operational  efficiency  of metadata  management  within an organization.  
Catalog  features,  such as task automation,  metadata  standardization,  
and machine  learning  integration,  can help organizations  manage  the 
growing  volume  and complexity  of their data assets. These features  
reduce the manual  effort required  for metadata  management,  allowing  
catalogs  to scale with the organization’s  data needs.8.1.4. Tool feature prioritization
Our research  enables  data catalog  tool developers  to evaluate  the 
maturity  of their tools in terms of their support  for the requirements  
of modern  big data architectures  and holistic  metadata  management  
processes  within organizations  (Section  4.6). For example,  features  such 
as real-time  data integration,  dynamic  schema  evolution,  data product,  
and data quality assessment  are underrepresented  in the tools we ana-
lyzed. By closing these gaps, these tools can expand  their capabilities  
to meet the diverse  needs of various  use cases within organizations.
8.2. Research  challenges  and the road ahead
Our results provide  insights  into the potential  research  directions  
related to data cataloging.  We identified  three key directions:  collabo-
rative and AI-driven  capabilities  of data catalogs,  data governance  in 
emerging  big data architectures,  and tool interoperability.
8.2.1. Advanced  data catalog features
We observed  that data catalogs  employ  AI to implement  features  
such as contextual  metadata  enrichment,  metadata  search and recom-
mendation,  and predictive  data governance.  These features  would make 
data catalogs  more proactive,  helping  organizations  anticipate  data 
needs, recommend  relevant  datasets,  and surface  insights  tailored  to 
user context  (Petrik et al., 2023 ).
Research  on the collaboration  features  of data catalogs,  such as 
texting,  sharing,  reviewing,  and rating, can assess how they impact 
teamwork  and data usage, and identify  ways to enhance  their ef-
fectiveness  and integration.  Insights  into user behavior,  preferences,  
and collaboration  barriers  could drive the development  of more im-
pactful,  integrated  collaborative  functionalities  within data catalog  
environments  (Jahnke  and Otto, 2023b ; Labadie  et al., 2020 ).
An important  aspect that emerged  but remains  underexplored  is the 
maturity  of data catalogs.  Literature  offers limited  guidance  on assess-
ing or defining  it (Immuta , 2025 ; Corcho  et al., 2024 ; Travkin , 2024 ). 
As part of our future work, we plan to investigate  this concept  further 
through  interviews  with practitioners  and organizations  actively  using 
data catalogs,  to identify  practical  indicators  and stages of maturity.
8.2.2. Data governance  in modern  data architectures
With the rise of decentralized  architectures,  governance  across dis-
tributed  data domains  presents  new challenges,  including  inconsistent  
policies  and standards,  global data visibility,  compliance  enforcement  
and auditing,  and operational  overhead.  The researchers  could focus 
on federated  governance  models  that ensure consistent  data policies,  
central visibility,  and compliance  monitoring  while respecting  domain  
autonomy  (Dehghani  and Fowler , 2022 ).
8.2.3. Tool interoperability
As organizations  increasingly  adopt hybrid and multi-cloud  strate-
gies, there is a growing  need for interoperability  across data catalogs  
from different  vendors  or platforms.  Research  could address  the devel-
opment  of standardized  metadata  protocols,  APIs, and data exchange  
formats  that enable seamless  interoperability  between  catalogs  (Jahnke  
and Otto, 2023b ).
9. Threats  to validity
During our study, we identified  several threats to validity.  We 
addressed  or mitigated  most of them to ensure the reliability  and ro-
bustness  of our findings.  We divided  these threats into three categories:  
internal,  external,  and construct  validity  (Wohlin  et al., 2012 ).The Journal  of Systems  & Software  230 (2025)  112584

Internal  validity.  This validity  concerns  the validity  of the methods  
employed  to select and analyze  the literature  sources  and data catalog  
tools. A potential  threat is the risk of missing  relevant  literature  sources  
and tools due to deficiencies  in the search process.  As discussed  in 
Section  3, we systematically  formulated  the search queries  based on 
research  questions.  Moreover,  we conducted  searches  on the digital 
libraries  and search engines  used by existing  literature  review studies.  
We applied  inclusion/exclusion  and quality assessment  criteria  to select 
the most suitable  sources  from peer-reviewed  journals,  conferences,  and 
grey literature.  Researchers’  personal  biases could lead to misinterpre-
tation of data catalog  features  and influence  the analysis.  To mitigate  
this threat, we implemented  a systematic  coding process,  with regular  
reviews  among authors,  to maintain  consistency  and objectivity.  We de-
veloped  a clear and consistent  framework  through  iterative  refinement.  
We included  grey literature  sources  and official tools documentation  to 
avoid oversampling  positive  findings  on data catalogs,  to incorporate  
various  perspectives.
External  validity.  Threats  to external  validity  can challenge  the gener-
alizability  of our findings.  A potential  external  threat is the evolution  
of data catalog  tools and grey literature  sources,  which can impact the 
longevity  of some of our findings.  To partially  mitigate  this risk, we 
attempted  to identify  data catalog  features  that can cover the entire 
metadata  management  lifecycle,  which has been standardized  and 
adopted  by both practitioners  and researchers.  Another  potential  threat 
is excluding  literature  sources  and tools that provide  new insights.  As 
discussed  above, we systematically  searched  for sources  and tools to 
mitigate  this issue.
Another  potential  threat to the validity  of our findings  is the limited  
exploration  of the maturity  of data catalog  implementations.  While 
the concept  appears  relevant  and potentially  impactful,  the reviewed  
literature  provides  limited  information  or frameworks  for assessing  
maturity  levels across different  organizational  contexts.  As a result, our 
current  understanding  of this dimension  remains  preliminary.  In future 
work, we plan to address  this gap through  follow-up  interviews  with 
practitioners  and adopters  of data catalogs.
Construct  validity.  We provided  explicit  definitions  for each term to 
avoid misunderstandings  about key concepts  that could impact the 
accuracy  of our mappings.  Incorrect  mapping  of features  to metadata  
life-cycle  management,  maturity  levels, and big data architecture  re-
quirements  may lead to flawed insights.  To mitigate  this threat, the first 
two authors  of the paper collaboratively  created  and refined  mappings  
over multiple  interactions.  When disagreements  occurred,  the other 
authors  were involved  in resolving  them. The subjective  nature of 
qualitative  data analysis  can lead to inaccuracies.  To address  this issue, 
we employed  triangulation  by cross-referencing  findings  from multiple  
data sources  to validate  interpretations.
10. Conclusion
This study aimed to identify  the features  of modern  data catalogs  
and their roles within metadata  life-cycle  management  and metadata-
driven big-data  architectures,  such as data mesh, data lake, and data 
lakehouse.  With this intention,  we conducted  an MLR (Multivocal  
Literature  Review),  analyzing  86 academic  and grey literature  articles,  
as well as the documentation  of catalog  tools.
First, we employed  the selected  literature  to develop  a classification  
framework  for data catalogs  comprising  five high-level  features  and 24 
fine-grained  ones. Next, based on those features,  we identified  three 
maturity  levels for data catalogs.  We analyzed  and classified  39 tools 
using the identified  features  and maturity  levels. Finally,  we mapped  
the features  to the metadata  management  life cycle steps and modern  
big data architecture  requirements  to understand  the potential  usage of 
data catalogs  in metadata  management  and data architectures.
Our results indicate  that data catalogs  have progressed  beyond  basic 
data indexing  and search capabilities,  incorporating  essential  functions  for data governance,  quality assessment,  and collaboration  to meet the 
complex  demands  of modern  data architectures  and metadata  use cases. 
Despite  their advancements,  gaps remain,  particularly  in supporting  
user collaboration,  data and metadata  governance,  tool interoperabil-
ity, and utilizing  AI to enhance  data catalog  features.  These limitations  
present  opportunities  for future research  to improve  data catalog  capa-
bilities.  Addressing  these areas will be crucial as organizations  continue  
to scale their data operations  and seek more decentralized,  agile data 
management  solutions.
As data catalogs  evolve,  they will play an increasingly  central role in 
enabling  organizations  to derive value from data by providing  a unified,  
governed,  and accessible  metadata  environment.  This will empower  
data-driven  decision-making  and foster collaboration  across diverse  
data landscapes.
