Deep Research Agents increasingly automate survey generation, yet whether they match human
experts atretrievingessential papers andorganizingthem into expert-like taxonomies remains
unclear. Existing benchmarks emphasize writing quality or citation correctness, while standard
clustering metrics ignore hierarchical structure. We introduceTaxoBench, a benchmark of 72
highly cited LLM surveys with expert-authored taxonomy trees and 3,815 papers mapped to paper
categories.TaxoBenchevaluates (1) retrieval via Recall/Precision/F1, and (2) organization at a
leaf level(paper-to-category assignment) and ahierarchy levelvia two new metrics: Unordered
Semantic Tree Edit Distance (US-TED/US-NTED) and Semantic Path Similarity (Sem-Path). Two
modes are supported: Deep Research (topic-only, end-to-end) and Bottom-Up (expert paper set
provided, organization-only). To distinguish disagreement with a single expert reference from
genuine model failure, we explicitly partition findings into capability-based (reference-free) and
alignment-based (reference-dependent) groups. Evaluating 7 Deep Research Agents and 12 frontier
LLMs reveals a dual bottleneck. On the capability side, the best agent retrieves only 20.92%
of expert-cited papers, and 1,000 model taxonomies show 75.9% sibling overlap, 51.2% MECE
violations, and 83.4% structural imbalance, all detectable without any reference. On the alignment
side, all 12 LLMs converge toSem-Path28–29%, well below 47–58% achieved by three independent
human-annotator groups on the same paper sets. Our benchmark is publicly available at https:
//github.com/KongLongGeFDU/TaxoBench.

hierarchical taxonomy that becomes the backbone of the survey. Current benchmarks focus on writing
quality, factuality, or citation correctness (Eldifrawi et al., 2024; Wadden et al., 2020), rather than

2.TaxoBench
Ataxonomyconsists of (i) acategory hierarchythat organizes topics into a tree, and (ii) apaper-to-
category assignmentmapping each paper to a leaf category.TaxoBenchextracts such taxonomies
from published expert surveys and uses them as references to evaluate whether agents can replicate
this process. Formal definitions appear in Section 3; Table 1 summarizes the dataset.
Survey collection.Starting from 200+ candidate LLM-related surveys, we apply four sequential
filters: (1) ataxonomy filterthat retains only surveys with explicit taxonomy figures, identified by
caption-based search; (2) animpact filterbased on citation counts, with the final set averaging 354.5
citations; (3) ascope filterthat excludes overly broad surveys without a coherent expert taxonomy;

4.1. RQI: Can current agents replicate expert-level literature discovery?
Finding 1: Deep Research agents exhibit severe retrieval bottlenecks.Table 2 reveals that current
agents fail to retrieve core literature at scale. The best-performing agent, o3, achieves only 20.92%
Recall and 24.41% F1, missing nearly 80% of expert-cited papers, and four of the seven agents fall
below 10% F1. Precision is similarly limited: Grok leads with 29.35%, indicating that retrieved sets
are dominated by peripheral rather than foundational work. This is a capability-based finding that
does not depend on the expert taxonomy: any synthesis pipeline built on top of these agents inherits
an upper bound of ≤21%Recall on the papers experts deem essential, which directly constrains
downstream organization.

5. Related Work
Deep-research-aided survey generation.Most pipelines adopt a structure-then-content scheme:
AutoSurvey (Wang et al., 2024) uses two-stage generation, Hu et al. (2024) build taxonomies
from citation networks, and SurveyForge (Yan et al., 2025) couples human-curated outlines with

tests this on expert-authored references.
Deep-research evaluation.Existing benchmarks target multi-hop retrieval (Ai-ling et al., 2025;
Chen et al., 2025), web browsing (Deng et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025), and report generation (Du
et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a). Decompositions of Deep Research (Shi et al., 2025) are complemented
byFINDER(Zhang et al., 2025a) on evidence integration andResearchRubrics(Sharma et al.,
2025) on prompt–rubric-based human-judged reports.TaxoBenchis complementary: it isolates

6. Conclusion
We introduceTaxoBench, an expert-grounded benchmark that decouples retrieval from hierarchical
organization via three hierarchy-aware metrics (US-TED,US-NTED,Sem-Path), with all findings
partitioned intocapability-basedandalignment-baseddiagnostics. Evaluating 7 Deep Research Agents
and 12 frontier LLMs reveals a sharpdual bottleneck: a 20.92% Recall ceiling, reference-independent
defects (75.9% sibling overlap, 51.2% MECE violations, 83.4% structural imbalance), andSem-Path
convergence to 28–29%, well below 47–58% from three independent human-annotator groups.
TaxoBenchthus provides a reusable target for tracking progress toward credibly automated scientific
synthesis.
Limitations
We discuss limitations along five dimensions, indicating where each is mitigated empirically and
where it remains an open direction.
Alignment vs. capability.The headline alignment-based numbers in Findings 3–5 (Sem-Pathand
US-TED/US-NTED) measure how well a model reproducesthis particularexpert’s organization, not
the absolute defensibility of model taxonomies. To avoid conflating these two questions, we separate
alignment-based from capability-based diagnostics throughout the paper, as discussed in Section 3.4
and Finding 6. The reference-independent defects in Finding 6 (75.9% sibling overlap, 51.2% MECE
violations, and 83.4% structural imbalance) bound the capability gap, but a fully reference-free,
expert-judged “defensibility” study at scale remains future work.
Single-reference evaluation and taxonomy subjectivity.Each survey contributes one published
expert taxonomy; multiple equally valid expert taxonomies may exist for the same topic. We mitigate
the resulting subjectivity in two ways: (i) we report three independent human-construction baselines
in Appendix C.4, all of which score 47–58%Sem-Pathon the same paper sets that models score 28–
29% on, ruling out the interpretation that models are simply exploring valid alternatives; and (ii) we
report capability-based defects that are reference-independent. A multi-reference extension analogous
to multi-reference BLEU, where multiple expert taxonomies per topic yield a richer alignment target,
is a natural and important direction for future work.
Domain scope.The benchmark is constructed from 72 highly cited LLM-related surveys. While these
span eight subdomains within AI/ML, the absolute numerical findings may not transfer unchanged
to fields with different citation cultures, such as biology, physics, or social sciences. As preliminary
cross-domain evidence, we evaluate Claude-4.5-Sonnet on two interdisciplinary surveys spanning
finance and medical domains and observe the same qualitative pattern of high homogeneity, moderate
ARI, andSem-Path ≈30%; see Appendix G.5. ExtendingTaxoBenchto non-AI/ML disciplines is
straightforward in principle (our four-stage pipeline and metric definitions are domain-agnostic), and
doing so will broaden the empirical scope of the dual-bottleneck claim.

C. Error Analysis and Case Studies
C.1. Summary of Four-Dimensional Error Analysis
To gain deeper insights into the limitations of current LLMs in taxonomy generation, we conducted a
fine-grained error analysis on 1,000 sampled taxonomies. We categorized structural and semantic
failures into four distinct dimensions:
•Semantic Coverage:Evaluates the completeness and relevance of the taxonomy. Errors include
omitting core subfields recognized by experts or including hallucinatory/irrelevant branches.
•SiblingOrganization:Assessesthequalityofnodeswithinthesamehierarchicallevel. Common
failuresinvolvesemanticredundancy(violatingtheMECEprinciple)orinconsistentclassification
criteria.
•Hierarchical Logic:Examines the validity of parent-child relationships. Errors include abstrac-
tion mismatches (e.g., placing a high-level concept under a specific method) or misclassification
of paper categories.
•Structural Topology:Analyzes the overall shape and balance of the tree. This dimension cap-
tures issues such as structural imbalance, excessive depth, or insufficient granularity compared
to human-curated benchmarks.
Figure 7 summarizes the top 3 error types for each category. The data reveals thatStructural
Imbalance(83.4%) andMissing Core Branches(77.5%) are the most prevalent issues, highlighting the
challenge models face in maintaining a global perspective on domain structures.

H. Decomposition Analysis and Mechanistic Probe
H.1. Decomposition by Depth and Size
To diagnose whether the organization bottleneck arises fromscale(large paper sets) ordepth(deep
hierarchical reasoning), we decompose Bottom-Up results by paper count and by hierarchy depth.
Two clear patterns emerge: (i)US-TEDgrows monotonically with paper count, indicating that
more papers drive structural divergence; (ii)Sem-Pathdrops sharply with depth—e.g., Qwen3-Max:
43.5→34.7→25.6%as depth grows from 3 to ≥5—indicating that the bottleneck specifically lies
in deep hierarchical reasoning rather than shallow categorization.
H.2. Constrained-𝐾Mechanistic Probe
To causally test whether granularity selection drives the over-segmentation pattern observed in
Finding 4, we ran a Constrained- 𝐾experiment on 15 sampled surveys: GPT-5 was prompted to
produce exactly 𝐾paper categories (where 𝐾is the expert category count). This isolates granularity
from semantic-assignment ability.
Two effects are observed (Table 21): (i) ARI improves substantially (13.05% →20.54%, +7.49
pp), confirming that granularity selection is a major source of error; (ii) however, ARI plateaus at
20.54%, far below the human ceiling, and homogeneitydrops(73.84% →62.87%), revealing that the
previously high homogeneity was largely the by-product of over-segmentation rather than genuine
semantic understanding.
We also report the average paper-category counts produced under the three input settings
(Bottom-Up): richer input causes models to produce more fine-grained categories, amplifying over-
segmentation (Table 22).
H.3. Metric–Human Correlation
We compute the Spearman correlation betweenSem-Pathand the LLM-as-Judge scores (which are
themselves validated against human evaluators with Cohen’s 𝜅=0.89, see Appendix B). In Deep
Research mode, where the 7 agents exhibit meaningful variance in performance,Sem-Pathachieves
near-perfect correlation with the average judge score (Table 23). In Bottom-Up mode, all 12 models
converge into a 1pp band onSem-Path, so rank-based correlations become unstable with only 6
unique data points; this instability is itself diagnostic of the convergence in Finding 5 rather than a
metric artefact.

annotation conducted entirely by a recruited team. All annotators are recruited experts holding Ph.D.

literature analysis. The primary annotation tasks encompassed: (1) systematically collecting survey

extracting hierarchical classification structures and mapping cited papers to corresponding paper
categories based on the full text, figures, and references, ultimately constructing an unambiguous
