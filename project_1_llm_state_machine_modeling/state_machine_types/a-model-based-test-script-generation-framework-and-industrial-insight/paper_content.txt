Abstract
Model-based testing (MBT) generates test cases through a model representing the software under test (SUT). The generated 
abstract test cases need to be transformed into concrete or executable test scripts. Despite the benefits offered by MBT, its 
industrial adoption is slow. This paper aims to propose a Model-Based Test scrIpt GenEration fRamework (TIGER) based 
on GraphWalker (GW), an open-source MBT tool, to evaluate the accuracy of generated test scripts to reflect real-world 
scenarios defined by the model, and to report on the findings of an industrial survey on MBT adoption. We have validated 
the robustness of the TIGER using an industrial case study from Alstom Rail AB, Sweden. We have injected faults into the 
model of the SUT based on three mutation operators to generate faulty test scripts. The aim of generating faulty test scripts 
is to produce failing test steps and to guarantee the absence of faults in the SUT. Moreover, we have also generated the test 
scripts using the correct version of the model and executed them to analyze the behavior of the generated test scripts in 
comparison with manually written test scripts. The experimental results show that the generated test scripts are executable, 
provide 100% requirements coverage, and can be used to uncover faults at the software-in-the-loop simulation level of system 
testing. Additionally, the analysis of the survey data reveals that MBT can address most of the identified testing challenges, 
but there remain certain barriers to its adoption.
Keywords Model-based testing · Test script generation · Case study · Industrial survey
Introduction
Safety-critical embedded system software is an essential 
part of modern cyberphysical systems (CPSs), and errors 
in CPS software can lead to catastrophic events. Software 
testing for safety-critical embedded system software needs 
to be thorough and effective. Research into Model-Based Testing (MBT) [1 ] has shown that it can be a resource-effi-
cient technique for generating test cases, test scripts, and 
test verdicts, as well as effective in terms of fault detection. 
MBT generates abstract test cases based on a model of the 
System Under Test (SUT) [2 ]. The abstract test cases can be 
transformed into concrete, executable test scripts that can 
eventually produce test verdicts. However, we have observed 
that this concretization step is not covered well enough in the 
literature or is discussed only in the context of web-based 
and mobile applications where somewhat mature script gen-
eration frameworks exist, such as Selenium and Appium. 
Thus, only a few papers (i.e. [3 , 4]) report on the details of 
the concretization step and even fewer have addressed it for 
embedded software testing at integration and system levels.
Despite the benefits that MBT offers in terms of test gen-
eration efficiency and fault effectiveness, the industrial adop-
tion of MBT is relatively slow [5 ]. Different factors such as 
lack of domain-specific and platform-dependent solutions, 
switching to a modeling paradigm, and lack of industrial 
success stories, etc., have been reported as key contribu-
tors to its gradual adoption in various studies (e.g., [5 , 6]).

However, to make a conscious decision about MBT adoption 
for particular contexts, it is crucial to gather and analyze 
empirical data, insights, and feedback from industrial prac-
titioners. Hence, we also find it interesting to investigate 
the challenges and perceptions of MBT among industrial 
practitioners to assess the level of its adoption and to under -
stand the factors contributing to or limiting the successful 
industrial adoption of MBT.
In this paper, we have developed a Model-Based Test 
scrIpt GenEration fRamework (TIGER)1 based on Graph-
Walker (GW),2 a freely-available MBT tool. The generated 
test scripts validate the functionality of sub-systems con -
trolled by the train control management system (TCMS) 
software developed by Alstom Rail, Sweden, AB. GW is uti-
lized for generating abstract test cases that traverse the nodes 
and edges of the Extended Finite State Machine (EFSM) 
model representing the SUT. The developed framework 
uses GW to generate abstract test cases, concretizes them 
by following the defined mapping rules, and generates test 
scripts in C# language. To ensure the credibility of TIGER-
generated test scripts capturing the intended functionalities 
defined in the model and identifying their potential weak -
nesses, we have analyzed the behavior of the generated test 
scripts by injecting faults into the model of a fire detec-
tion system controlled by the TCMS. The injected faults are 
based on the work on mutation operators by Belli et al. [7 ]. 
The test scripts are then generated from the correct and fault-
induced version of the models and executed at the software-
in-the-loop (SiL) execution platform at Alstom. The aim 
of introducing faults in the model is to generate faulty test 
scripts. These should fail when executed on the SUT to 
guarantee the absence of induced faults in the SUT with no 
known fault. We have also analyzed the input combinations 
generated by GW to evaluate the requirements coverage of 
test scripts. The results show that TIGER can generate test 
scripts that are readily executable on Alstom’s SiL platform 
and have the potential to uncover interaction faults at the 
sub-system level of TCMS testing.
The work presented in this paper is an extension of a prior 
contribution made at a conference [ 8]. In addition to the 
previous contribution, we have conducted an industrial sur -
vey by collecting qualitative and quantitative data in order 
to gain an understanding of the industrial viewpoint on the 
adoption of MBT and associated challenges. We have also 
discussed the indirect correlation between the survey find-
ings and the practical relevance of TIGER in an industrial 
setting. Furthermore, we have also extended the related work 
for an in-depth analysis of the factors contributing to slow MBT adoption reported in the previously conducted indus-
trial surveys and reports. The results of the industrial sur -
vey show the positive expectations of industrial practitioners 
from MBT in terms of solving the problem of unambiguous 
requirements through modeling, increased test coverage, and 
resource reduction through automatic test generation. It also 
uncovers the challenges influencing the industrial adoption 
of MBT.

Preliminaries
This section briefly introduces model-based testing, Graph-
Walker, model-based mutation testing, and qualitative data 
analysis, along with related work concerning model-based 
test generation techniques and industrial studies on MBT 
adoption.
Model‑Based Testing
Model-based testing [ 9] comprises mainly three distinct 
steps; (i) the creation of a model in conformance with the 
SUT and requirement specification, (ii) the generation of 
abstract and concrete test cases, and (iii) the execution of a 
test suite on the SUT to validate its behavior. Depending on 
test objectives and test artifacts, various modeling notations 
and languages (e.g., class diagrams, state machines, and 
finite automata) can be used to create MBT models repre-
senting the abstract view of the system’s functionalities and 
interaction of a SUT. These models serve as a foundation 
to facilitate abstract test case generation based on a specific 
generation algorithm and coverage criteria. Test generation 
algorithms are strategy-based methods such as random, com-
binatorial, or symbolic execution, which automatically cre-
ate the test cases systematically. On the other hand, coverage 
criteria determine the extent to which different functionali-
ties of a SUT need to be exercised (e.g., statement coverage, 
path coverage, etc.).
The abstract test cases generated from a model do not 
contain concrete execution information about the SUT, and 
hence, require an approach (i.e., transformation, adaptation, 1 TIGER is available at https:// github. com/ Muham madNo umanZ

transformation approach, abstract concepts of the models 
captured by the abstract test cases are directly converted into 
actionable and precise values for input data and expected

a manual written intermediary adapter code that contains 
a translation between the abstract test cases generated from 
the model and corresponding concrete actions executable 
on a SUT [9 ]. On the other hand, a mixed approach is a 
combination of transformation and adaptation approaches. It 
generates test inputs and expected results with precise imple-
mentation-specific values and utilizes a layer of adapter code 
to execute the tests on a SUT to produce test verdicts.
GraphWalker
GraphWalker (GW) is an open-source model-based testing 
tool that offers different generation algorithms (e.g., random, 
weight_random, quick_random, etc.) and stopping condi-
tions (e.g., edge_coverage, vertex_coverage, time_duration, 
etc.) along with online and offline test generation features. 
GW comes in three versions: GraphWalker studio, Graph-
Walker Command Line Interface (CLI), and GW4E (an 
Eclipse plugin) [10]. Our framework utilizes GW studio to 
create and validate models and GW CLI to generate abstract 
test cases.
GW studio provides a user-friendly web-based graphical 
modeling environment to create an explicit model of a SUT 
using Extended Finite State Machines (EFSM). An EFSM 
model in GW comprises extended and shared states, data 
variables, guard conditions, actions, and transitions. The 
extended states in an EFSM model incorporate dynamic 
data and variables. In contrast, shared states facilitate the 
reusability of states and traversing across different state 
machines to generate test scenarios. On the other hand, the 
data variables in an EFSM model hold values between states, 
guard conditions are boolean expressions depicting the logi-
cal conditions based on requirements of a SUT to influence 
the model’s behavior, and transitions represent the dynamic 
behavior and relation between the states dictated by guard 
conditions. GW studio also contains model simulation for 
the validation and analysis of the graphical model based on 
the provided generation algorithm and stopping condition.
GW CLI3 provides a command-line interface to generate, 
execute, and manage test cases supporting various formats 
used to define a model, i.e., GraphML and JSON. It allows 
a user to specify test generation parameters (i.e., generation algorithm and stopping condition) to generate test paths, 
execute test cases, enable online or offline features, and 
produce test coverage results and reports. It also provides 
different features to control the levels of details to be gener -
ated in test cases, e.g., the generation of test data along with 
test paths and unvisited elements in models. Furthermore, it 
can also be incorporated into industrial testing workflows, 
providing seamless integration with other development tools 
(e.g., Selenium)  [11].
Model‑Based Mutation Testing
Model-based mutation testing (MBMT) is a fault-based 
black box testing technique that involves generating or 
improving a test suite by making different versions of an 
original model when the source code of a SUT is inacces-
sible. These versions can also be referred to as faulty models 
or mutants. Each mutant contains a small systematic modi-
fication representing an error in the SUT based on a specific 
mutation operator [9 ]. After creating mutants, MBMT auto-
matically generates test cases containing the scenarios to dis-
tinguish the behavior of the original model and mutants [12]. 
We have used an instance of the MBMT approach proposed 
by Belli et al. [7 ] to assess the fault detection capabilities of 
the test suite generated by TIGER. In traditional MBMT, 
the generation of test cases aims to kill the non-equivalent 
mutants through an equivalence check between the original 
and mutated models [ 13], whereas Belli’s approach focuses 
on assessing the fault detection capabilities of a test suite 
generated from an original model in comparison to test 
suites generated from mutated ones.
In this approach, the behavioral model (M ) and its cor -
responding SUT are assumed to have already been vali-
dated by a test suite (T ) generated from M  through the MBT 
approach using a specific generation algorithm and cover -
age criterion. It involves creating mutants (Mﬁ) based on the 
proposed elementary mutation operators, i.e., insertion and 
omission, generating test suites (Tﬁ) based on Mﬁ using the 
similar test generation algorithm and coverage criterion as 
T, and comparatively analyzing the test results to evaluate 
the fault effectiveness of generated test suites. With respect 
to this approach, a mutant (Mf1) is said to be killed if the 
corresponding test suite (Tf1) produces failed test steps when 
executed on the SUT, otherwise, Mf1 is considered alive. 
Furthermore, alive mutants could indicate the presence of 
faults in the SUT that have not been detected by the test 
suite T.

objectives in step four. Finally, in the last step, a discus-

Related Work
Over the past few decades, researchers have been explor -
ing multiple techniques to automate the software testing 
process and MBT is one of them. Automation of software 
testing involves the generation of test cases and test scripts 
and the execution of test scripts to generate test verdicts. 
We have found numerous studies (e.g., [3 , 4, 16–24]) that 
have provided multiple solutions for automatically generat-
ing test artifacts (i.e. test cases and test scripts) using MBT 
techniques, particularly, with behavioral model. Moreover, 
various studies (i.e., [1 , 5, 6, 25]) have also explored the 
capabilities of MBT or reported the experience of industrial 
practitioners with respect to its applicability. In this section, 
we briefly summarize these studies and for a complete over -
view of MBT techniques in general, we refer the interested 
reader to several papers on the topic [26– 33]. We further -
more summarize studies describing the industrial adoption 
of MBT in “ Industrial Studies on the Adoption of Model-
Based Testing”.
Model‑Based Test Generation Techniques
A model-based solution has been presented by Mohacsi 
et al. [16] to address the testing challenges and to automati-
cally generate the test scripts for distributed Multi-Mission 
User Services (MMUS) systems. They developed the model 
representing the SUT, linked it with the GUI, and gener -
ated the test cases using a tool named ‘TEMPPO Designer’. 
The generation of executable test cases/scripts has been 
done with Micro Focus TestPartner. The results showed an improvement in test quality, removal of redundancies, 
reduction in test effort and increase in test efficiency. Wang 
et al. [17] proposed a model-based framework for Cloud API 
testing. It uses data modeling, individual API modeling, and 
API scenario modeling using directed graphs to generate test 
scripts containing test cases as well as test data. Another 
MBT framework has been presented by Marinescu et al. [18] 
to generate test cases and test scripts for embedded systems 
by extending EAST-ADL with timed automata semantics 
and also provided a chain of tools for automatically generat-
ing and executing the test script for code validation.
Xu et al. [3 ] proposed a novel automated model-based 
test script generation technique for functional and security 
testing. They used Petri-nets to model the control and data 
flow for the system’s functional dependencies. They also 
provided a mapping between the model elements and imple-
mentation constructs for test script generation and found that 
the proposed technique is significantly effective in terms of 
fault detection. An automated framework to generate test 
cases and scripts for Android applications based on sys -
tem sequence diagrams has been reported by Anbunathan 
et al. [19]. The experimental evaluation showed a significant 
improvement in bug detection along with better effective-
ness in terms of test coverage when compared with other 
methods. Similarly, a test script generation approach has 
been proposed by Bonfanti et al. [22]. They have proposed 
a tool that automatically generates the test scripts in C++ by 
translating the constructs of scenarios written in AVALLA 
language based on some defined rules.
Gupta et al. [20] and Moura et al. [21] developed model-
driven approaches for generating test cases and scripts based 
on Business Process Modelling Notations (BPMN) focused 
on functional testing for web-based applications. The empir -
ical evaluation not only showed the completeness and cor -
rectness of the proposed approaches but also turned out to be 
efficient in terms of performance. Similarly, a model-based 
approach for generating test cases and test scripts (accord-
ing to Robot Framework) from Requirements Specification 
Language (RSL) has been proposed by Paiva et al. [4 ]. They 
have also defined a mapping for RSL constructs and GUI 
elements for the concretization of test data in test scripts. 
The results showed that the use of RSL improved the qual-
ity of requirements and reduced the manual effort and time 
required to generate test artifacts.
Vanhecke et al. [23 ] developed a plugin to concretize 
abstract test cases by using a transformation and adaption 
approach. They have also provided the mapping of opera-
tions and verification with corresponding assertions and 
actions defined in abstract test cases to generate executable 
Python scripts in QTaste format. The results show that the 
plugin increased the generality of QTaste by raising the 
abstraction level of the SUT interface and the test API. Simi-
larly, a test reuse strategy to minimize the concretization

Industrial Studies on the Adoption of Model‑Based Testing
An industrial survey to assess the expectations of industrial 
practitioners from model-based testing has been presented 
by Kriebel et al. [25]. The results of the survey show a gen-
eral positive expectation of industrial practitioners in terms 
of high-quality test case generation. Based on the survey 
results, the authors also proposed a model-based methodol-
ogy and created a toolchain to improve the quality of test 
cases for industrial systems used by Bayerische Motoren 
Werke AG (BMW), a luxury vehicle manufacturer. Another 
interview survey with an aim to provide best practices guide-
lines for MBT has been reported by Alegroth et al. [6 ]. The 
results of the survey suggest 23 technical and non-technical 
guidelines for utilizing MBT at an organizational level. It 
also identified the need for more experience reports and suc -
cess stories for its successful adoption.
Binder et al. [1 ] conducted a series of follow-up sur -
veys to evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of MBT 
in comparison to the expectations of the practitioners with 
MBT. The results show the positive effects of diverse MBT 
approaches on the effectiveness and efficiency of testing 
activities, however, MBT fulfills only a fraction of the 
extremely high expectations of practitioners. The reported 
expectations include the efficient and early design of the 
system, managing the complexity of the system and efficient 
communication with stakeholders through modeling, and 
reusability of models for test generation. Similarly, Kholkar 
et al. [5 ] provided an experience report on deploying MBT 
for the user acceptance testing of industrial embedded sys-
tems. The results of the study show an increase of 20–30% 
efficiency in designing and planning test activities as well as 
generating high-quality and effective tests. It also provides 
evidence of a high potential to pay back the cost in the long 
term.
Research Gap
The reviewed model-based test generation approaches were 
often found inadequate to comprehensively address the chal-
lenges of state-dependent behavior, real-time constraints, 
and complex interactions between different components of 
embedded software, especially at the system level. Moreover, 
we have found only a few studies that provide high-level 
details of the concretization process to generate executable test scripts. Hence, we argue that the increasing interaction 
complexity of embedded software at the system level neces-
sitates proposing, developing, and validating a new testing 
framework.
Proposed Framework
The proposed Model-Based Test scrIpt GenEration fRame-
work (TIGER) is intended to provide cost and time-efficient 
solution to automatically test embedded software at the 
system level. The framework consists of three main parts 
(Fig.  1): 
1. Abstract test case generator
2. Test case concretizer
3. Test script generator.
In the text below, we describe each of these main parts of 
TIGER in detail with respective constituent steps.
Abstract Test Case Generator
The framework is based on GraphWalker (GW), which is 
used to generate abstract test cases from a model represent-
ing a SUT. These test cases do not explicitly contain the 
precise information about the input/output signals involved 
in the operation of the SUT and thus can not be executed 
directly on the SUT to verify the correctness of results [2 ]. 
GW uses a model file in JSON or GraphML format and 
generates the abstract test cases in the form of multiple test 
steps by traversing through the model elements (i.e. nodes 
and edges) using a generator and a stopping condition. It 
provides various options that could be used to generate dif-
ferent information about the model in test cases. For exam-
ple, one could generate test cases without including the test 
data (variables and their respective values). In this case, the 
test cases generated through GW can only contain the names 
of the traversed nodes and edges. On the other hand, options 
to include test data and filtering blocked elements could be 
used to include several pieces of information related to the 
model in test cases. However, in our case, the verbose  
feature for generating test cases is useful as it generates all 
the relevant details related to the model in test cases, such 
as initialized variables, actions, and properties of nodes and 
edges. This information, along with test paths, helps in iden-
tifying the combination of input values, expected outputs, 
and timing constraints to validate a system’s behaviour.

Test Case Concretizer
To execute the test cases on Alstom’s test execution plat-
form, we need to have concrete test cases, which contain the 
precise actions, data values, and signals’ names not present 
in the abstract test cases [2 ]. Our framework uses a mixed 
approach [9 ] for the concretization and execution of gener -
ated test scripts. The transformation of abstract test cases to 
concrete test cases is done while generating the test scripts, 
whereas the adaption layer is added at the execution level. 
The concretization of test cases includes the extraction of 
the test data from the actions (generated in a test step) from 
the JSON file and providing the system’s signals in an XML 
format, i.e. logical and technical signal names used by the 
TCMS, as shown in Fig.  3. Engineers at Alstom uses these 
logical signal names in requirements and test specifications 
at the initial levels of development and testing phases. Logi-
cal names are the initial names for signal representation, 
which are available to the developers and testers. However, 
these logical signal names must map to one or more techni-
cal signal names, where technical signal names are the actual 
signal names used by the SUT to execute operations. So, 
the last step in the concretization process includes rules to

Test Data Extractor
The test data extractor in the framework is responsible for 
reading the JSON file and extracting useful information from 
it such as model name, currentElement, currentElementID 
and actions containing the test data for test cases.
Signals’ Information Extractor
The signals’ information extractor in the framework is 
responsible for reading the XML file and extracting the 
information about each signal, such as the signal type (i.e. 
Input or Output), technical name(s) (i.e. primary and second-
ary technical names), and information related to the data 
type of its respective values.Mapping Rules

which not only preserve the continuity of test cases and tra-
versing order but also prevent information loss (Table  1). 
These rules work as a guideline for the framework to con-
cretize the test cases and to provide a mapping between the 
test data (consisting of the logical name of the signals as per 
the requirement specification of the SUT) and the technical 
name of the signals (used by the SUT in the real world) 
along with their respective generated values.

Test Script Generator
The test script generator uses the mapping rules, the data 
extracted from the JSON formatted test cases, the XML 
file containing logical and technical signal names, and the 
implementation details of the test scripts (i.e. format, classes 
and methods to be executed on the target test execution plat-
form) to generate executable test scripts. The generated test 
scripts contain two main steps for each test case: forcing 
the input signals and verifying the output signals within a 
specific time. Based on the signal type, the test script gen-
erator generates the scripts to force and verify the signals 
accordingly. An example of a generated test script is shown 
in Fig.  4 where line 1 of the code represents the method 
name, lines 2 and 3 are used to write the logs for actions of 
the test script, lines 4 and 5 are used to force the signals, line 
6 writes the log for verification steps, and lines 7 and 8 are 
used to validate the output signals within a timing constraint.
The generated test scripts can be executed on the test 
simulation platform for TCMS. However, for execution, the 
simulation platform also requires some libraries (specifically 
designed for the TCMS developed at Alstom) and a con-
figuration file containing information related to SUT, paths 
for storing test logs, etc. The test simulation platform, after 
executing the test scripts, generates test verdicts specifying 
the failed and passed test steps.
Study Design
This section provides an overview of the research method-
ology used to perform an experimental evaluation of the 
proposed framework along with the process of industrial 
survey planning and execution.
Objectives and Research Questions
The main objectives of this study are threefold. Firstly, 
developing the test script generation framework based on 
MBT principles for SiL testing of embedded software. Secondly, the generated test scripts are validated to ensure 
their accuracy in reflecting the behavior and conditions out -
lined in the model. Lastly, to gain insights into the possible 
industrial adoption of MBT. Based on these main objectives, 
we have defined the following research questions:
– RQ1: How accurately do the TIGER-generated test 
scripts reflect the behaviour of a SUT, and to what extent 
can they be used to detect faults?
– RQ 2: To what extent can industries adopt and benefit 
from model-based testing methodologies, and what are 
the key factors influencing its successful adoption?
Experimental Design (RQ1)
We performed the evaluation of generated test scripts using 
an industrial case study of a fire detection sub-system con-
trolled by the TCMS to address RQ1. Initially, we developed 
an EFSM model representing the SUT using requirements 
and test specifications. We have generated test cases and 
scripts with the TIGER and executed the test scripts on the 
SUT. We have also injected some faults in the model, and 
generated and executed test scripts to evaluate the framework 
in terms of fault detection from a conformance perspective.
SUT
The case selected for the evaluation belongs to an ongoing 
TCMS development project for the MOVIA vehicle product 
of Alstom’s family of metro cars. TCMS is the center of 
a distributed system, built upon an open standard IP tech-
nology to communicate between and control multiple sub-
systems (i.e. doors, air conditioning, ventilation, etc.) of the 
modern train. In discussions with Alstom, we have selected 
requirements of the SUT related to the fire detection sub-
system of TCMS [10]. TCMS uses a fire detection system for 
the indication of two types of fires in the driver’s cab: inter -
nal fire and external fire. The fire detection system uses two 
instances of a Fire Detection Control Unit (FDCU) device. 
Both FDCUs can have two states: slave and master. The 
fire detection system sends a signal to the TCMS about the 
status of fires depending on the state of each FDCU. Based 
on these signals, TCMS indicates internal or external fire.

Modelling of SUT
We have modeled the expected behavior of the aforemen-
tioned sub-system in GW studio by exploring and under -
standing the requirements and test specifications, as well as 
by getting continuous input from the testing team at Alstom. 
GW studio is one of the versions of GW that create and vali-
date the system’s EFSM model. The model created in GW 
Studio consists of nodes, edges, and guard conditions. Nodes 
represent the states, edges represent the transition taken by 
the system from one state to another, and guard conditions 
are Boolean expressions representing the expected behavior 
of the model. To model the fire detection system, we have 
identified all possible states, transitions, and guard condi-
tions of the system based on requirements and test specifica-
tions. Figure  5 depicts the EFSM model in three diagrams 
representing the SUT. The model explicitly representing the 
requirements of the fire detection system of TCMS consists 
of three diagrams, 14 states, 39 edges, and 15 data variables 
(i.e., 12 boolean inputs, two boolean outputs, and one integer 
representing timing constraint).
Two diagrams Fig.  5a and b represent the FDCUs and 
one diagram Fig.  5c represents the TCMS as a black box. 
FDCU1 is an initial as well as shared node of the model. 
FDCU1Signal, FDCU2, FDCU2Signal, TCMSisAc-
tive and FDCUsFireSign-als are the rest of the 
shared nodes of the models. These shared nodes are used 
by GW to traverse between different models while validat-
ing the model and generating test cases. Master, Slave, 
InternalFire, ExternalFire, InternalA-
ndExternalFire and Reset nodes represent the other 
states of the SUT based on requirements and test specifica-
tions. Similarly, edges, data variables, and corresponding 
guard conditions were added based on the expected behavior 
of the SUT.
Fault Injection into EFSM Model
Due to the unavailability of source code, we have injected 
some faults based on the different mutant operators, which 
omit or insert model elements [7 ] to produce faulty test 
scripts. We have used three mutant operators (output 
exchanged, change in guards conditions, and state miss-
ing) and created three versions of the faulty model. In the 
first version of the faulty model, we exchanged the output 
values of internal and external fire such that if a system is 
supposed to indicate the internal fire, it will indicate the 
external fire and vice versa. Similarly, in the second version, 
we made some changes in the guard conditions while we 
removed the Master state of one of the FDCUs in the third 
version to make these models contradictory to the original 
specification.Survey Design (RQ2)

RQ1: Experimental Results
This section describes our findings based on fault injection 
analysis to evaluate the test scripts generated by TIGER.
Generation and Execution of Test Scripts
After creating different versions of the model, we generated 
the test cases and scripts multiple times using each model. 
It is important to mention here that to ensure comprehensive 
and effective test coverage, we used a random generation 
algorithm and 100% edge_coverage as stopping condition 
offered by GW to generate abstract test cases guaranteeing 
that all functional paths, states, and edges in the model are 
exercised. All the other applicable generation strategies, 
except for the quick_random algorithm with 100% edge 
coverage, which produced similar results, resulted in fewer 
test cases and only partial model coverage, offering no sig-
nificant value to our research objectives. We also provided 
logical and technical names of the signals to TIGER and 
executed the test scripts on the SUT. Here, we report results 
from five test generations based on the variation in the num-
ber of generated test steps and the failed test steps. Moreo-
ver, to validate the accuracy and fault detection capability, 
we have also comparatively analyzed the verdict produced 
by TIGER-generated test scripts and test scripts written 
manually by a tester at Alstom.

The survey results show a generally positive industrial 
viewpoint on MBT capabilities with respect to overcoming 
existing testing challenges. However, modeling the system 
under test was regarded as a time-consuming process, that 
also requires extensive domain knowledge and knowledge 
about the modeling language. Thus, research into auto-
mating the model generation from existing artifacts is an 
interesting area of research. The survey also highlights 
factors limiting the adoption of MBT, and two of them 
relate to a lack of awareness and required resources for 
training. We believe these two factors can be overcome 
to a certain extent by working towards integrating MBT 
within in-house tools, which our survey results have iden-
tified as feasible.
Due to TIGER-related training costs required by indus-
trial practitioners and for not diverting them from their rou-
tine tasks, we did not mention TIGER in the industrial sur -
vey. Also, our target was to keep the survey topic general and 
not tool specific. Yet, the survey results indirectly identify 
opportunities to further develop TIGER and to improve its 
practical relevance. For instance, the analysis indicate that 
FSM models can also be used to address the problems of 
ambiguous requirements, lack of domain knowledge, and 
comprehensive test coverage at the system level. However, it 
also suggests training industrial practitioners with modeling 
notations (i.e., FSM) and the complexity of modeling a sys-
tem with stateless functions. Similarly, multiple respondents 
also highlighted the importance of effective resource utili-
zation through MBT, indicating the need to minimize the 
number of TIGER-generated test cases while preserving its 
fault detection effectiveness. This is something that we have 
started investigating already [39].Validity Threats
We generated the test cases using an abstract model 
representing the behavior of the SUT and ensuring the 
correctness of the model contributes to one of the poten-
tial threats to the internal validity of this study. It took 
us several rounds of modeling to completely understand 
the requirements and the test specifications to arrive at 
a correct model that an Alstom test engineer eventually 
confirmed as correct. The other internal validity threats 
are related to the selection bias of respondents and the ran-
dom selection of closed-ended questions due to different 
interpretations of the questions or attitudes toward MBT. 
We mitigated these threats by targeting a diverse audience 
with a particular interest in software testing, asking the 
participants to specify the reason to justify their selection, 
and performing the thematic analysis based on guidelines 
provided in [15].
The threats to external validity and reliability related 
to the evaluation of the proposed framework include 
human experience, modeling notations, and generator 
algorithms. We expect that if a person with similar mod-
eling and testing experience will replicate this study using 
random walk and edge coverage criterion of GW, similar 
results should be achieved. However, different modeling 
notations and generator algorithms may produce different 
results. Another issue is that the framework is specifically 
designed for CPS testing at Alstom, so it has particularities 
that may not be applicable to other CPSs but still be appli-
cable to multiple projects inside Alstom. Nevertheless, the 
description of the framework and the mapping procedure 
can give clues to companies operating in similar domains 
to apply MBT in practice. Moreover, another threat to the 
reliability of this study is the lower number of mutants. 
We created distinct mutants at the model level based on 
mutation operators, however, due to the abstract nature 
of the model, various mutants either provided an over -
lap in the type of faults they introduced (as discussed in 
“Discussion”) or failed to represent realistic faults. Hence, 
we carefully analyzed and selected each mutant to reflect 
significant changes and distinct issues in the generated test 
suite. We may also want the mutation testing at the model 
level to be supplemented with lower, code-level mutations 
and then validate our framework. We did not have access 
to code for this study, but this research direction is worth 
investigating if it becomes possible. Other potential threats 
to the external validity and reliability of the survey include 
sampling bias, sample size, low response rate, and con-

Conclusion and Future Work
We have proposed an MBT framework, TIGER, focused 
on the concretization of abstract test cases and the gen -
eration of test scripts for CPSs where embedded software 
plays an important part. There are three main parts of 
TIGER: abstract test case generator, test case concretizer, 
and finally, test script generator. We have evaluated the 
capabilities of TIGER-generated test scripts to represent 
real-world scenarios and detect faults using model-based 
mutation analysis. Moreover, we have also conducted an 
industrial survey to gain an understanding of industrial 
insight into the real-world adoption of MBT and asso-
ciated challenges. The experimental results show that 
test scripts generated by TIGER are executable, contain 
concrete test data, and can be used to uncover interaction 
faults at the SiL simulation level. The test scripts gener -
ated through the correct model did not result in any failed 
execution step, confirming the correct generation and 
execution, and ensuring conformance to the requirements 
specifications and the implemented SUT. Furthermore, 
the qualitative and quantitative analysis of the conducted 
survey shows that MBT can address most of the identi -
fied industrial challenges in testing. The practitioners also 
agree that MBT tools can be integrated with existing in-
house tools and can be adopted at the system level for the 
generation of test cases for embedded software. However, 
a lack of resources, a lack of awareness about MBT tools 
and methodologies among industrial practitioners, and 
ensuring the reliability and validation of MBT tools and 
processes were found to be the main contributing factors 
to its limited adoption.
For a thorough evaluation of TIGER, a proper cost-
benefit analysis is required. Although, a large number of 
generated test steps from TIGER yielded greater execution 
time than manually written test steps, an added benefit may 
be better combinatorial coverage than manually-written 
test scripts. However, a proper quantitative comparison 
with combinatorial testing is left as an interesting future 
work, along with the optimization to reduce the generation 
time and configuration for use at a different level of simu-
lation testing (application level) for testing Alstom’s CPS. 
Moreover, we also see the potential of conducting follow-
up industrial surveys by targeting industrial practitioners
