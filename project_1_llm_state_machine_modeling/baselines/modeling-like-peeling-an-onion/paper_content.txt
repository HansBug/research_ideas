Abstract
As software complexity skyrockets and requirements evolve at
breakneck speed, traditional human-centric behavioral modeling
can no longer keep pace in terms of efficiency, accuracy, and scalabil-
ity. While existing automated approaches can produce models, they
still struggle with deep semantic understanding of textual require-
ments or with reasoning about intricate system logic, especially
nested relationships. Inspired by the way experienced analysts “peel
back” layers of a problem, we propose LATO, aLayerwiseAnalysis-
Driven AuTomatic Behavioral MOdeling approach. It employs a
progressive decomposition strategy to guide large language models
in incrementally parsing requirement structures, deconstructing be-
havioral dependencies, and ultimately generating executable UML
activity diagrams. Comprehensive evaluations on four open-source
datasets and two real-world industrial systems show that LATO
comprehensively outperforms state-of-the-art baselines in accuracy,
completeness, and syntactic compliance: 𝐹1scores for behavioral
node-extraction improve by up to 71.1%, relation-extraction 𝐹1
by 52.4% relatively, and syntactic pass rates remain above 96.67%.
The framework also exhibits strong robustness to input pertur-
bations, confirming its cross-domain generalizability. This paper
is the first to tightly fuse human-inspired strategies with LLMs
in behavior modeling, yielding an intelligent infrastructure that
exhibits expert-level logical understanding and generalization. By
closing the modeling-skills gap, LATO delivers a next-generation,
low-cost, and explainable solution for requirements engineering
and AI-native software development.

1 Introduction
Behavioral models (e.g., state machines, activity diagrams, and
sequence diagrams) serve as a critical bridge between software
requirements and implementation, significantly enhancing devel-
opment efficiency. By formally capturing system dynamics through
state transitions, activity flows, and interaction patterns, behavioral
models provide an executable specification that aligns stakeholder
expectations with technical realization [ 22]. This intermediate rep-
resentation not only facilitates clearer communication across devel-
opment teams, but also enables early validation through techniques
like model checking [ 3,4] and simulation [ 13] to identify design
flaws, requirements inconsistencies, and edge cases during the ini-
tial development phases. Their applicability is broad: they are indis-
pensable in safety-critical systems development [ 3], while in agile
environments, they act as lightweight disambiguation tools [17].
Typically, the quality of behavioral models depends on the re-
quirements engineer’s expertise and experience in formal model-
ing [ 8,36]. However, the construction of accurate and complete be-
havioral models faces growing challenges due to i) limited availabil-
ity of requirements engineers with specialized expertise in formal
modeling, and ii) the escalating scale and complexity of modern soft-
ware systems. This dual constraint frequently results in manually
derived behavioral models that either inadequately capture system
requirements or contain errors that propagate through downstream
development phases [ 12,18]. In addition, the widespread adoption
of agile development methodologies and accelerated software re-
lease cycles has further exacerbated the limitations of manual be-
havioral modeling, rendering traditional approaches increasingly
inadequate for maintaining model accuracy while meeting demands
for rapid iteration and reliable system delivery [1, 20].

To achieve more efficient behavioral modeling, existing studies
present automated generation methods to automatically generate
behavioral models from textual requirements, which fall mainly
into three categories, i.e., Rule-based methods [ 15,24], Machine
Learning (ML)-based methods [ 16,40,43], and Large Language
Model(LLM)-based methods [ 17,21,37] respectively. Specifically,
the rule-based methods primarily rely on predefined templates to
(semi-)automatically extract key elements and generate correspond-
ing behavioral models. ML-based methods [ 16,40,43] adopt various
machine learning models to automatically learn patterns from a
vast amount of software requirements data. Recent LLM-based ap-
proaches [ 17,21,25,37] try to leverage LLMs’ powerful natural
language understanding and generation capabilities for modeling,
free from the constraints of sentence structure and training data,
thus showing great potential. Preliminary evaluations of these meth-
ods [ 9,17] indicate that they perform well on straightforward tasks
such as generating simple sequence diagrams.
However, existing LLM-based approaches still exhibit significant
limitations when handling complex nested behavioral relationships,
as they struggle to accurately capture multi-layered control logic
(e.g., parallel branches within loops) in requirements. Consider the
user account authentication flow illustrated in Fig. 1, a case that
combines conditional, sequential, and concurrent operations. Exist-
ing approaches, lacking a proper grasp of its layered dependencies,
produce models plagued by systematic deficiencies. Structurally,
these include missing links and broken integrity; semantically, they
involve redundant, incorrectly simplified, or omitted critical depen-
dencies and timing sequences. This implies that the generated code,
while potentially syntactically correct, suffers from compromised
logical consistency, which, in turn, invalidates its executability in
practice. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop auto-
mated modeling approaches capable of effectively handling complex
nested behavioral relationships.
We note that even professional engineers struggle to directly ex-
tract complex behavioral relations from requirements and must relyon layered analysis strategies. Inspired by this, we propose LATO,
aLayerwiseAnalysis-Driven AuTomatic Behavioral MOdeling ap-
proach. LATO constructs a novel LLM-powered framework whose
core innovation is the systematic integration of expert-inspired
layered reasoning with the semantic comprehension capabilities of
LLMs. By emulating the progressive analytical process employed by
humans, the framework can deconstruct hierarchical dependencies
within requirements layer by layer, automatically extract and syn-
thesize behavioral relations, and thereby accomplish the automated
construction of behavioral models from complex requirements.
To evaluate the effectiveness of LATO, we conducted extensive
experiments on four commonly used datasets and two real-world
system scenario datasets that we collected. The results show that,
compared with several state-of-the-art methods, LATO achieves the
best performance in terms of extraction accuracy and robustness,
especially in scenarios with deeply nested hierarchies. Concretely,
it boosts behavioral-node extraction 𝐹1by up to 71.1% and relation-
extraction𝐹1by up to 52.4%, while maintaining a syntactic pass
rate above 96.7%. The approach remains highly resilient to input
perturbations, underscoring its cross-domain generalizability. This
confirms its role as an intelligent infrastructure endowed with
expert-level logical understanding and cross-domain generalization
in behavior modeling. By effectively bridging the expertise gap in
behavioral modeling, LATO delivers a next-generation solution that
is efficient, low-cost, and explainable for requirements engineering
and AI-native software development.
In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:
•We propose LATO, a layerwise analysis-driven automatic
behavioral modeling approach. Our approach pioneers the
systematic integration of hierarchical reasoning with LLM
workflows, effectively overcoming the limitations of existing
methods in capturing complex behavioral logic.
•We evaluate LATO on four widely adopted open -source
benchmark datasets as well as two scenario -specific datasets

2 Expertise-inspired Framework Design
To equip LLMs with expert-level logical understanding and cross-
domain generalization of behavioral modeling, the core idea of this
paper is to distill a universal behavioral-modeling framework for
LLMs from the modeling practices of human experts [ 32,41]. As
shown in Fig. 1, the core of expert methodology lies in the hierar-
chical decomposition strategy and systematic integration process:
experts first deconstruct complex requirements into fundamental
elements (activities and relations) through layered analysis, then
systematically reassemble these elements into a coherent behavioral
model (verified through consistency checking).
This cognitive paradigm directly shapes our three-component
architecture: theKey Activity Identifierinherits the expert’s ca-
pability to rapidly pinpoint core entities in the initial phase; the
Layer-wise Relation Extractorrecreates the expert’s thinking
mode in deconstructing complex logic—by establishing a hierar-
chical analytical framework to progressively unravel nested rela-
tionships; finally, theBehavioral Model Constructoradopts the
expert’s systematic thinking during the integration phase, while
internalizing the originally independent quality checks into self-
verification mechanisms within each component.
Note that our approach goes beyond merely replicating the pro-
cedural steps of expert workflows—it internalizes their quality con-
trol logic. By implementing LLM-driven verification checkpoints
at each component’s output node, we effectively transform the
experts’ deliberative decision-making processes into executable
validation steps. This design preserves the “divide and conquer”
methodological advantage of human experts while achieving sig-
nificant improvements in both quality and efficiency through the
scalable reasoning capabilities of LLMs.
The resulting LATO framework (as shown in Fig. 2) essentially
represents a structurally transformed expert workflow: it main-
tains the rationality of human cognitive processes while break-
ing through the efficiency limitations of manual operations via an
LLM-tool collaborative paradigm, ultimately achieving effective
integration of expert knowledge and LLM reasoning capabilities.
Guided by this design philosophy, LATO achieves automated
modeling through the coordinated operation of three core mod-
ules: TheKey Activity Identifieridentifies core entities, employ-
ing LLMs to directly extract fundamental activity elements from
text; TheLayer-wise Relation Extractordeconstructs complex
logic, adopting a hierarchical analysis strategy to progressively
uncover relationships between elements; TheBehavioral Model
Constructortransforms structured elements into formal models
while maintaining semantic consistency. Each module follows an
“LLM Core Reasoning + External Tool-assisted” architecture, en-
suring both depth of semantic understanding and reliability of the
modeling process. The detailed explanation of each module is as
follows.
Key Activity Identifier:As depicted in Fig. 2, this module first
employs the Activity Extractor to identify activities within thetextual requirements. An external tool-based Activity Filter is then
used to remove any spurious activities that do not appear in the
original requirements. Finally, the LLM-based Activity Calibrator
assesses the accuracy of the identified activities.
Layer-wise Relation Extractor:Due to the complex contexts
typically involved in relationships, the layered relation extractor
adopts an iterative approach to extract relations layer by layer based
on the requirements structure. As shown in Fig. 2, the process op-
erates as follows: First, the LLM-based framework extractor parses
the layer framework of the inputted textual requirements. Then, the
LLM-based layer populator performs detailed content population
based on the extracted framework. Finally, the external tool-based
layer inspector analyzes the descriptions generated in the previous
steps to verify the relations within the current layer, and feeds the
sub-layer descriptions back into the layer populator to initiate the
next round of population.
Behavioral Model Constructor:This module initiates the pro-
cess with the Structure Analyzer, which leverages elements ex-
tracted by the Key Activity Identifier and Layer-wise Relation Ex-
tractor to construct a sketch model structure. Subsequently, the
Model Generator refines the sketch, culminating in the generation
of a comprehensive behavioral model. To ensure correctness, the
external tool-based Syntax Checker validates the generated model
and generates the eventual artifacts, e.g., UML activity diagrams.
3 Methodology Implementation
3.1 Architecture Selection and Prompt Design
The system architecture and prompt design in this work adhere to
systematic and modular engineering principles. Given that the core
modules (activity identification →relation extraction →informa-
tion integration) exhibit strict temporal dependencies and require
no dynamic interaction, we adopt a pipeline architecture. Based on
this architectural choice, we further design corresponding prompts,
with the detailed design process described below.
1). Modular Task Decomposition and Role Definition: Each mod-
ule in the pipeline is assigned a single, well-defined objective (e.g.,
activity identification). This highly focused design makes the con-
struction of corresponding prompts relatively straightforward. Each
prompt explicitly defines the role the LLM plays in a specific step,
the task instructions it must follow, and the output constraints it
must satisfy. For instance, the Activity Extractor, as the starting
point of the pipeline, is strictly defined to identify atomic activities
from the text without any additional commentary or reasoning.
2). Iterative Optimization with Structured Templates: We en-
hance the model’s capability to analyze complex requirements by
integrating Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning and few-shot ex-
amples into the prompt template. Each template comprises the
following components: task objective, few-shot examples, opera-
tional instructions, format constraints, and input text specifications.
For instance, the activity extractor template shown on the left side
of Fig. 3 incorporates the instruction “do not include any additional
commentary” to ensure the generation of a standardized activity list.
All templates underwent multiple rounds of iterative refinement
on a held-out development set until performance stabilized.

3.2 Key Activity Identifier
This module is dedicated to the precise extraction of behavioral
activities from unstructured textual requirements, thereby establish-
ing the essential atomic units that form the foundation for effective
behavioral modeling. Its core operation relies on the collaborative
work of three specialized components: the Activity Extractor, the
Activity Filter, and the Activity Calibrator.
Activity Extractor:The Activity Extractor serves as the starting
point of the extraction pipeline. It utilizes a carefully designed
structured prompt template (Fig. 3) to systematically guide the
LLM in activity identification by clearly defining task objectives,
input text, and output format. The output is a standardized activity
list based purely on textual features, with formats such as: “User
Account verification, Abort Operation, ...”.
Activity Filter:This component is responsible for semantic pu-
rification. It employs the FastCoRef 2.1.6 [ 29] tool to resolve refer-
ential relationships within the text. This component can accurately
establish associations between pronouns and their antecedents (e.g.,
mapping “it” to “the system”), effectively identifying and filtering
out redundant behaviors caused by ambiguous references, thereby
ensuring the clarity and relevance of the output activity set.
Activity Calibrator:This component enhances semantic con-
sistency and textual grounding. During this phase, the LLM per-
forms iterative refinement through a constrained multi-round loop,
systematically comparing its extraction results with the original re-
quirements. This calibration process (prompts are shown in Fig. 3)
includes comprehensive validation of initial extractions, resolu-
tion of remaining coreference issues, and thorough completeness

3.3 Layer-wise Relation Extractor
The core objective of this module is to decompose hierarchical
behavioral relations within the text—such as conditions, loops, and
parallel flows—layer by layer, akin to peeling an onion, ultimately
constructing clearly structured and well-layered activity relations.
The entire decomposition process is accomplished through the
collaborative efforts of three core components: the Framework
Extractor, the Layer Populator, and the Layer Inspector.

parsing method, synergistically combining structured task instruc-
tions with curated few-shot examples to derive a logical skeleton
from input requirements. The process is as follows.
The parsing process begins with top-level instructions that re-

identification, which recognizes the primary logical relations (e.g.,
sequence, condition, concurrency) within the current text layer;

rules established by few-shot examples, which define the correspon-
dence between linguistic cues and standard structural relations:
conditional relations are triggered by hypothetical phrases like
“if–then”; sequential relations are indicated by temporal markers
like “first–then”; concurrent relations are signaled by parallel indica-

When nodes marked “to be decomposed” appear in the model
output, the extractor automatically triggers a recursive process.
It creates a new parsing instance for each such node, injecting
specialized lower-level instructions that inherit the parent context
while clearly defining the parsing scope. This process iterates until
all nodes are broken down into indivisible atomic units. Finally, the
outputs from all levels are systematically integrated to construct a
complete, well-structured logical framework.
For instance, when processing the input text shown in Fig. 1, the
extractor generates the structured output presented in Fig. 4(a). This
output reveals a clear four-layer logical architecture: the top-level
(Level 1) decision drives the loop execution at the second level (Level
2); within the loop body, three concurrent processes (Level 3) and
one conditional judgment (Level 3) are nested, with each concurrent
process containing an internal conditional relation (Level 4). All
levels maintain encapsulation of internal implementation details,
representing core logical relationships solely through abstraction.
Layer Populator:This component is responsible for progres-
sively populating the activity content for each substructure marked
as “to be decomposed.” It employs an LLM-powered “onion-peeling”
strategy to perform in-depth expansion of these marked nodes.
During execution, each substructure carrying the “to be decom-
posed” marker is processed recursively as an independent unit: the
system identifies its internal logical patterns (such as conditional
branches) and populates the corresponding specific activity steps. If
newly generated nodes are still found to contain internal complex-
ity during this process, they retain the “to be decomposed” marker,
driving the parsing process to deeper levels. The recursive process
automatically terminates when no more “to be decomposed” mark-
ers appear in the output, indicating that all substructures have been
transformed into directly executable atomic-level activity units.

parsing) and the sequential node “authentication succeeded,” with

next iteration, the Populator further parses Level 2, identifying a
loop structure along with its two internal Level 3 components and
the “authentication failed” handling flow. The population results are
shown in lines 9-18 of Fig. 4(c). In subsequent iterations, the parsing
progresses to lower levels. For example, the Level 3 structure (lines

inspector verifies: the correctness of branch counts at the current
layer, the accuracy of activity execution order within each branch,
the completeness of identification and positioning for all nested
substructures, and the consistency between the overall structure
and both the input text semantics and the logic implied by the
dependency tree. When parsing deviations are detected, the inspec-
tor automatically generates diagnostic reports containing specific
linguistic evidence and sends these reports to the Layer Populator,
providing precise guidance for subsequent targeted corrections.
Collaboration Process:The Framework Extractor first extracts
the logical skeleton framework from the text and marks complex
nodes as “to be decomposed”; the Layer Populator then receives
this framework and recursively populates the marked nodes; the
Layer Inspector subsequently validates the populated results—if
issues are detected, it generates a diagnostic report and feeds it
back to the Populator to trigger repopulation. This creates a closed-
loop workflow of “extraction-population-inspection-repopulation,”
which iterates until all nodes pass quality inspection, ensuring the
accuracy and completeness of the parsing results.
3.4 Behavioral Model Constructor
In this module, we integrate the information extracted to generate
an executable behavioral model. It includes three core components,
Structural Analyzer, Model Generator, and Syntax Checker.
Structure Analyzer:This component is responsible for system-
atically integrating key activities and their hierarchical relation-
ships extracted from previous phases. It aligns scattered activities

--- Page 6 ---

and relationships into a unified structured intermediate representa-
tion, while preserving the full hierarchical semantics of the original
requirements and organizing all components according to their
logical relationships.
Model Generator:This component leverages LLMs to convert
the intermediate output from the Structure Analyzer into formal
diagram code (e.g., PlantUML [ 33]). The task of the generator is to
map hierarchical activities and their relationships to syntactically
correct modeling language constructs through the LLM.
Syntax Checker:This component validates the syntactic cor-
rectness of generated diagram. It utilizes the compilation feedback
from the rendering engine (such as the PlantUML command-line
tool(version v1.2025.4) [ 33]) as an authoritative basis for judgment
to systematically detect syntax errors and structural defects in
the code. Based on the detection results, the component generates
detailed diagnostic reports that precisely identify error locations,
types, and provide specific correction suggestions.
Collaboration Process:The Structure Analyzer passes the inte-
grated structured intermediate representation to the Model Gener-
ator; the Model Generator then generates formal code and immedi-
ately submits it to the Syntax Checker for validation. The Checker
obtains feedback through the PlantUML. When syntax errors are
detected, it sends diagnostic reports containing specific error loca-
tions and correction suggestions back to the Generator, triggering
a regeneration process. This “generate-check-regenerate” iteration
loop continues until all diagram code passes the syntax check.
4 Experiment Design
To evaluate the effectiveness of LATO, we conducted a large-scale
study to answer four research questions.
4.1 Research Questions
RQ1 (Performance Comparison): How does LATO’s perfor-
mance compare with existing generation methods? This question
aims to evaluate the effectiveness of LATO. To address this RQ,
we will conduct a comparative study by applying LATO and rep-
resentative baseline methods to the same dataset using the same
underlying LLM. Their performance will be evaluated using quality
metrics to determine whether LATO achieves superior results.
RQ2 (Impact of selected input examples): How does the se-
lection of example seeds influence the LATO’s ability to deal with
complex logical requirements not seen in the examples? This ques-
tion aims to investigate the generalizability of LATO. By examining
outputs generated with diverse example seeds, this RQ seeks to un-
cover how seed selection shapes LATO’s effectiveness in addressing
complex nested structures using the same LLM.
RQ3 (Ablation Study): What are the respective contributions
of the three core components (Key Activity Identifier, Layer-wise
Relation Extractor, and Behavioral Model Constructor) to the overall
system performance? This question evaluates the contribution of
each module. We selected one base LLM, and then individually
removed a module and reported the fluctuations in result quality.
RQ4 (Different LLMs’ influence): What is the impact of differ-
ent LLMs on the performance of LATO? This question investigates
the robustness of LATO. To this end, we selected several LLMs from
different model families and evaluated their performance on the
same dataset using LATO.4.2 Datasets
The datasets selected for our experiments satisfy two key criteria:
(i)Diversity in complexity and domain: The datasets span multi-
ple domains and exhibit a range of requirement complexities, from
simple to highly nested structures; (ii)Representativeness of
real-world requirements: In particular, the constructed datasets
are sourced from genuine industrial documents, ensuring practi-
cal relevance and authenticity. Experiments are conducted on six
datasets, whose detailed statistics are presented in Table 1. The
specifics of each dataset are described below.
•Functional Scenario Descriptions (FSD)[ 14] comprises
116 natural language descriptions from embedded systems,
encompassing deeply nested functional behaviors such as
device control, data processing, and fault handling.
•Real Automotive Case (RAC)[ 14] contains 20 requirement
scenarios from the automotive domain, representing typical
control logic and communication processes.
•PURE Dataset (PURE)[ 10] consists of 79 documents across
multiple application domains and formats. For our evalua-
tion, we extract 99 textual samples from simulated function-
response sequences.
•Business Process Dataset (BP)[ 43] includes 30 examples
of business software requirements, covering a range of oper-
ational scenarios.
•User Stories Dataset (US)[ 6] comprises 22 sets of user sto-
ries collected from various open-source projects, reflecting
typical agile requirements.
•LM Challenges (LMC)[ 27] features 10 requirements docu-

Among these, theFSDandRACdatasets are newly constructed
and collected directly from real-world industrial projects. TheFSD
dataset is sourced from authentic embedded system documenta-
tion and project artifacts, while theRACdataset is obtained in
collaboration with industry partners in the automotive sector. Both
datasets contain original handwritten requirements that capture the
complexity and specificity of actual engineering scenarios. Their
inclusion ensures that our evaluation encompasses not only a broad
spectrum of domains and complexity levels, but also the practical
challenges encountered in industrial behavioral modeling tasks.
For all datasets, the corresponding activity diagrams were manu-
ally annotated using PlantUML to facilitate quantitative evaluation.
The annotation was performed by three domain experts specializing
in software engineering and behavioral modeling. All annotators
adhered to a standardized guideline defining the identification of
activities, relations, and control structures. Each expert indepen-
dently labeled a shared subset of the data, and any discrepancies
were resolved through discussion. The resulting Cohen’s kappa[ 5]
exceeded 0.8 across all annotation dimensions, demonstrating high
inter-annotator consistency and reliability.

6 Threats to Validity
Construct Validity. 1○The Accuracy-Efficiency Trade-off:Eval-
uation shows that while LATO achieves higher accuracy than
single-pass baselines, its iterative design demands significantly
more computational resources (e.g., API calls and latency). This
establishes a new state-of-the-art in parsing fidelity but may limit
its practicality in resource-constrained scenarios. Therefore, claims
about LATO’s “effectiveness” should be contextualized: it excels in
accuracy-critical tasks but incurs inherent efficiency costs.
2○Context Length Constraints: Although excessively long re-
quirement texts may reach the LLM’s context limit, a requirements
document is typically divided into multiple independent behavioral
models, each with a modest description size. Consequently, current
mainstream LLMs can readily accommodate the input.
3○Domain Knowledge Limitation: Behavioral modeling inher-
ently hinges on the domain context. Our current approach targets
general scenarios and remains domain-agnostic. For highly special-
ized domains, we recommend augmenting the LLM with domain

edge embedding—to further enhance performance.
4○Selected Input Examples: LATO relies on few-shot prompting;
domain-specific instances could further boost performance. Never-
theless, the RQ2 experiments indicate that even generic examples
yield only marginal impact, preserving overall robustness.
External Validity: 1○Scenario Coverage Breadth: While the
current dataset spans multiple domains, it contains limited indus-
trial cases with relatively simple nested and concurrent require-
ments. The approach remains untested on ultra-large-scale, highly-
coupled, or safety-critical systems. Future work will incorporate
high-complexity industrial scenarios to rigorously evaluate LATO’s
robustness and generalizability under extreme requirements.
2○Evaluation Granularity: Current evaluation focuses on LATO’s
overall effectiveness, but lacks fine -grained analysis by relation type
or nesting pattern, leaving its consistency in specific scenarios (e.g.,
“parallel -within -loop” structures) unconfirmed. Aggregate metrics
may mask localized performance fluctuations. Future work will
develop fine -grained assessment profiles to more precisely charac-
terize LATO’s strengths, limitations, and applicable boundaries.
Conclusion Validity.The conclusions of our study may be af-
fected by experimental design and data annotation. Although stan-
dardized annotation protocols and multiple experimental runs were
employed to reduce random fluctuations, the use of human -annotated
ground truth may still introduce subjective bias and inconsistency.
Furthermore, the inherent stochasticity of LLM -based methods can
lead to minor performance variations across trials. To mitigate
these effects, we averaged results over multiple runs, though subtle
sources of error may still remain.
7 Related Work
Rule-based Methodsrely on predefined structured rules to auto-
matically convert textual requirement descriptions into behavioral
models. For example, Sholiqet al.[ 34] extract semantic fact types
and map them to BPMN constructs. Jahanet al.[ 16] employ rule-
based parsing to generate UML sequence diagrams from natural-
language requirement statements. De Biaseet al.[ 7] automatically
extract event, guard, and action patterns from Given–When–Then
requirements and map them to predefined SysML transition tem-
plates. Despite their strengths, these methods often rely heavily on
precise and well-defined inputs, which may not always be available.
ML-based Methodslearn the structure and rules of behavioral
models by analyzing system data or manually annotated informa-
tion. For example, Zhuet al.[ 43] employ richly annotated pairs
of business text and UML activity diagrams to deeply supervise
a neural generator for modeling. Mammadovet al.[ 26] introduce
Modelizer, a seq2seq model that learns reversible behavior from
synthetic input-output pairs. Tavassoliet al.[ 35] propose PL*, an
adaptive L*-based learning method that reuses sequences across
product variants. Although such methods can capture system be-
havioral characteristics, their performance is highly dependent on
the scale and quality of training data.
LLM-based Modeling Methodsleverage the powerful seman-
tic understanding and generation capabilities of LLMs to automati-
cally convert natural language requirements into behavioral models.
For instance, Kourani et al. iteratively prompt LLMs to generateand refine BPMN/Petri-net models with quality controls [ 21]. Jahan
et al. conducted a comparative study exploring the capabilities of
generative AI for generating UML sequence diagrams from user
stories [ 17]. Wang et al. demonstrated that LLMs can assist novice
analysts in generating sequence diagrams [ 37]. Ferrari et al. sys-
tematically evaluated ChatGPT’s ability to create UML sequence
diagrams from requirements [ 9], noting its fluent syntax but occa-
sional content omissions, highlighting the potential for LLMs to
streamline modeling tasks. However, none of these methods pro-
vides a systematic solution for behavioral modeling with complex
nested relationships.
In contrast, this paper employs a layer-wise relation decompo-
sition strategy to guide LLMs in relation extraction, thereby more
accurately handling complex behavioral logic. To the best of our
knowledge, our work presents the first method that decomposes
complex nested behavioral logic for guided LLM-based modeling
via an expertise-inspired workflow.
8 Conclusion
This paper proposes LATO, a layerwise analysis-driven automatic
behavioral modeling approach specifically designed for handling
complex behavioral logic. It employs a layered reasoning mecha-
nism that replicates how domain experts process complex require-
ments, guiding LLMs through a progressive analysis of unstruc-
tured textual inputs: starting with deconstructing fundamental
behavioral elements, then clarifying nested relationships and con-
straints among these components, and ultimately constructing exe-
cutable models with complete logical semantics. Experiments on
cross-domain benchmarks demonstrate that LATO significantly
outperforms existing methods in the modeling accuracy of complex
behavioral relationships while maintaining stable generalization
capabilities across diverse domains and model architectures.
This demonstrates that the combination of “human-inspired
strategies + LLM“ not only enables more intelligent and natural
understanding of the implicit logic in complex requirement texts,
akin to human comprehension, but also offers scalable and cost-
effective adaptability to complex scenarios. This approach provides
a new infrastructure-level capability for requirements engineering,
system design, and even AI-native software development.
Looking ahead, we plan to extend LATO to support a broader
range of output formats and domain-specific modeling languages,
thereby enhancing its applicability across diverse industrial con-
texts. Simultaneously, we will delve into exploring the accuracy-
efficiency Pareto frontier, establishing differentiated resource allo-
cation strategies for high-responsiveness scenarios like real-time
control systems and high-accuracy scenarios such as safety-critical
systems, to better balance performance and resource consump-
tion. Finally, as LLMs continue to evolve, we aim to investigate
the integration of more advanced architectures to further boost
performance, interpretability, and efficiency.
