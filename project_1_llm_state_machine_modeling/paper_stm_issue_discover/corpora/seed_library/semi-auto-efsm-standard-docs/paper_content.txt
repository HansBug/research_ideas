Abstract —Many requirement documents are written in natu-
ral language and, therefore, may contain problems such as in-
consistencies and ambiguities. To minimize these problems, there
is a trend in Software Engineering to use models to represent
systems. These models are obtained from textual requirements.
However, manual modeling is a complex task and, in order to
do it semi-automatically, one has to deal with problems such
as the kind of model to be generated, the automation degree
to be achieved, and the quality of the document that must
be processed. We propose a methodology to semi-automatically
generate Extended Finite State Machines (EFSMs) from natural
language standard documents. We used Natural Language Pro-
cessing (NLP) techniques and tools to extract information from
the document, and implemented a prototype which generates
EFSMs. The generated EFSMs were validated with a model
checking tool, and manually evaluated by comparing them with
the manually generated models
Keywords :Natural Language Processing, Model Genera-
tion, Aerospace Domain, Extended Finite State Machines.
I. I NTRODUCTION
The transformation of requirements into models has mo-
tivated many researchers over the years. There is no manual
or guideline to indicate how to perform this transformation.
The quality of the document has a direct inﬂuence on the
transformation process [1]. Meyer [2] and Firesmith [3] iden-
tiﬁed a set of common problems in requirements document,
such as ambiguity, inconsistency, and the use of inappropriate
vocabulary, which may increase the development cost and
cause a lot of rework. Mich, Franch, and Inverardi [4] showed
that the majority of the requirements documents is written in
natural language. This suggests that requirement analysis could
strongly beneﬁt from NLP-based support systems [4]. In this
context, several researchers have proposed approaches to im-
prove the quality of the requirements document, and to extract
information used to generate formal or semi-formal models,
therefore improving the entire development process. Many
of these approaches use a set of tools and NLP techniques
to pre-process and extract information from the documents
[5][6][7]. Text pre-processing usually relies on a syntactic
analyzer (or parser) and/or a Part-of-Speech (PoS) tagger. The
parser provides a valid syntactic structure for a sentence. The
process is supported by a grammar that deﬁnes a set of valid
structures for a given domain [8][9]. The PoS tagger associatessyntactic and morphological features to the words of a sentence
or text [8]. In Figure 1, we show a sample sentence, processed
by the Stanford Parser [9] and Stanford Tagger [10].
Sentence: The system sent many error codes.
Parsing result
(ROOT (S (NP (DT The) (NN system)) (VP (VBD sent)
(NP (JJ many) (NN error) (NNS codes))) (. .)))
Tagging result
The DT system NN sent VBD many JJ error NN codes NNS . .
Fig. 1. Sentence processed by the Stanford Parser and the Stanford Tagger
The result from the parser shows that the sentence is
composed of a noun phrase (NP) and a verb phrase (VP). The
head of the NP is the noun “system”, and the head of the VP
is the verb, in past tense, “sent”. The result from the tagger
shows the lexical categories attributed to each word of the
sentence, e.g., “sent” is a verb, in the past tense (tag VBD),
and “many” is an adjective (tag JJ). This kind of information
can be very helpful in generating different kinds of models.
Results from the pre-processing are used to obtain struc-
tured information from the text. In our case, the text is a
requirements document, and the structured information are
models representing the requirements. A system model is
an abstraction of the system under consideration, and it is
expected to be less prone to ambiguities and inconsistencies
than a natural language description. Moreover, it can be
more efﬁcient in the communication with the development
team. There are different kinds of system models that can be
generated from requirement documents. Our focus is on the
extraction of system behavior models from natural language
requirements. The extracted behavior model is represented by
an Extended Finite State Machine (EFSM).
EFSMs are usually used to deal with more complex sys-
tems, such as applications in the aerospace domain. They can
represent different layers of abstraction [11], and are very
useful for modeling applications in which it is necessary to
represent control and data information, as it is the case of
communication services for space missions. Furthermore, an
EFSM can drive speciﬁc tools, providing an efﬁcient way to
test the system, as well as an easier way to detect errors or
omissions in the requirements description [12].

Requirements can be documented in various forms. For
some applications, requirements are described in a standard
document, e.g., communication protocols, automotive systems,
and on-board services. We are interested in standard documents
because they are the basis for many implementations. Besides,
they go through less frequent changes than ordinary require-
ment documents. Although problems such as ambiguity and
incompleteness appear less frequently in standard documents,
other issues must be addressed, such as the possibility that
relevant information for the same element could be distributed
over different sections of the document, or that important
information be presented on tables or ﬁgures, among others.
Information extraction for model generation must deal with the
correct identiﬁcation of atomic and relational information. A
major difﬁculty in this task is dealing with ambiguity; some ap-
proaches propose rewriting techniques to eliminate ambiguities
in the text. Other problem is nontextual information, expressed
in the form of tables or ﬁgures. This is a problem for automatic
model generation, specially the behavioral model, because
essential facts for behavior speciﬁcation are omitted. Although
there are many approaches to extract information from these
data, many of them only apply to web documents and marked
text [13][14][15]. There are few approaches to treat unmarked
texts (e.g. “.pdf”) [16]. The presence of relevant information
in tables and ﬁgures makes NLP pre-processing more difﬁcult
and, considering the complexity that could be involved, in this
work we have manually identiﬁed and stored this information
in a database, to be used during EFSM generation.
This work presents a methodology to semi-automatically
transform a standard document in natural language into
Extended Finite State Machines. The information used for
EFSM generation is extracted from a standard document with
NLP tools and techniques; structured documents are specially
treated, with explicit use of the structure in this phase. The
complex problem of extracting information from tables and
ﬁgures is ﬁnessed, through manual identiﬁcation and storing
it in a database used during EFSM generation.
A standard document, developed by the European Coopera-
tion for Space Standardization (ECSS), a cooperation between
the European Space Agency (ESA) and aerospace industries
[17], was used in a case study. The standard document “Space
Engineering - Ground Systems And Operations - Telemetry
And Telecommand Packet Utilization Service”, called PUS,
and proposed by ECSS, describes a set of services for space-
craft monitoring, and can be used in different missions [17].
The National Institute for Space Research (Instituto Nacional
de Pesquisas Espaciais - INPE) in Brazil, uses this standard
document in some of its projects. We had the collaboration of
researchers from INPE and from the Aeronautics Technology
Institute (ITA) to develop a case study whose aim was to
evaluate the proposed methodology.
This paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents the
background, Section 3 introduces the proposed methodology
for information extraction, and in Section 4 the work is
evaluated with a case study and conclusions and future work
are presented in Section 5.
II. B ACKGROUND
The transformation of requirement documents into formal
representations has been explored along the years, and theefforts range from improving the quality of the document to the
kind of model to be generated. Some researchers convert text
requirements to mathematical or logical formulae, and others
convert text requirements to models, mainly UML models.
Y ue, Briand and Labiche [1], present a systematic review
in which they described several approaches which propose
the use of different concepts and techniques to perform this
transformation. The authors deﬁned taxonomies to classify
requirements, restriction rules, analysis models, and transfor-
mation approaches. The available approaches may or may not
use domain speciﬁc information as part of the input document
to aid information extraction; also, the requirements document
may or not be restricted to a speciﬁc format, and so do the
allowed vocabulary and sentence structure. Some approaches
provide a template to represent requirements in a structured
format. The most common is the use case representation.
The vocabulary and sentence structure can be restricted to
reduce problems and the complexity of the documents. This
makes the transformation process more efﬁcient and reliable.
However, such restrictions vary from one approach to another.
Regarding the analysis models, the authors found that many
approaches generate objects, classes and abstractions, and
some of them also generate behavioral features. UML is the
most common language used to represent the analysis model.
Regarding the automation aspect, the same authors found that
most of the approaches are automated or semi-automated.
Nevertheless, they have not found an efﬁcient and sufﬁciently
documented approach to generate analyis models, either au-
tomatically or semi-automatically. Most reviewed approaches
use at least one pre-processing technique. PoS tagging and
syntactic analysis are largely used to facilitate transformation
and to determine the grammatical structure of the sentence.
Also, most approaches use at least one intermediate model.
The transformation processes identiﬁed by the authors are:
rule-based, pattern-based, ontology-based, and identity-based
approaches. The rule-based approach is the most common, and
uses a pre-deﬁned set of rules to transform requirements into
analysis models; an ontology-based approach uses an ontology
as an intermediate model that is transformed into an analysis
model; pattern-based analysis looks for patterns, transforming
them into a target pattern; and, ﬁnally, the identity-based ap-
proach transforms a model into another representation, without
changing the information content.
In addition to the approaches analyzed in [1], Y ue, Briand
and Labiche [5][18] present an approach to converting natural
language use cases to UML models (class, activity, and state
diagrams). It was implemented as part of a tool, called aTou-
can. Transformation algorithms and NLP tools were used to
extract information and to convert it to UML analysis models.
Use cases are speciﬁed according to a use case modeling
approach (RUCM) [19] based on a template, and on rules for
Use Case Speciﬁcation (UCS). It is an automatic rule-based
approach that uses as input use cases written according to a
pre-deﬁned format, and pre-process the text using a syntactic
analyser, generating an intermediate model.
Deeptimahanti and Babar [20] transform requirement doc-
uments into UML models (class, use cases, and collaboration
diagrams), implemented with a tool called UMGAR. The
tool contains two components: the normalizing requirements
component and the model generator component. The process

initiates with the normalizing process, i.e. requirements are
rewritten to remove ambiguities from the sentences. After the
text has been normalized, the model generator extracts the
information needed to generate the analysis model. The model
can be converted to XMI ﬁles, allowing requirements trace-

and identity-based), which takes natural language requirements
as input, pre-processes the text using a syntactic analyser and
other NLP tools to resolve ambiguities and other inconsistency
problems in the rewriting process, and generates intermediate
models.
Kof [6] presents an approach to convert textual descriptions
of an automaton into a ﬁnite automaton model. The identiﬁ-
cation of states is based on speciﬁc word categories and terms
deﬁned by the user. The identiﬁcation of transitions is based on
the segmentation of the sentences, and on the categorization of
the sentence segments. A set of rules were deﬁned to ﬁnd the
text segments that should be used. The generated automaton
is represented in a tabular form. It is an automatic rule-based
approach which uses a textual description of an automaton as
input and pre-process the text using a PoS Tagger.
The approach proposed here is semi-automatic, rule-based,
and uses a PoS tagger to pre-process the text. It takes a
standard document as input, and generates Extended Finite
State Machines (EFSMs). Differently from [5][18], the input
is not restricted to a speciﬁc format, and there is not a
normalizing process, as in [20][7]. The search for patterns is
similar to [6], but in addition, our approach uses structural
patterns from the standard document to extract information
which will be used for model generation.
III. M ETHODOLOGY
Our approach was inspired by Kof’s [6]. However, in
addition to the information from the PoS tagger, the approach
proposed here uses the structure of the document as help to
identify the relevant information to be extracted, and which
will be used to compose the models. It is important to note
that the writing style and the structure of the document are very
relevant; the information description, as well as the structure
of the document, must follow some patterns. The proposed
approach uses the structural patterns of the sentences and
of the document to identify and extract information. The
information extraction process presents fewer problems than
those reported in the literature: a standard document is usually
organized in chapters and/or sections, and the description of
the element that must be modeled (e.g. a service or a procedure
for a speciﬁc application) may refer to other sections (or
chapters) of the same document, or to other documents. In
some domains, the document may describe the terms and
abbreviations, and other relevant information that might be
used in the implementation of any element described in the
standard document. To illustrate the methodology we present
a sample text (Figure 2) showing some relevant characteristics
present in a standard document, together with some problems
identiﬁed and treated in this work.

Step 2: Reading of the text
After ﬁnding patterns in the sentences, the next activity
is the careful manual reading of the entire text. References
to other documents or to a different section of the text must
be marked for future processing. Information represented in
tables or ﬁgures that are not explicit in the text, and are
related to the described element, must also be marked and
processed in a later stage. In Figure 2, the dependency relation
of the functionalities is described only in the table and must
be marked to be used in later stages of the process.
Step 3: Reading of the element description
The analysis of the section/chapter that will be used for
model generation must be performed to better understand the
structure of the section and relations between information. The
text must be pre-processed using a PoS Tagger, which will help
the analyst to identify word categories that can be used in the
information extraction process. Each category attributed to a
word is a tag, and can be used in the extraction process. In
addition to the categories, the structural pattern identiﬁed in
the document can also be used for information extraction.

Step 5: Identiﬁcation of transitions
A transition is the change from a state to another, according
to a set of events or conditions being satisﬁed or not [11], that
may require an execution of an action. Identiﬁcation of transi-
tions proceeds very similarly to identiﬁcation of states, starting
with the analysis of the text. In the text fragment presented in
Figure 2, events were identiﬁed using the following pattern:
<event name>(<functionality number >,<frequency >)
The relation of the identiﬁed events with the speciﬁc
element to be modeled was extracted from the pair of numbers,
enclosed with parenthesis. The ﬁrst number relates the event
with the functionality, and the second indicates if it is a
mandatory or optional functionality. To identify the source
state of the transitional ﬂow, the event name (indicated by the
text segment before the parenthesis) will be searched in the
text description of each identiﬁed state. If the event name is
found in the state name or in the state description, this state
is the source of the transitional ﬂow and the destination is the
next state identiﬁed in the text. A transition is also composedby actions and conditions. Considering item 3.2.a in Figure 2,
the text segment “the data must be divided into small pieces.”
is an action to be performed. In the sample text, it is possible to
observe that the text fragments “different delivery mechanisms
must de used to avoid packet loss.” (item 3.2.b), and “all
packets must be veriﬁed.” (item 3.2.c) are both composed of
a determiner (tag DT) or an adjective (tag JJ), followed by
any number of nouns (tags initiated with NN) - “the data”,
“different delivery mechanisms” and “all packets”. Each one
is the subject of the action. The rest of the text fragment is the
modal verb “must”, indicating the obligation of the action, the
verb “to be” and, ﬁnally, a verb, in past participle (tag VBN),
expressing the action - “divided”, “used” and “veriﬁed”. The
sentences searched for must satisfy the following description:

The last activity is the identiﬁcation of the conditions, also
known as guards. In general, guards are conditions that must
be satisﬁed to enable the transition from a state to another.
In most cases, sentences that express conditions contain the
conjunction “if” and the condition is expressed after the
conjunction. It is not rare that sentences expressing conditions
also deﬁne the actions to be executed in case these conditions
are satisﬁed.
The identiﬁcation of conditions starts of searching for
sentences containing the conjunction “if”. The text fragment
after the conjunction is a condition. If the text segment after
the conjunction is divided by a comma, the text segment after
the comma is the action directly related to the satisfaction of
the condition. To illustrate this, consider item 3.2.c. in Figure
2: the second sentence starts with the conjunction “if” and
the following text segment, until the comma, is the condition
- “no packet transferred was lost”. After the comma, the
segment represents the action to be execute d-“a success
message must be transmitted to the sender.”. Another way to
identify conditions is to search for exceptional conditions, i.e.,
conditions that, if satisﬁed, must generate an error. In this case,
the exceptional action must be searched before the conjunction
“if”, and the condition is deﬁned after the conjunction. The
difference is that the sentence ends with the deﬁnition of the
condition. Consider the item 1.2.b in Figure 2: the second
sentence starts with the expression “An error shall occur”
which is the action, and can be used as a key expression
in the information extraction process to identify exceptional
conditions. After the conjunction “if”, the subsequent text is
the condition to be satisﬁed - “there are packets delivered out
of order”. In the identiﬁcation of conditions and actions there
are not sequences of tags to be searched, only key expressions.
The marks used in the search process are the conjunction “if”,
the division of the sentence by a comma, and the exceptional
condition that starts with a key expression.

categories and uses these categories to identify transitions and
their parameters. We do not use these categories because they

IV . E V ALUA TION
The proposed methodology was evaluated with the PUS
standard document case study. The developed prototype gener-
ated the EFSM for two selected services. The document speci-
ﬁes requirements of use of telecommand packets and telemetry
source packets for monitoring and controlling subsystems and
payloads [17]. It is applicable to any mission, and it takes
into account that different missions require different levels of
complexity and functionality. No service is mandatory, but
each one has a set of minimum capabilities that must be
always included in any implementation of a service, and a
set of additional ones [17]. With this case study we answered
the following research questions: a) Are the automatically
extracted EFSM similar to those manually created? b) Are
the automatically generated EFSM free from deadlocks1?c )
Do the automatically generated EFSM satisfy the properties
of reachability and liveness?
The evaluation of the generated EFSMs was performed in
two ways: 1) A manual analysis, comparing the automatically
generated EFSM with a manually created EFSM, available in
V´eras [22]; 2) A model checking using the DiVinE Model
Checker [23], which veriﬁes the presence of accepted cycles,
the reachability, the presence of deadlocks, and assertion
violation.
The ﬁrst comparison showed some differences between the
resulting state machines for Telecommand V eriﬁcation service;
as it can be seen in Figure 4, the automatically generated state
machine starts in the state “Acceptance”, while the manually
created state machine (Fig. 5) starts in “Without TC”.

of this section. In addition, the On-board operation procedure
service was processed, and an EFSM for the normal and the
exceptional ﬂows was generated. However, due to the lack of a
manual model for this service no comparative evaluation was
possible.
The output generated by the prototype is a text ﬁle with
the .sm extension. This is the extension used by the State
Machine Compiler (SMC) [24], an application that allows
deﬁning state diagrams using an easy-to-understand language.
It can be used to generate source code of the state machine in
many programming languages, such as C and Java. SMC also
generates Graphivz [25] .dot ﬁles from .sm ﬁles. Graphviz is
an open source software for graph visualization. It is used in
our prototype to generate a graphical view of the EFSM.
The second evaluation process was performed using the
model checker tool DiVinE 3.2.2 [23]. This tool is able to ver-
ify models in a wide range of languages. The generated state
machines were modeled using the .dve language, the modeling
language created speciﬁcally for DiVinE; the resultant models
were submitted to DiVinE to be validated for safety and
some liveness properties. The generated state machines for the
Telecommand V eriﬁcation and On-board Operations Procedure
services were validated and all properties held: no deadlocks
were found in either model, and the reachability and liveness
also held, thus answering afﬁrmatively the second and third
research questions posed at the beginning of this section.

V. C ONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
The transformation of natural language documents into
models present many challenges, even if the text does not show
the most common problems reported in the literature, such as
ambiguity and incompleteness.
The transformation of standard documents into EFSM
can be helpful. Nevertheless, the set of rules created for
information extraction are very dependent on the structure
of the document, and on the writing style. Since a standard
document will be used by many people, for research, industrial
or critical purposes, the considerable work involved is paid
back later with increased productivity. Indeed, effort applied
in the adequacy of the rules may be rewarded by the beneﬁts
that brought by a representation that can be shared by all sorts
of development groups.
EFSMs can be helpful in many development phases, spe-
cially in the test phase, since it can be automatically drive
appropriate tools. Still, the automatically generated EFSM is an
initial representation of what is been required by the standard
document, and the expert users can use this as help in the
development of the ﬁnal models. However, just by themselves,
these models may not be sufﬁcient for a complete analysis
and, as future work, we must investigate which models could
be generated on extending the methodology proposed here. As
self loops, hierarchies can be treated only if the relationships
are explicit in the text.
The use of structural information of the standard document
in the information extraction can help the model generation,
requiring less pre-processing of the text, eschewing a repre-
sentation in a speciﬁc format, or any change in the sentences.
