1.1. Overview of Traffic Signal Control

Traffic signal control systems are fundamental components of urban transportation networks, ensuring 
the safe and efficient movement of vehicles and pedestrians at intersections. These systems regulate the flow 
of traffic by using light 
signals
—
typically red, yellow, and green
—
to control vehicle movement and optimize 
the use of road infrastructure. Proper traffic signal control not only helps in minimizing accidents and ensuring 
the safety of road users, but also plays a crucial role in r
educing congestion, improving fuel efficiency, and 
decreasing pollution by minimizing unnecessary waiting times at traffic signals.
 
Traditionally, traffic signal systems have relied on 
fixed
-
time control
, where the signal phases (i.e., 
green, yellow, and red lights) are pre
-
set with fixed durations, often determined by the time of day or standard 
traffic patterns. While this approach is simple and easy to implement, it lacks flexibility and responsiveness
 
to real
-
time changes in traffic conditions. As a result, these systems often lead to inefficient traffic flow, 
especially during peak hours or when unexpected events, such as accidents
 
or weather conditions, disrupt 
typical traffic patterns.
 
To address these inefficiencies, more advanced 
adaptive traffic control systems
 
(ATCS) have been 
developed. These systems use real
-
time data from traffic sensors (e.g., inductive loops, cameras, or radar 
detectors) to adjust signal timings dynamically, depending on the actual traffic demand. While adaptive 
systems improve traffic flo
w, their complexity and reliance on expensive hardware and sophisticated 
algorithms can make them difficult and costly to implement. As cities move towards 
smart transportation 
systems
, the demand for affordable, efficient, and easily deployable solutions is increasing.
 
In this context, 
Finite State Machines (FSMs)
 
offer a promising approach to enhancing the 
adaptability and efficiency of traffic signal control systems without the complexity associated with traditional 
adaptive systems. By utilizing FSMs in combination with 
discrete logic design
, it is possible to create systems 
that are both simple to implement and capable of dynamically adjusting to varying traffic conditions.
 
 
1.2. Introduction to Finite State Machines (FSM) and Their Role in Discrete Logic Design
 
A 
Finite State Machine (FSM)
 
is a computational model used to describe systems with a limited number of 
distinct states and the transitions between those states. It is widely used in digital circuit design, control 
systems, and software development. An FSM consists of three main comp
onents:
 

 
States
: Represent different conditions or phases of the system (e.g., traffic signal states such as Red, 
Green, Yellow).
 

 
Transitions
: Define how the system moves from one state to another, typically triggered by specific 
events or inputs (e.g., vehicle detection or timer expiration).
 

 
Inputs
: Events or conditions that trigger transitions (e.g., vehicle count, pedestrian button presses, or 
time intervals).
 
FSMs are particularly useful in systems where the behavior is predictable, and the system's current state 
depends only on its previous state and input conditions, rather than the entire history of past inputs. This makes 
FSMs an ideal model for traffic sig
nal control, as the signal state at any given time (Red, Green, Yellow) is 
determined by a limited set of conditions, such as vehicle presence, waiting time, or pedestrian demand.

2. LITERATURE REVIEW
 
2.1. Evolution of Traffic Signal Systems
 
Traffic signal control systems have evolved significantly over the last century, driven by the increasing 
demand for better traffic management as urban populations grew and vehicular traffic intensified. The early 
traffic signals, first introduced in the e
arly 20th century, were basic mechanical systems with fixed timing 
cycles, often governed by manual operation. These systems were relatively simple, but they quickly became 
inefficient as cities grew and traffic volume fluctuated. Fixed
-
time signal control

predetermined durations for each signal phase (e.g., red, yellow, and green), became the standard method. 
While reliable, fixed
-

In response to the limitations of fixed

demand

inductive loop detectors, infrared sensors, or cameras. The main goal of these systems was to adjust the signal 
timings dynamically based on real
-
time traffic demand, improving the flow of vehicles and reducing 
congestion. The introduction of sensors marked a significant advancement, allowing for adaptive
 
control, but 
these systems remained complex and expensive to implement due to the need for additional infrastructure, 
communication networks, and data processing algorithms.

2.2. Use of Finite State Machines (FSMs) in Traffic Signal Control
 
The application of 
Finite State Machines (FSMs)

FSMs offer a structured, predictable, and computationally lightweight approach to designing traffic signal 
controllers. An FSM model consists of a finite set of states (each representing a signal phas

Yellow, or Red), with transitions between states triggered by specific inputs (e.g., vehicle detection or time 
elapsed). FSMs are particularly advantageous for traffic signal systems because

The concept of FSMs in traffic signal control was first explored in the 1960s, when digital systems

Recent studies have shown that FSM

FSMs are also beneficial in terms of scalability and ease of modification. Since FSMs are highly

5. RESULTS AND EVALUATION
 
The FSM
-
based traffic signal control system presents several benefits in terms of efficiency, simplicity, 
and reliability. However, it also has some limitations, particularly when it comes to adaptability in dynamic 
traffic conditions. Below is an analysis
 
of the effectiveness of this approach, followed by a discussion of 
potential drawbacks.
 
5.1. Effectiveness of FSM
-
Based Traffic Signal Control
 
Efficiency:
 

 
Predictable Timing
: One of the major strengths of the FSM
-
based traffic signal control system is its 
predictability
. Since the system follows a well
-
defined set of states and transitions based on time or 
simple inputs, it ensures that the traffic light phases are consistent and reliable. This can reduce 
congestion at intersections during normal traffic flow and improve
 
traffic management efficiency.
 

 
Low Computational Overhead
: The FSM is a 
simple and low
-
complexity system
, which does not 
require intensive computation. It operates based on a fixed set of rules, making it computationally 
efficient and inexpensive to implement, especially for smaller intersections or cities with limited 
resources.
 

 
Reduced Accidents and Confusion
: The clear and deterministic state transitions reduce the chances 
of 
confusion
 
at intersections, which in turn decreases the likelihood of accidents. The well
-
structured 
cycle of green, yellow, and red phases ensures a predictable flow of traffic and enhances road safety.

Simplicity:
 
Easy to Implement
: The design of the FSM



based traffic signal 
control a good option for cities or areas with limited budgets.
 
5.2. Potential Drawbacks and Limitations
 
Lack of Adaptability in Dynamic Conditions:
 

 
Fixed Time Phases
: One of the most significant drawbacks of an FSM
-
based traffic signal control 
system is its lack of 
adaptability
. Since the FSM follows predefined time intervals for each phase, it 
cannot adjust dynamically to fluctuations in traffic volume or congestion. For instance, during rush 
hours, the system may fail to allocate longer green light phases to heavily trafficked
 
lanes or reduce 
green time on less busy lanes, leading to 
inefficiency
.
 

 
No Real
-
Time Adjustment
: FSMs are not inherently capable of responding to real
-
time data (e.g., 
vehicle presence, traffic speed, or pedestrian crossings). A static FSM system cannot prioritize certain 
vehicles, such as 
emergency vehicles
, or adapt to unusual traffic conditions, such as a 
traffic accident
 
or a 
construction zone
. As a result, the system may either over or underutilize the available time for 
green lights in certain directions.
 
Scalability:
 

 
Limited Scalability
: FSM
-
based systems are typically effective for simple intersections with 
straightforward traffic patterns. However, as the complexity of the traffic network increases, such as 
with multiple lanes, turning signals, or complex intersections, the FSM design 
can become more 
complicated. Managing the interactions of various traffic flows with an FSM becomes harder, 
especially when the number of states and transitions increases, making the system less scalable.
 
Lack of Traffic Sensing Integration:
 

 
Fixed Signal Timing
: While FSMs can be enhanced with 
external sensors
, such as inductive loops 
or cameras, to detect vehicle presence, this requires additional hardware and can increase the 
complexity of the system. Without such sensors, FSMs are purely time
-
based and cannot adjust based 
on the actual number of vehicles at 
the intersection. This lack of 
real
-
time traffic sensing
 
means that 
the system could either be inefficient during peak traffic times or underutilized during off
-
peak periods.
 
Complexity in Multi
-
Intersection Coordination:
 

 
Coordination Issues
: In larger cities or high
-
traffic areas, traffic signal systems need to be coordinated 
across multiple intersections to avoid congestion and ensure smooth traffic flow. FSM
-
based 
controllers, while effective at a single intersection, are not inherently de
signed to coordinate traffic 
lights across multiple intersections without additional logic or communication between signals. This 
can lead to 
inefficiencies
 
in traffic flow, as signals may not be synchronized or may cause "bunching" 
of c
ars when moving from one intersection to another.

Hybrid Systems
