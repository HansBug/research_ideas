Elias Goldsztejn, Dan Rouven Suissa, Ronen Brafman
Department of Computer Science, Ben Gurion University of the Negev, Beer Sheva, Israel
Keywords: Cognitive Robotics, Robot and Multi-Robot Systems, Task Planning and Execution, Vision and Perception
Abstract: Autonomous mobile robots in human environments must balance efficiency with social compliance and safety.
While Vision-Language Models (VLMs) enable semantic scene understanding, existing frameworks often lack
dynamic reconfiguration in response to changing scenarios, gestures, and hazards. We present zero-shot VLM
integration without fine-tuning using a modular state-machine architecture that dynamically adapts navigation,
gesture-triggered transitions, and safety-critical overrides. Experiments show robust gesture recognition, ob-
ject detection, and scenario adaptability, with seamless behavior switching at expected transition points.
1 INTRODUCTION
Effective interaction with complex environments
and humans is a key challenge for autonomous
robotic systems. Vision-Language Models (VLMs)
are promising tools for addressing these challenges
thanks to their semantically rich perception and
decision-making capabilities (Liu et al., 2024; Jeong
et al., 2024). In this paper, we utilize VLMs to con-
trol and coordinate multiple off-the-shelf behaviors,
allowing them to adapt dynamically to environmental
changes, object detection, and human gestures. Un-
like prior work relying on fine-tuning (Yang et al.,
2024; Li et al., 2023) or end-to-end policies (Wang
et al., 2024), our modular system requires no training
and uses zero-shot VLM outputs to adapt its behavior.
Our system leverages the VLM to interpret scenes,
gestures, and hazards, dynamically adjusting behavior
without per-task training. For navigation, the robot
adapts its algorithm parameters based on scene un-
derstanding; for Human Robot Interaction (HRI), it
interprets gestures to trigger behaviors like following;
for safety, it recognizes hazards and either navigates
more cautiously or hands control to a human oper-
ator. These behaviors are synchronized by a state-
based controller informed by the VLM output. Rather
than aiming for exhaustive coverage, we showcase
how combining VLMs with control structures enables
rich, adaptive robot behavior in diverse settings.

2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED
WORK
Visual Language Models (VLMs). Vision-
Language Models (VLMs) (Li et al., 2025) combine
computer vision and natural language processing to
process and relate visual and textual data. As mul-
timodal systems, they enable foundational tasks such
as image captioning, VQA, and image-text retrieval.
A typical VLM uses specialized encoders to estab-
lish semantic connections across modalities. Recent
efforts apply VLMs to robotics: (Li et al., 2023) in-
troduced RoboFlamingo for manipulation, and (Song
et al., 2024) proposed VLM-Social-Nav for socially
aware navigation. (Cheng et al., 2024) also developed
NaVILA, integrating VLM perception with locomo-
tion for legged robots. These works demonstrate the
VLM’s capacity to enhance flexibility and context-
awareness in robotic systems.Adaptive Navigation using VLMs. Effective navi-
gation requires dynamic parameter adjustment based
on environmental elements like corridors or door-
ways. VLMs generate high-level semantic represen-
tations (Liu et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2023) that
integrate perception with real-time decision-making.
VLM-GroNav (Elnoor et al., 2024) estimates terrain
properties using VLM and proprioception to dynami-
cally adjust traversability. BehA V (Weerakoon et al.,
2024) utilizes zero-shot scene understanding, allow-
ing robots to follow complex instructions and respect
constraints. Unlike fixed-policy pipelines (Yokoyama
et al., 2024; Elnoor et al., 2024; Weerakoon et al.,
2024), our system leverages VLM outputs to adjust
a state machine, enabling more flexible and adaptive
high-level behavior.
Gesture Recognition using VLMs. Human ges-
ture understanding is critical for HRI, yet traditional
methods often rely on intensive tracking (e.g., HMMs
(Chen et al., 2003)), large labeled datasets (Yu et al.,
2022), or wearable sensors (Cheng et al., 2021).
VLM-based methods offer a flexible alternative. Re-
cent work, such as GIRAF (Lin et al., 2023), com-
bines vision models with language prompts to inter-
pret gestures and infer intent. GestLLM (Kobzarev
et al., 2025) uses large language models and fea-
ture extraction (MediaPipe) to recognize diverse ges-
tures without training. Our work moves beyond sim-
ple recognition by integrating gestures into a state-
machine framework that directly triggers behavioral
changes, such as initiating a ”follow” mode.
Object and Hazard Detection. Accurate object
recognition is vital for robot navigation. While clas-
sical methods like YOLO offer speed (Terven et al.,
2023), VLMs provide greater flexibility and contex-
tual understanding (Zhang et al., 2021; Li et al.,
2024). VLMs also excel in hazard detection. IN-
SIGHT (Chen et al., 2025) is a hierarchical VLM
framework for autonomous driving that integrates in-
puts to detect unpredictable scenarios and improve
safety. We utilize the VLM to detect both general con-
texts and specific dangerous objects, incorporating
this information into a state-machine-governed safety
protocol for an immediate switch to human control.
Distinction from Prior Work. Our approach in-
tegrates continuous scene understanding and adap-
tive control into a unified modular framework, ex-
tending capabilities beyond monolithic methods like
OpenFMNav (Kuang et al., 2024). Compared to
VLM-based systems—VLM-Social-Nav (Song et al.,
2024), BehA V (Weerakoon et al., 2024), and NaV-
ILA (Cheng et al., 2024)—our system offers critical
advancements. Where these often focus on naviga-
tion or fixed policies, our system leverages zero-shotVisual Language State Machine Robot

3 APPROACH
3.1 Robot
We use a RoverZero robot (Fig. 5) with a top-mounted
computer providing real-time visualization. The state
machine (external computer) sends messages to the
robot. The robot operates using ROS (Quigley et al.,
2009) and employs the Dynamic Window Approach
(DWA) (Fox et al., 1997) for Navigation. In this state,
the state machine sets maximum speed and inflation
radius parameters while the robot autonomously fol-
lows goals. In Follow and Remote states, the state
machine directly controls velocity.

3.4 Tracking
This module activates when a nearby person raises
a hand (detected by VLM). Tracking is performed
using Detectron2 (Wu et al., 2019), which runs lo-
cally and is faster than the VLM for identification.

son using segmentation and RGB-D data. At approx.
10 Hz, we assign positions by identifying the clos-
est updated center of mass. To handle occlusions,
the system stores the last known position and waits
a short interval. Proximity constraints stop the robot

3.5 Message Notification Application
Even state-of-the-art navigation can falter in haz-
ardous situations, so a readily activatable fallback is
essential.
In our implementation, VLM-detected objects are
validated by the state machine against a predefined
list of hazards (e.g., wet-floor sign, glass door); on a
match, the Message module is invoked.
We developed a notification application for An-
droid, using the Firebase (Firebase, 2011) protocol to
broadcast messages to all app users. The Message
module connects to a dedicated Ktor (Ktor, 2018)
server and sends the message: ”Request to Help
Robot: <dangerous −ob ject >”. All users receive
this notification.
The robot then halts and waits for the first user to
establish a connection. Upon connection, the robot
sends the message: ”Got Help, Thanks!” The user
then remotely controls the robot to move it away from
the dangerous object. See Fig. 7

Implementation runs on a PC (13th Gen i7, 32GB
RAM, RTX 4070) and a RoverZero robot (Intel NUC
i7, RPLiDAR A3, RealSense TD256 & D435). We
used Llama-Vision 11b.
We first evaluate VLM recognition capabilities,
followed by autonomous navigation trials in two cir-
cuits to measure expected switches against ground-
truth annotations.

4.3 Open-Ended Recognition with VLM
A key advantage of VLMs is their open-world adapt-
ability. To test this, we introduced a general ”haz-
ardous object detected” prompt to trigger Remotemode, bypassing predefined object lists. Experiments
with unseen hazards—specifically a ladder and a bar-
rier string—resulted in successful state transitions
(see Fig. 9), demonstrating the system’s capacity to
recognize and react to novel situations dynamically.
5 CONCLUSION
In this work, we demonstrate how a zero-shot sys-
tem can leverage image descriptions provided by
a VLM to dynamically adapt a robot’s operational
modes. Our approach integrates three key compo-
nents for mobile navigation: (1) adaptive navigation,
(2) gesture recognition, and (3) hazardous object de-
tection. By deploying this system on a real robot,
we validate its effectiveness and highlight the essen-
tial components required for practical implementa-
tion. This work offers a unified framework for high-
level robot control using zero-shot learning, allow-
ing for reliable decision-making without the need for
task-specific training. Although we expect VLM ca-
pabilities to continually improve, their successful per-
formance within our system indicates that they al-
ready offer much utility for controlling autonomous
robots.
6 LIMITATIONS
A key limitation of this work is the lack of temporal
awareness. The system only queries prompts based
on the current situation and ignores the past. While
this is effective for clear, short-term decisions, it may
be less suitable for long-term planning. Integrating
temporal logic could help address this limitation.
