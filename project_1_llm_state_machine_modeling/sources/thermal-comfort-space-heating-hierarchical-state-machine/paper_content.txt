Thermal comfort driven space heating control via hierarchical state 
machine strategy interacting with multiphysics simulation
Yunfei Bai, Chenghao Li, Sunku Prasad Jenne , Sizhe Zhang , Jihong Wang*
School of Engineering, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, United Kingdom

Co-simulation platformABSTRACT
Improving energy efficiency of space heating systems is as important as adopting emerging clean energy tech-
nologies for decarbonising heating. While exploring strategies for reducing energy consumption, the space oc-
cupants ’ expectations to thermal comfort should not be sacrificed. Conventional space heating control systems 
are often developed under the hypothesis of uniform temperature distribution in a bounded space and the 
heating system controller is primarily operated based on the information from the temperature sensor at a fixed 
position. That is, the measured temperature does not represent the temperature surrounding the occupant ’s 
location. This paper explores a new control method; the heating system control is led by the occupant ’s thermal 
experience, expressed by the temperature surrounding the occupant. A new hierarchical state machine control 
strategy interacting with a Multiphysics simulation model to dynamically estimate the temperature at the point 
of the occupant is developed. The accuracy of the Multiphysics simulation model is verified based on the cor-
responding experimental test room data. A co-simulation platform is built to allow COMSOL and SIMULINK to 
communicate in real-time for control strategy realisation. The experimental results show that the maximum and 
average mean square error between the measured and simulated temperature under different operating modes is 
less than 0.21℃ and 0.06℃, respectively. The simulation results indicate that energy consumption could be 
potentially reduced around 20% with an improved thermal comfort of the occupant by 30%, compared with the 
conventional fixed sensor control method.
1.Introduction
In the past ten years, CO2 emissions from power sectors in the UK has 
been greatly reduced because of the rapid increase in power generation 
from renewable energy sources [1]. However, the emissions from 
heating are almost unchanged [2]. It is impossible to achieve the Net 
Zero goal without decarbonising the heating as it contributes nearly half 
of the total energy consumption, mainly using gas, in the UK, in which 
57 % is used to meet domestic space heating and hot water demands 
[3,4] . Electric heating using power from renewable energy can promote 
heating decarbonization and proper electric heating management may 
support flexible grid operation to allow increased integration of 
renewable energy [5]. So, electrifying heating, including direct elec-
trical heating, is considered as one of the feasible ways forward [6–8]. 
Currently, most of the electric heating control systems are developed 
based on model-free approaches, which is based on the ambient tem-
perature measured by the temperature sensors at the fixed locations 
without considering the influences of the thermodynamics of the building space and the thermal experience of the occupants. Achieving 
more efficient space heating control is highly appreciated in the UK, and 
worldwide [9]. Essentially, the space heating control system is expected 
to provide better scheduling to avoid wasting heat and potentially 
zoning in the room to allow varying space conditions [10].
To include the thermodynamic characteristics into temperature 
control, thermodynamic modelling is essentially required [11,12] . 
Thermodynamic models can be analytic with the fundamental physical 
mathematical equations [13], or no-analytic data driven models [14] or 
the mixed physical analytic equation descriptions with data driven 
model blocks [15,16] . In [17], a dynamic energy performance model is 
built with considering realistically measured physical parameters to 
analyse and evaluate the retrofits in buildings based on the derivation of 
physical equations and the assignment of values to the parameters in the 
model using prior knowledge. Broujeny et al. [18] reported modelling 
heating dynamics of buildings by a data-driven machine learning 
approach and then applied this model in space heating control system.

2.Multiphysics simulation model for space thermodynamic 
analysis
Multiphysics simulation considers the interaction process of multiple 
physical fields in a system with the purpose of simulating and analysing 
the system ’s behaviour more comprehensively. COMSOL Multiphysics is 
a software with finite element analyser and solvers built in, which is 
suitable for analysing engineering systems with coupled Multiphysics. 
COMSOL Multiphysics software is chosen in the paper for a bounded space thermodynamic modelling to gain the space temperature distri -
bution while considering the influence of multi-physical fields such as 
heat transfer in solids and fluids and fluid flow.
2.1. Multiphysics simulation system setup
In this study, a kitchen room in the authors ’ research laboratory is 
chosen as a test space as shown in Fig. 1(a), which could represent 
general type of rooms in a building. The dimensions of the room are 
listed in Table 1. The room is assumed to be exposed to the environment 
with an invariable ambient temperature. Some objects inside the room 
are not captured in the modelling if they have little influences on 
simulation results. An electric storage heater is installed inside the room 
as a heating source. The heater contains three electric heating modules 
with magnetite as a storage medium and uses a fan to extract the stored 
heat from the bottom of the heater into the space. This storage electric 
heater is well insulated, so during operation, the surface temperature is 
just slightly different from the ambient temperature and primarily uses 
forced convection method to heat the space. An experiment was con-
ducted to compare the average surface temperature of the electric 
heater, the ambient temperature, and the temperature of the hot air 
released from the bottom of the heater during its operation. Based on the 
test, even the heater operated at its maximum power of 3 kW, its surface 
temperature was only 3.1℃ higher than the ambient temperature, 
whereas the hot air released from the heater was 28.7℃ higher. With the 
support of these test results, the effect of radiation from the heater was 
not considered in the simulation to simplify the model.

3.Hierarchical state machine control strategy
Hierarchical State Machine (HSM) decomposes the state machine 
model of an entire system into multiple layers, each layer being 
responsible for a specific function or module. Compared to conventional 
control strategies such as setpoint control and PID control, the HSM 
control strategy offers several advantages, including enhancing modu -
larity, maintainability, and scalability in system design and operation 
[28].This approach allows for a more structured and flexible system, 
facilitating easier updates and improvements while maintaining per-
formance across different operational conditions. In this paper, the HSM 
is introduced into the control of storage electric heating system, which 
benefits greatly from HSM ’s modularity; a three-layer HSM strategy in 
association with the operating power of the electric heater is proposed, 
which is illustrated in Fig. 2.
The storage electric heating system used in this paper is Quantum 
series electric heater QM150RF which is an advanced product made in 
the UK. This storage electric heater contains three electric heating 
modules inside, each module has a working power of 1 kW, and uses a 
fan to extract the stored heat out from the bottom of the heater into the 
space. The average air velocity from the fan is 1 mEs. As shown in Fig. 2, 
the storage electric heater has three modes of operating power, namely 
Mode 1 (1 kW), Mode 2 (2 kW) and Mode 3 (3 kW).
The storage electric heater can either heat the space using only stored 
heat or by combining electric heating with stored heat. While using only 
stored heat conserves electricity, it results in a longer response time to achieve occupants ’ thermal comfort. This study considers both energy 
consumption and response time, establishing two operational states for 
each mode: fast heating (FH) and normal heating (NH). The switch be-
tween these states is driven by temperature feedback: the heater oper-
ates in fast heating mode when the temperature change exceeds the set 
deviation range (ΔT), and in normal heating mode when it remains 
within the range.
The co-simulation platform to accommodate the HSM control sys-
tem, the Multiphysics model and the interface for data communication is 
illustrated in Fig. 3. The hierarchical state machine control strategy 
receives the feedback temperature and changes at each control time 
step. Then, it formulates the working power of each heating module in 
the storage electric heating system and the heat release rate of the 
heating system. Through real-time interaction, after receiving the ac-
tions formulated by the hierarchical state machine control strategy, the 
Multiphysics model performs a Multiphysics simulation to predict the 
temperature distribution in the room. After the occupant ’s position is 
obtained, the Multiphysics simulation results at that position are 
extracted and fed back to the control strategy.
The models for evaluating human thermal comfort consider different

for assessing thermal comfort [33]. It takes a wide range of factors to 
human perception of thermal environments into account, providing 
valuable insights for creating comfortable indoor spaces. The detailed 
calculation process of the PMV-PPD model is provided in Appendix B.

4.Experimental system setup
An experimental test system is established including NI CompactRIO 
controller, NI thermocouple input module, NI voltage output module, 
sensors, driving circuit, and storage electric heater to validate the nu-
merical accuracy of built Multiphysics simulation model. The structure 
of the test system is shown in Fig. 5, where K-type thermocouple sensors 
are deployed in the test room and the temperature is measured through 
NI 9213 thermocouple input module. The power supply for the heater ’s 
three heating modules and fan is connected to the NI 9263 voltage 
analog output module through a drive circuit which consists of four 
operational amplifiers, four solid state relays and a power supply. The 
thermocouple input module and voltage analog output module are 
connected to the NI CompactRIO controller. Finally, the temperature 
data collection and heater control programs are coded in LabVIEW and a 
user interface (UI) front panel is designed to interact with the 
components.
During the test, the sensor temperature data is monitored and 
collected through the LabVIEW control UI by switching on the fan and 
different heating modules of the heater respectively. Temperature data 
of the indoor space were collected under different heater modes and 
mode transitions. A total of six tests were conducted, each lasting for one 
hour, with several days between each test. The six tests were as: (1) 
operate the heater at 1 kW power; (2) operate the heater at 2 kW power; 
(3) operate the heater at 3 kW power; (4) operate the heater at 3 kW 
power, then switch to 2 kW power; (5) operate the heater at 3 kW power, 
then switch to 1 kW power; (6) operate the heater at 2 kW power, then 
switch to 1 kW power. Finally, the collected temperature data from the experiments were compared with the corresponding Multiphysics 
simulation results to verify the numerical accuracy of the simulation 
model.
5.Experiment test, simulation study and results analysis
This research is simulated based on multi-software platform co- 
simulation. The process of multi-software platform co-simulation is 
shown in Fig. 6. First, the 3D model is built in INVENTOR software, 
which fed the dimension information to COMSOL via the LiveLink 
interface. Then multiple physical fields and boundary conditions are 
added to the 3D model in COMSOL to simulate the temperature change 
in space during the operation of the electric heating system. Then, the 
hierarchical state machine control strategy is built in SIMULINK soft-
ware, and LiveLink interface realizes the real-time interaction between 
SIMULINK and the Multiphysics simulation model in COMSOL. Finally, 
the co-simulation is completed in SIMULINK software environment. 
During the co-simulation phase, the simulation interval was set to one 
minute.
All simulations for this study were conducted on computers equipped 
with AMD Ryzen 5600H 3.30 GHz processors, 16 GB of RAM, NVIDIA 
GeForce GTX 1650 GPU, and 64-bit Windows operating systems. Three 
control systems are studied, namely HSMC@h, HSMC@d, and 
HSMC@o. In the Multiphysics simulation model, the indoor space is 
divided into eight regions, with the centre positions of each zone shown 
in Fig. 7(a). During the experimental validation, thermocouple tem-
perature sensors were fixed at these centre positions. The coordinates of 
the eight sensors in this space are (1910, 810,1610), (1910, 1510,1610), 
(1910, 2210,1610), (1910, 2910, 1610), (1310, 810, 1610), (1310, 
1510, 1610), (1310, 2210, 1610), and (1310, 2910, 1610). In order to 
test the performance of different space heating systems, it is necessary to 
provide a trajectory of the occupants ’ activities in the indoor space. In 
this paper, the activity trajectory of a resident is obtained by random 
ordering: 5-F3-F2-F7-F6-F4. Assuming that the occupants stay in each 
area for half an hour, the resulting time-location path is shown in Fig. 7
(b). The trail takes a total of 3 h.

systems are presented in Fig. 10. Fig. 10(a), (d), and (f) depict tem-

control strategy is to maintain the temperature surrounding the occu-
pant within the comfort range of 22 •C –23 •C. During the three-hour 
simulation, temperatures at occupant activity locations under the 
HSMC@h control system frequently fell below the comfort range. Under 
the HSMC@d control process, temperatures at occupant activity loca-
tions were often above the comfort range. In the HSMC@o control

maintained within the comfort range. The thermal discomfort of occu-
pants during the control process was quantified using a thermal

time for all three control systems. After 40 min, the thermal discomfort

discomfort of occupants showed significant fluctuations. In the subse -

gradually decreased. At the end of the 3-hour simulation, the thermal 
discomfort indices for occupants under HSMC@h, HSMC@d, and

A. Comparison of results between cylinder and line models of the human body in physics simulation
A Cylinder is one of the simplest ways to represent the human body and it is often used in thermal field simulations [34–36]. However, it is typically 
used in physical field simulations where the position of the human body is fixed. The position of the human body is in reality moving frequently, and to 
obtain the temperature around the body during this process, the Cylinder human body model needs to change with the position of the body in the 
physical field simulation. However, in continuous Multiphysics simulations, a change in the position of the Cylinder model will inevitably lead to a 
change in the solution mesh, and the position of the Cylinder model cannot be set as a controlled variable in COMSOL because it is a parameter that 
constitutes the geometry. Although moving mesh can contribute to the solution of the problem, its introduction can complicate the solution and 
greatly increase the solution time. In reality, the temperature around the human body is measured using temperature sensors, but deploying a large 
number of sensors in a space is not feasible. Therefore, this paper explores adding line domains in a 3D spatial Multiphysics simulation model and 
using its average temperature to approximate the air temperature around the human body. Since the line model does not account for the human body ’s 
impact on the thermal environment, this paper conducts Multiphysics simulations with both the line and cylinder human body model, comparing the 
results to analyse the performance of the line model in simulations.
According to reference [36], the cylinder human body model has a height of 1.6 m, a radius of 0.14 m, a surface area of 1.41 m2 and is suspended 10 
cm above the ground. In order to obtain the air temperature around the human body, this paper adds a cylinder surface 1 cm outside the human body 
model, as shown in Fig. A1(a). This approach ignores the different thermal sensitivities of various body parts and uses the average temperature of the 
cylinder surface as the air temperature around the human body, as indicated in Eq. (A1).
