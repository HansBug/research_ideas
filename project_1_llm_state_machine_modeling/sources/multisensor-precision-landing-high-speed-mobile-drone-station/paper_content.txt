Abstract
Battery-powered multicopter drones are constrained by limited range and endurance due to the low energy density of onboard
batteries. To overcome these limitations, mobile drone stations capable of battery charging and replacement have been
introduced. However, achieving precision landings on high-speed mobile stations–especially when they are initially beyondthe visual range of the drone’s onboard sensors–remains a signiﬁcant challenge. To address this, this paper proposes a precision
landing approach that enables the drone to reliably land on a high-speed mobile drone station, even when the station initially
lies beyond the visual range of the onboard camera. Speciﬁcally, we present a multi-sensor-based localization method thatleverages the complementary characteristics of the global positioning system, ultra-wideband, and camera vision’s sensory
data to robustly estimate the relative position and orientation of the mobile station. We further introduce a dynamically feasible
three-phase ﬂight guidance strategy that aligns the drone’s motion with the station’s velocity, ensuring smooth and accuratelandings. Building upon this, to support the proposed method, we design a dedicated hardware conﬁguration for both thedrone and the mobile station. We validate our approach through real-world ﬂight experiments, demonstrating consistent and
successful landings on a mobile drone station traveling at speeds up to 25 km/h and starting from distances exceeding 20 m.

2 Related Work
Precision landing is a critical capability for achieving full
autonomy in drone operations. Numerous studies have
actively investigated this topic [ 2,3]. A notable milestone was
2017 MBZIRC competition [ 4–9], which included a mission
requiring a drone to autonomously land on a vehicle moving
at 15 km/h. [ 4–7] employed MPC(Model Predictive Control),
which requires a dynamic model of the drone. Speciﬁcally,[4,5] used a dynamic model of the vehicle as well, and they
utilized RTK(Real-Time Kinematic)-GPS for drone local-
ization. [ 8] also relied on a dynamic model for a nonlinear
controller. In contrast, [ 9] employed a model-free visual ser-
voing approach. Despite the diverse methods proposed, the
competition was held in a constrained environment with vehi-
cle speeds below 15 km/h and relied solely on visual detectionof a ﬁducial marker. This restricted the applicability of the
approaches to more general or high-speed scenarios.
Beyond competitions, [ 10–13] address autonomous drone
landing on moving platforms in practical contexts. In [ 10],a drone lands on a ground vehicle equipped with a Stew-
art platform, demonstrating capability beyond visual range.However, the system relies on RTK-GPS and has not been
validated at speeds above 20 km/h. In [ 11], an indoor exper-
iment combines UWB and vision-based localization, yet thespeed is low, and the test environment is spatially constrained.[12] presents a vision-based landing without RTK-GPS or
drone dynamics, but it only works within visual range and is
validated only at low speeds. [ 13] proposes an MPC-based
high-speed landing at 12 m/s from long range, but is validated
only in simulation.
As summarized in Table 1, our approach offers several
advantages over prior works. First and foremost, while prior
works are constrained in speed, reliance on environments, our
method enables precision landing from 20 m away at speedsover 20 km/h. Furthermore, our method operates robustlywithout requiring RTK-GPS. Although RTK-GPS provides
high-precision positioning, its operational ﬂexibility is con-
strained by high cost, the time required to acquire an initialﬁx, and reliance on a dedicated base station, which restricts
the operational range. Additionally, we employ a model-free
approach for both the drone and the station. We deﬁne model-free as an approach that does not require a dynamic model.
The presence of signiﬁcant modeling challenges for both the
station and the drone in our scenario necessitates this model-free approach. The station’s dynamics are unknown to thedrone because the mobile station can be mounted on any
platform, causing the model to change with each deployment.
Furthermore, even if a model is identiﬁed, the station is pas-sive and non-cooperative, making its motion fundamentally
unpredictable. Although a drone’s dynamics can typically
be modeled, our scenario involving high-speed, low-altitudeﬂight presents challenges in altitude estimation, which in
turn leads to signiﬁcant modeling inaccuracies. A model-free
approach is therefore well-suited to overcome these chal-lenges. Moreover, our approach is validated in real-worldexperiments.
3 Multi-Sensor-Based Station Localization

the top of the station and estimates the full 6-DoF(Degree-of-
Freedom) relative pose of the station, including both positionand orientation. The UWB sensor measures the distance
between a tag and an anchor. Unlike indoor setups with mul-
tiple anchors for 3-DoF localization [ 14,15], our outdoor
scenario with a single station provides only 1D range. Lastly,
the GPS provides the 3-DoF global position of the station.

4 Flight Guidance for Precision Landing
The precision landing procedure is divided into three primary
phases: (1) Trajectory generation and following, (2) Decel-
eration, and (3) Terminal descent with PID(Proportional–
Integral–Derivative) control. The overall landing procedureis depicted in Fig. 4as a state machine. The landing procedure
begins by ensuring that the drone is at an initial point. Once
the drone reaches the initial point, the drone generates a tra-
jectory toward a landing point above the station and followsthe trajectory. Since the station is moving, the drone must ﬂy
at a higher speed to catch up and align with the station. To
achieve this, the horizontal speed of the drone is consistentlyset to exceed that of the station by a ﬁxed margin v
0.
As the drone approaches the station, it continuously evalu-
ates the horizontal distance to the station. When the distancefalls below a threshold, the drone stops trajectory generationand transitions to the deceleration phase. The objective of this
phase is to reduce the relative horizontal velocity between the
drone and the station to zero. Since the drone is initially fasterthan the station in this phase, a smooth and controlled decel-
eration process is crucial. To achieve this, the target speed
is discretized into a sequence of decremental values. Duringthe deceleration, the drone maintains a ﬁxed height. Also, the
drone’s horizontal velocity always points toward the landing
point, and its magnitude is limited by the current step in thetarget speed proﬁle used by the PID controller.

zero and the marker is detected, the system transitions to
the terminal descent phase, which is governed by a PID
controller. The horizontal velocity commands are generatedbased on the position error between the drone and the landing
point. To ensure a safe and accurate descent, descent com-
mands are only issued when two conditions are satisﬁed: (1)The horizontal distance between the drone and the marker

To safely land on a high-speed station, achieving zero rel-
ative velocity is essential. The drone generates a minimum
snap trajectory to guide it along a linear path aligned with thestation’s velocity. This trajectory generation process requirestwo inputs derived from the station’s GPS data, which is
transmitted to the drone via a communication link. The ﬁrst
is the station’s velocity, taken directly from the GPS data,and the second is the station’s position, estimated from the
same data using the method described in Sect. 3. The trajec-
tory is deﬁned by three keyframes: start, intermediate, andendpoint. The start point is the drone’s position for the initial
path generation. Thereafter, to ensure a smooth connection,
the start point is updated to the last target point the drone wasfollowing on the previously generated path. The end point,identical to the landing point, is located at a height h
0above
the station marker. To quickly align the drone with the path,
the intermediate point is set on the 2D xy-plane of {G}by
ﬁrst projecting the trajectory start point perpendicularly onto
a line parallel to the station’s velocity through the end point.
The intermediate point divides the segment from this foot tothe end point in a 1:2 ratio. Real-time generation and tracking
of this trajectory enables the drone to achieve zero relative
velocity, even when the station moves along a curved path.
4.1.2 Pure Pursuit
To follow the generated trajectory, we adopt the computa-
tionally efﬁcient Pure Pursuit algorithm [ 17]. By using only
the geometric properties of the minimum snap trajectory, this
model-free method is robust to modeling errors and avoidsthe computational load of the controller. This allows the
drone to dynamically adjust its speed to the station’s motion
through a velocity control scheme. The horizontal velocityof the drone, which is managed by the PX4’s internal PIDcontroller, is directed toward a target point on the path and
set to exceed the station’s speed by v
0, while a PD controller
independently adjusts vertical velocity and yaw to match thetarget point and heading. At each control loop, the horizon-
tal distance to the target point is measured, and when it falls
below a look-ahead distance, the target point is updated tothe next point. The look-ahead distance is set based on thedrone’s speed, with a minimum value to ensure both tracking
accuracy and smoothness.

4.3 Terminal descent with PID control
During deceleration, if the drone detects the marker or
reaches the landing point, the speed limit is lifted and thesystem transitions to terminal descent. If the marker is not
detected due to errors in station localization, the drone initi-
ates a search by ascending to a height h
max. After reaching
hmax, if the marker remains undetected for /Delta1T1, the landing
is aborted and the drone hovers.
Once the marker is detected, the drone maintains its height
and aligns horizontally and in yaw with the marker using
separate PID controllers. While the marker is detected, the
system records the horizontal offset eVision between the GPS-
and marker-based positions. The recorded eVision corrects the
landing point when the marker is lost. After the marker is
detected again, the horizontal distance error stays within a
threshold for /Delta1T2, the drone begins descending at a con-
stant vertical speed. If the marker becomes undetectable due
to the limited onboard camera’s FOV(Field of view) near
the marker, the drone continues descending at a horizontalvelocity matched to that of the station.

6 Conclusion
We propose a long-range precision landing on a high-speedmobile drone station by combining three sensors: Vision,UWB, and GPS. We ﬁrst conduct experiments to comparethe localization accuracy of the station under various com-
binations of these sensors. The results of station localization
show that combining UWB and GPS yields more accurateestimates than using GPS alone. Moreover, when the vision
sensor detects a ﬁducial marker, using vision alone pro-
vides the highest accuracy. Accordingly, the proposed systemselects the appropriate sensors for station localization based
on the relative distance between the drone and the station.
The precision landing, based on the results of multi-
sensor-based estimation, consists of three phases. The ﬁrstphase guides the drone toward the station along a straight path
aligned with the station’s velocity, using a minimum snap tra-
jectory and pure pursuit-based following. The second phaseapplies PID control with stepwise speed limits to decelerate
the drone. The third phase performs the terminal descent with
PID control. Without requiring any dynamic model of boththe drone and the station, the proposed method adaptively
generates velocity commands according to the changing sta-
tion velocity. As a result, the proposed method achieveslong-range precision landing on the high-speed mobile drone
