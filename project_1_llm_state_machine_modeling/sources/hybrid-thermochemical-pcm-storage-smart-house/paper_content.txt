Abstract 
The development of integrated renewable en ergy and high-density thermal energy 
storage systems has been fueled by the need for environmentally friendly heating and 
cooling in buildings. In this paper, MiniSt or, a hybrid thermochemical and phase-change 
material storage system, is presented. It is equipped with a heat pump, advanced elec-
tronics-enabled control, photov oltaic–thermal panels, and flat-plate solar collectors. To 
optimize energy flows, regulate charging and discharging cycles, and maintain opera-tional stability under fluctuating solar irradiance and building loads, the system utilizes 
state-of-the-art power electronics, variable-frequency drives and modular multi-level 
converters. The hybrid storage is safely, reli ably, and efficiently integrated with building 
HVAC requirements owing to a multi-layer co ntrol architecture that is implemented via 
Internet of Things and SCADA platforms that  allow for real-time monitoring, predictive 
operation, and fault detection. Data from the MiniStor prototype demonstrate effective 
thermal–electrical coordination, controlled energy consumption, and high responsive-
ness to dynamic environmental and demand conditions. The findings highlight the vital 
role that digital control, modern electronics, and Internet of Things-enabled supervision play in connecting small, high-density th ermal storage and renewable energy generation. 
This strategy demonstrates the promise of electronics-driven integration for 
next-generation renewable energy solutions an d provides a scalable route toward intel-
ligent, robust, and effectiv e building energy systems.

[10–12], though most are limited to labora tory-scale prototypes. Numerical simulations 
have modeled TCM kinetics and integrated storage systems [13,14]. Center for Research

Matlab/Simulink [10]. As part of the Horizon 2020 project MiniStor [15], a working pro-
totype has been deployed at CERTH’ s Smart Home in Thessaloniki [16]. 
Advances in power electronics have become critical enablers for optimizing the 
performance, control, and reliability of such  renewable energy systems and their associ-
ated TES solutions. In particular, power el ectronic components such as wide bandgap 
semiconductors, for example, silicon carbide (SiC) and gallium nitride (GaN), offer 
higher efficiency, higher switching frequencies, and improved thermal management, 
enabling more compact and reliable converters  for integrating PV, solar thermal, and TES 
technologies into building energy systems. 
Innovative circuit topologies such as multi-level converters and soft-switching de-
signs minimize switching losses and electrom agnetic interference, improving system ef-
ficiency and lifespan, which is crucial for residential and commercial heating and cooling 
applications. The modular design of power electronic systems facilitates scalability and 
maintenance, enhancing uptime and adaptability in smart grid environments. 
Control strategies increasingly leverage predictive and adaptive algorithms to 
manage energy flow dynamically, optimizing  charging and discharging cycles of TES 
while coordinating with the electrical grid  or smart building energy management sys-
tems. Advanced digital control processors , embedded sensors, and IoT connectivity 
support real-time monitoring, fault detectio n, and predictive maintenance, enhancing 
reliability and longevity of both power electronics and TES units. 
Energy storage control systems have been widely studied by energy practitioners. 
PCMs have been widely studied for latent thermal energy storage, but control strategy 
research is focused primarily on building and heating, ventilation, and air conditioning integration rather than hybrid TES systems. In PCMs, control strategies usually aim to 
optimize charging/discharging to reduce energy consumption and peak demand. They 
are either rule-based (on temperature threshol ds or time schedules), optimization-based 
(through predictive control within building energy management systems), or de-
mand-response-based [17]. 
With TCM, on the other hand, due to its comp lexity, as it relies on reversible chem-
ical reactions for storing and releasing energy,  explicit control systems are less mature 
and often rely on the control of the chemical reaction rather than a dedicated hybrid TES 
framework [18].

2.1. Description of the System 
The MiniStor system is compos ed of four main subsystems: 
1. Solar Field—includes photovol taic–thermal (PVT) and flat-plate solar (FPC) thermal 
collectors, along with a buffer tank for heat generation. 
2. TCM Reactor—a thermochemical reactor paired with an ammonia (NH 3) sorption 
cycle. 
3. Heat Pump and PCM Storage—a heat pump integrated with both hot and cold 
phase-change material (PCM) tanks. 
4. Battery System—powered by electricity from  the PVTs via an inverter to cover in-
ternal electrical loads (e.g., pumps, compressors). 
An overview of the subsystems and their integration with the building’s heating, 
ventilation, and air condit ioning (HVAC) system is shown in Figure 1 [10]. 
 
Figure 1. Overview of MiniStor th ermal system components. 
MiniStor is installed at CERTH’s Smart Home in Thessaloniki, Greece (40.56° N, 
22.99° E). The building has two floors and 317.7 m2 of habitable area (182.7 m2 ground 
floor, 135 m2 first floor). Currently used as an office space, it operates mainly during 
business hours. MiniStor covers the heating and cooling requirements of a 49 m2 room via 
a dedicated fan coil unit (6 kW heating, 4 kW cooling) directly connected to the PCM 
tanks. All PCM tanks are filled with materials based on sodium acetate trihydrate. Piping 
between the solar field, container, and Smart Home is insulated and buried to minimize 
heat losses. 
In winter, heat is supplied in two modes: 
Charging Mode:  Heat for salt desorption is primarily provided by the solar field, 
with a 2 kW e auxiliary heater if needed. Heat input initiates the endothermic decomposi-
tion reactions in the TCM reactor, generating gaseous NH 3. The gas is compressed, con-
densed, and stored. The condensation heat is upgraded by a heat pump to 336 K to meet 
building heating demands. 
Discharging Mode:  This mode is used during nights or cloudy days. Stored NH 3 
evaporates when ambient temperature is lowe r than the reactor’s equilibrium pressure. 
The resulting exothermic reaction releases heat  (330–336 K) in the reactor, which is stored 
in the hot PCM tank.

2.2. Control of the System (Hardware) 
The thermochemical subsystem uses NH 3 (R717) as the working fluid and includes 
the TCM tank, an NH 3 receiver, an oil separator, a flooded evaporator, an NH 3 separator, 
and multiple valves and sensors, as shown in Figure 4. During the charging mode, NH 3 
vapor is extracted from the TCM tank by a variable-speed compressor and condensed 
into the NH 3 receiver. During discharging, liquid NH 3 is expanded through an EEV 
(electronic expansion valve) into a flooded evaporator connected to the cold PCM tank. Critical auxiliary hardware such as a desuperheating valve, a hot gas bypass line, and an 
oil-return solenoid ensure stable operat ion under open-loop dynamics. Key measure-
ments include pressure, level, and temperature sensors throughout the NH
3 circuit.

heat pump, and thermal PCM buffers. It is driven by multiple circulation pumps and features control valves, strainers, and expansion vessels. The hydraulic topology allows 
dynamic routing of heat to or from the TCM unit, hot PCM, DHW tank, or cold PCM 
modules.

the compressor (Figure 7). These subsystems  address the unique requirements arising

https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics15020279  Oil Management and Recovery : As the NH 3 circuit operates in open-loop 
mode—unlike the typical closed-loop refrigerat ion cycles—oil return to the compressor 
cannot rely on standard internal circulation. To address this, an oil separator is installed 
on the compressor discharge line. An oil solenoid valve is used to control the return of 
separated oil to the compressor. This valve is  periodically triggered by the TCM control-
ler, depending on the compressor operating ti me and the calculated pressure ratio, en-
suring continuous lubrication and compresso r protection. With the compressor running, 
when the discharge to suction pressure ratio (PR) exceeds the configured setpoint, the oil-return solenoid valve is activated for 2 sec and then is inhibited for 60 sec before any 
subsequent activation. In addition, if PR stay s above the setpoint continuously for 60 sec, 
a single pulse opens the valve for 2 sec (high pressure ratio assist). The valve’s actual state is fed back to the trigger logic to block overlapping commands and prevent imme-
diate retriggers, ensuring cl ean, time-accurate pulses. 
 
Figure 7. Hardware configuration. 
Compressor Pressure Ratio Control : The compressor’s rotational speed is regulated 
using a variable frequency drive (VFD) in order to maintain the desired pressure ratio during the charging mode. If VFD regulation alone is insufficient to keep the pressure 
ratio below the defined limit (typically PR < 5.5), a hot gas bypass valve is activated. This 
valve redirects a portion of the compressor’s discharge vapor back to the suction line, 
thereby reducing the effective pressure ratio and keeping the compressor within its safe 
operational envelope. 
Thermal Protection Measures : To prevent excessive superheat at the compressor 
suction, especially when high-temperature solar heat is supplied to the TCM, a desu-
perheating mechanism is implemented. This consists of a thermostatic expansion valve 
combined with a solenoid valve, installed on the compressor suction line. When the 
compressor discharge temperature exceeds a set threshold (typically 363 K), the solenoid 
is activated for 2 sec, to expand liquid NH
3 into the suction line. This evaporating liquid 
reduces the suction gas temperature, provid ing thermal protection for the compressor. 
2.3. Control of the Systems (Software) 
To ensure comprehensive and efficient control of the thermal system, a modular 
architecture was developed, composed of independent yet interconnected functional 
submodules, each addressing a specific system component. 


--- Page 10 ---
Electronics  2026 , 15, 279 10 of 30 
 
https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics15020279  One of these submodules is responsible for thermal demand management, with its 
primary function being to determine whether th e system is capable of supplying heat to 
the end user. This decision is based on real-t ime sensor data and weather forecasts, al-
lowing for dynamic evaluation of current and future operating conditions. 
Another submodule oversees solar thermal generation, coordinating its activation 
based on the availability of solar resource s and the system’s temperature levels. This 
component enables efficient utilization of solar energy by adjusting its use according to 
production conditions. 
A third submodule manages the thermal batteries (PCMs) used for both heating and 
cooling. Its control logic analyzes the state of charge in the storage units, the availability 
of thermal sources, and signals from the PC M controller to determine optimal charging 
and discharging times. It also processes heat  discharge requests based on system demand 
and the operational status  of associated devices. 
The fourth submodule is dedicated to the control of the TCM reactor, managing its 
charging and discharging cycles through a rule-based logic system. This logic relies on 
key signals such as internal temperatures, st ate of charge, operational status, and energy 
availability. Based on these signals, the operational state of the TCM is determined and subsequently interpreted by the system’s overall state machine. 
All the described submodules generate status signals that are interpreted by a state 
machine. This state machine governs the tr ansitions between the system’s operational 
modes, based on logical conditions that combine inputs from the four submodules, 
physical sensors, and weather data. Each transition is defined as a logical expression that 
simultaneously evaluates multiple system va riables. All this code was implemented in 
JavaScript using Node-RED (Figure 8). 
 
Figure 8. Energy management system (EMS) control submodules and data flow in Node-RED. 
From a methodological pers pective, the MiniStor EMS implements a hierarchical, 
rule-based control strategy organized as a finite-state machine. The upper supervisory 
layer encodes the admissible operating modes of the system (standby, pressuriza-
tion/pre-heat, charging, depre ssurization, discharging, and safety modes) and the logical 
conditions for transitions between them, including time delays and hysteresis bands that 
prevent chattering. The lower layer comprises the four functional submodules and local 
regulators that act on individual subsystems (TCM reactor, PCM tanks, HP and so-lar/HTF loop). In this layer, on–off rules an d threshold-based logic controllers are used to 
track temperature and pressure setpoints, ma intain the compressor pressure ratio within 
its safe envelope, and enforce operational lim its such as maximum condenser and suction 
temperatures. Together, this architecture provides a transparent mapping from meas-
urements and user requests to actuator commands and ensures that every operating 
mode corresponds to a well-defined, reproducible configuration of pumps, valves, and compressors.

https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics15020279  The adopted control approach belongs to th e class of deterministic, rule-based su-
pervisory energy management systems coordinated through a finite-state machine. Control decisions are derived from explicit logical rules formulated a priori, based on 
thermodynamic constraints of the CaCl
2–NH 3 reaction, phase-change temperature limits 
of the PCM units, compressor operating enve lopes, and safety requirements of the NH 3 
circuit. System operation is th erefore governed by a discrete set of admissible operating 
modes and submodes, each associated with a predefined configuration of actuators and 
safety interlocks. This choice reflects the prototype-oriented nature of MiniStor and the need for robust, transparent, and certifia ble control behavior under highly coupled 
thermofluid dynamics and variable solar input. 
The rule set implemented at both supervisory and local levels was synthesized from 
a detailed analysis of system operating conditions, as documented during the design 
phase. In particular, temperature and pressure thresholds were defined to ensure: (i) 
sufficient vapor pressure for stable NH
3 circulation during chargi ng and discharging, (ii) 
compliance with compressor pressure-ratio limi ts, (iii) compatibility between heat-source 
and heat-sink temperatures at the heat pump interfaces, and (iv) reliable phase-change 
processes within the PCM storage units. Tr ansition rules incorporate minimum dwell 
times, hysteresis bands, and co nditional delays to prevent oscillatory behavior caused by 
transient solar fluctuations or short-term load variations. 
During the monitoring and commissioning phase of the MiniStor demonstrator, it 
became necessary to adjust low-level control st rategies to ensure safe and efficient oper-
ation under real-world conditions. Although the system was properly sized, specific 
adaptations were required to respond to loca l climate variability, demand profiles, and 
the behavior of the installed subsystems. 
The main improvements included revising  control modes, redefining operational 
thresholds, and optimizing fault-handling lo gic to enhance system responsiveness and 
robustness. One of the most relevant change s was the implementation  of a solar safety 
mode as a backup in case of failure of th e primary controller, which acts autonomously 
upon detection of critical temperatures to protect thermal components. 
Adjustments were also made to correct th ermal imbalances affecting the heat pump 
and storage systems. A pre-heating mode was introduced to ensure the heat pump op-
erates within optimal ranges before initiati ng the thermochemical storage charging. In 
addition, summer-specific dissipation strate gies were added to prevent shutdowns due 
to overheating. 
In the TCM subsystem, control logic was stabilized by enforcing minimum dura-
tions in pressurization phases, avoiding oscillatory behavior caused by solar fluctuations. 
Pressure and temperature limits were refined, and the NH 3 tank charge thresholds were 
adjusted to better reflect th e system’s reaction kinetics. 
Finally, the charging strategy for the PCM storage was revised to eliminate thermal 
losses and reduce charging times. It was observed that simultaneous charging and dis-
charging hindered phase transitions, so this condition was discouraged to improve thermal storage efficiency.

4.2. Pressurization and Charging Phase 
Prior to the initiation of the TCM charging phase, the pressurization sequence 
(Mode 1) from the standby mode is required. With the reactor isolated and all NH 3 side 
valves closed, the HTF pump is enabled once the solar buffer exceeds a temperature 
setpoint (usually > 328 K), and hot HTF circulates through the reactor jacket while the NH
3 circuit remains locked. As jacket heating proceeds, the reactor temperature rises to 
~339 K and the reactor pressure increases rapi dly toward ~2.5 bar, consistent with the 
Clausius–Clapeyron dependence of NH 3 vapor pressure on temperature. This 
pre-heating process causes the reac tor material to desorb vapor NH 3, elevating the suc-
tion pressure of the compressor. Throughout most of Mode 1, the receiver liquid level 
remains essentially constant—no mass transfer  is expected while the valves are closed 
and the compressor is idle. According to th e TCM control specification, Mode 1 termi-
nates when the tank pressure exceeds its setp oint (~2.5 bar) and is re-entered if the 
measured compressor pressure ratio later surpas ses its limit (e.g., PR > 5.5) or the TCM 
pressure falls below the threshold of 1 bar. 
Continuing with the charging phase, Figu re 18a,b captures the supervisory logic 
that alternates between Mode 2 (charging—NH 3 compressor active) and Mode 1 (pres-
surization) to keep the TCM subsystem within its envelope, ensuring safe operation.

conditions imposed on the reactor were kept within a narrow band, showing an average value of 329.5 K at the inlet temperature to the reactor and typically ranging ±1.5–4 K,

speed pump operating at constant flow mode . These measures ensure that externally 
induced fluctuations did not propagate into th e TCM system and affect the control logic.

along with the HP operation, discharge pressure again stabilizes initially near 18–20 bars, 
then gradually declines toward ~12–14 bars as the load and available TCM desorption 
heat and condensing temperature decrease. Suction pressure follows the same trend, 
falling from ~6 bar to ~3–4 bar, as the reactor driving force weakens. Additionally, the 
increase in the operating pressure ratio alon g with the compressor speed regulation leads 
to higher electricity cons umption, up to 0.8 kW. 
4.3. Depressurization and Discharging Mode 
When cold is requested and stored liquid NH
3 in the receiver is available, the su-
pervisory controller (high-level and low-le vel control) commands Mode 3 (depressuriza-
tion) and then Mode 4 (discharging). In this phase, neither the NH 3 compressor nor the 
VC heat pump operate, making this process simpler to control. In Mode 3, all NH 3-side 
valves remain closed, and the reactor is hydr aulically isolated. Since the presented data 
from discharging phase concern a summer-period operation where only cooling is re-
quired, the HTF circuit is routed through a heat -rejection path (fan-coil to ambient or, if 
beneficial, the hot-PCM loop operated as a si nk) and circulated through the reactor jack-
et. The objective is to lower the reactor temp erature and pressure to a temperature target 
(<333 K), so that the reactor pressure is lower than the evaporator pressure by a small, controlled margin. This condition ensures a stable vapor driving force from the evapo-
rator toward the reactor once the discharge begins. 
Upon satisfaction of this inequality, the controller transitions to Mode 4 (discharg-
ing). Liquid NH
3 from the receiver is transferred by gravity to a flooded evaporator, 
whose level is maintained by an on/off float switch located in a small liquid–gas separa-
tor placed upstream. The evaporator extracts heat from the cold-PCM circuit, while the produced vapor flows freely back to the reacto r where it is absorbed by the salt, causing 
an exothermic reaction whose heat is reject ed to ambient via the HTF loop. Discharging 
continues until the receiver liquid level reac hes the lower liquid level threshold (e.g., 
8%), at which point the mode is terminated.

5. Conclusions 
The MiniStor project has demonstrated th e successful design, development, and 
deployment of a novel compact hybrid th ermochemical–PCM thermal energy storage 
(TES) system integrated with advanced powe r electronics for sustainable heating, cool-
ing, and electricity storage in residential buildings. The system harnesses a 
high-performing CaCl 2/NH 3 thermochemical reaction combined with hot and cold PCM, 
delivering an exceptionally high overall energy storage density. This compact design ef-
fectively addresses the limitati ons of existing TES technologies by enabling long-term, 
seasonal thermal storage in a minimal footprint, making it highly suitable for retrofitting existing building stock in European cities. It further highlights the transformative poten-
tial of integrating advanced electronics wi th renewable energy and high-density thermal 
storage in residential buildings: 
• Electronics-enabled control and optimization:  Multi-level converters and 
VFD-driven compressors enable precise management of energy flows between the 
solar field, TCM reactor, heat pump, and PCM storage. 
• IoT and SCADA integration:  Real-time monitoring and supervisory control plat-
forms facilitate predictive operation, fault detection, and automated response, 
demonstrating how electronics and communi cation technologies enhance system 
reliability. 
• Efficient thermal–electrical coordination:  The combination of TCM–PCM storage 
with intelligent power electronics suppor ts stable heating and cooling delivery, 
demonstrating the feasibility of compact, high-density energy storage systems in 
smart buildings. 
• Scalability and adaptability:  Modular electronics and digital control architectures 
provide a blueprint for flexible deployment in urban energy networks, enhancing 
RES integration. 
All features considered, MiniStor is a pr ime example of how sophisticated electron-
ics, control schemes, and IoT-enabled monito ring may hasten the implementation of ro-
bust, efficient, and sustainable building energy  systems. Overall, this work confirms that 
the synergy between emerging power electronics technologies and advanced thermal energy storage solutions is vital in accelerati ng the transition toward sustainable, effi-
cient, and resilient energy systems for heat ing and cooling. Continued research and de-
velopment in component materials, control algorithms, and system integration will be instrumental in overcoming the remaining technical barriers and achieving large-scale 
adoption. Future work will focus on extending the high-level EMS toward 
grid-interactive operation and more advanced  optimization frameworks, potentially in-
cluding two-stage robust formulations and th e explicit use of building thermal inertia 
and external waste-heat sources as additional flexibility options, in line with recent de-
velopments in low-carbon operation of remo te hybrid energy systems. Furthermore, re-
cent developments in decentralized resilience-oriented restoration frameworks, such as 
those coordinating building thermal flexibility with external mobile resources, may also
