Abstract
Underground mine inspection is a critical operation for safety and resource management.
It presents unique challenges, including confined spaces, harsh environments, and the
lack of reliable positioning systems. This paper presents the design, development, and
evaluation of an Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) specifically engineered for supervised
autonomous inspection in subterranean scenarios. Key technical contributions include
mechanical adaptations for collision tolerance, an optimized sensor-actuator selection
for navigation, and the deployment of a mission-governing state machine for seamless
autonomous acquisition. Furthermore, we detail the data treatment workflow, employing a
multi-modal point cloud registration technique that successfully integrates high-resolution
visual-depth scans of critical mine pillars into a comprehensive, globally referenced map
derived from Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) data of the entire workspace. We
show experiments that illustrate and validate our approach in two real-world scenarios, a
simulated coal mine used to train mine rescue teams and an operating Limestone mine.
Keywords: unmanned aerial vehicles; underground mine inspection; sensor fusion; point
cloud registration; autonomous inspection
1. Introduction
Advances in commercially available drone technology, lower costs of electronic com-
ponents, and expanded research in Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) have opened op-
portunities to deploy these vehicles in the most unexpected territories. One of the most
valuable fields in which UAVs have been deployed is inspection across many sectors,
including buildings [ 1,2], infrastructure [ 3,4], agriculture [ 5], and mining [ 6,7]. The mining
industry has increasingly adopted UAVs for both surface and underground inspections,
as detailed in the comprehensive review by Shahmoradi et al. [ 6]. The proliferation of
user-friendly and affordable multi-rotor platforms over the last decade has accelerated this
trend [ 8,9]. However, significant hurdles remain, as was highlighted by a recent survey [ 10],
which emphasizes the persistent challenges in Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS)-
denied environments and identifies critical needs for enhanced autonomy, advanced sensor
integration, and better interoperability within mining infrastructure.
Besides the lack of GNSS signals, there are numerous challenges when deploying
multi-rotor drones in an underground mine, including a limited range of radio-based
teleoperation and harsh conditions such as humidity, dust, and, more importantly, dark-
ness. Modern algorithms and the increasing use of cameras are driving the widespread

adoption of Visual Inertial Odometry (VIO) across many GNSS-denied environments [ 11].
In illuminated mines, pure VIO can be highly reliable. However, a significant challenge
arises when inspecting mine sectors where lighting is not maintained, rendering this ap-
proach insufficient. In such cases, additional sensor technology and different algorithms are
required. One of the most effective solutions in these situations is the use of Light Detection
and Ranging (LiDAR) sensors, along with corresponding LiDAR Inertial Odometry (LIO)
algorithms. A few commercial drone models use this approach, including the Skydio
X10 [ 12], which uses infrared lights to navigate in the dark, and the ElioS 3 [ 13], which
features a hybrid LiDAR and camera system.
Current platforms lack real-world validation data demonstrating their capacity to
maintain localization and mapping integrity under severe noise, heavy dust, and illumina-
tion variance characteristics of active mining sites. While recent initiatives have begun to
address these issues [ 14], the technical literature demonstrating integrated UAV mapping
systems that operate consistently in uncontrolled environments is scarce. Much of the
research is limited to validation in controlled environments, rather than the dynamic, harsh
conditions of active industrial mines [ 15]. This gap in the literature is often exacerbated
by the current market. While commercial drones capable of subterranean mapping exist,
their high cost and closed architectures often limit their accessibility and adaptability for
specific research or industrial needs. This work focuses on a hands-on experimental ap-
proach, fusing established hardware and software into a modular, cost-effective platform
specifically tailored for underground mine inspection. Rather than proposing entirely new
algorithms, our contribution lies in the integration and real-world validation of a system
that can reliably perform autonomous missions in GNSS-denied, unlit, and dusty environ-
ments. By providing a detailed account of the hardware trade-offs and the multi-modal
data fusion process, this research demonstrates a practical methodology for achieving
engineering-grade 3D reconstructions using an open-architecture system.
2. Related Work
The deployment of autonomous robotic systems in subterranean environments
presents a unique intersection of challenges involving robust mechanical design, resilient
onboard real-time state estimation, and high-fidelity data acquisition, as well as inherent
deployment challenges that can significantly affect their effectiveness. These challenges
include limited flight time and the need for onboard computers to process data and execute
flight control algorithms, which must be lightweight yet sufficiently capable of handling
complex computational demands, often involving trade-offs between overall performance
and payload capacity [ 14]. Additionally, integrating various sensors such as high-resolution
cameras, LiDAR, and thermal imaging devices adds complexity, weight, and size, further
reducing flight time while enhancing the vehicle’s operational capabilities.
To contextualize this work’s contributions, it is worth noting that this is part of a
broader mine safety research project and is related to the work presented in [ 16–18]. Given
the multidisciplinary nature of the research, it is relevant to explore the existing literature
across three foundational domains: UAV mine inspection, navigation in GNSS-denied
environments, and 3D reconstruction techniques.
2.1. UAV Mine Inspection
The mining industry, like most industries in modern society, is currently undergoing
a transformation characterized by the integration of computational systems to remove
human operators from hazardous environments [ 19]. Traditionally, underground inspection
requirements for geotechnical assessments, monitoring, and analysis have been done
manually, exposing surveyors to risks such as rockfalls, toxic gas accumulation, and heavy

machinery traffic. One of the most advanced technologies in recent years has been the
Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM)-enabled handheld scanners [20,21].
Early efforts in the field of robotic mine inspection focus on Unmanned Ground Ve-
hicles (UGVs). One of the primary works was done by Thrun et al. [ 22], which presents
a mobile robot that builds maps of abandoned mines. While UGVs offer significant ad-
vantages in payload capacity and battery life, their utility in active mining zones is often
constrained by the terrain, which limits their ability to inspect difficult-to-access areas.
More recent advances have shown the utility of heterogeneous robot teams for exploring
mines. The winning team of the DARPA Subterranean Challenge in 2021 demonstrated
the individual advantages of UGVs and UAVs, as well as the use of different payloads
depending on the task [ 15]. Thus, recent literature has shifted focus toward UAVs, which
can provide the necessary agility to bypass ground obstacles and access vertical structures.
However, aerial platforms impose significant constraints on flight time and payload ca-
pacity. Unlike ground vehicles, multi-rotor UAVs must continuously expend energy to
overcome gravity, necessitating highly efficient hardware and software [ 23]. To address
these limitations, a leading strategy is to deploy heterogeneous teams that combine the
endurance of UGVs with the agility of UAVs [16,24].
When operating a UAV underground, it is important to distinguish between the
three applications discussed in the literature, as they dictate different architectures
and algorithms:
• Navigation: is the safe transit of a robot from a start pose to a goal pose. In un-
derground settings, this is complicated by the symmetric design of mines, narrow
corridors, and a harsh environment. Recent work has focused on topological graph-
based navigation to efficiently move through tunnel networks without requiring
dense maps [25].
• Exploration: refers to the autonomous navigation of completely unknown environ-
ments. Exploration algorithms often use Frontier-Based strategies, in which the robot
prioritizes moving to the boundary between known and unknown space. While
effective for autonomous navigation and search-and-rescue tasks, these strategies
often yield sparse, incomplete, or textureless maps that are inadequate for structural
engineering assessments [18,26,27].
• Inspection: differs from exploration by assuming that the general structure or a
specific region of interest (ROI) has been identified, and that the robot’s goal is to
gather high-fidelity data on that target [ 16]. Inspection is the main focus of this paper.
For inspection tasks, UAVs are typically equipped with a combination of LiDAR
sensors and depth cameras to ensure robust navigation and data capture. LiDAR-based
UAVs are shown to be more robust and reliable, but have a higher cost and weight. Depth
cameras are limited to short distances and are much more sensitive to lighting conditions.
Therefore, more complex approaches use multiple sensors and integrate them, at the cost
of high power consumption and increased weight, which affect the vehicle’s overall size.
Deploying UAVs underground requires hardware architectures that can survive harsh
conditions, such as dark, humid, and/or dusty environments. Commercial solutions like
the Flyability Elios 3 [ 13] use collision-tolerant cages to protect the drone, with the trade-
off in the aerodynamic and weight. On the academic side, research platforms prioritize
modularity to adapt to different constraints. For example, the Prometheus system [ 14] uses
a reconfigurable frame for narrow passages, and the Oxpecker [ 16] uses a tether to bypass
battery limits. The main hardware challenge across all these designs is balancing heavy
sensors—like 3D LiDARs and high-power lights—with the weight and power limits of a
multi-rotor drone. This trade-off directly dictates flight time and mission achievements.

2.2. Navigation in GNSS-Denied Environments
Reliable localization is the fundamental condition for robotics. Outdoors, GNSS
provides absolute positioning with bounded error. However, underground mining envi-
ronments are GNSS-denied, forcing robotic platforms to rely entirely on onboard sensors to
estimate their state (position, velocity, and attitude) relative to a local reference frame. This
process, known as dead reckoning, accumulates error over time (drift), necessitating sophis-
ticated sensor fusion strategies to bound this uncertainty [ 30]. Some relevant approaches
are VIO, LIO, and SLAM.
Visual Inertial Odometry fuses high-frequency inertial measurements from an Inertial
Measurement Unit (IMU) with visual features extracted from camera images. The IMU
provides accurate short-term motion estimation, while visual features such as corners,
edges, or distinctive texture patches are tracked across frames to correct the inertial drift.
Some state-of-the-art algorithms used for SLAM and VIO are Kimera2 [ 31], Fast ORB-
SLAM [ 32], VINS-Mono [ 33], ROVIO [ 34], and OpenVINS [ 35]. These tightly-coupled
frameworks combine camera and IMU states into a single nonlinear optimization graph or
filter, enabling robust estimation even during aggressive maneuvers. VIO is convenient
https://doi.org/10.3390/robotics15030063

--- Page 5 ---

when features are present in the context. However, the application of VIO in underground
mines faces critical limitations, including a lack of distinct features and airborne dust,
which can lead to catastrophic divergence of the estimator. This problem, in a more general
context, is called navigation in degraded environments [36].
To overcome the dependence on ambient light and surface texture, active sensing
methods have become a standard for harsh industrial environments. LiDAR sensors emit
laser pulses to measure distance, making them independent of direct light sources. One of
the initial research studies presented by Zhang and Singh [ 37] demonstrated that geometric
features could be used to register sequential scans and estimate motion. This approach
has evolved into tightly coupled smoothing frameworks such as LIO-SAM [ 38], which
integrate IMUs to handle the rapid dynamics of UAV flight. Most recently, FAST-LIO2 [ 39]
marked a significant advancement by using an incremental kd-tree (ikd-tree) data structure
and allowing direct registration of raw points to the map without explicit feature extraction,
reducing computational load and increasing robustness in unstructured environments.
In mining applications, where dust can degrade visual data, the geometric consistency
provided by LIO is often superior to that of visual methods.
It is important to distinguish between pure odometry (VIO/LIO) and SLAM. Odome-
try focuses on local consistency, estimating motion from one step to the next. SLAM adds
information to build a map concurrently. Most recent research has added loop-closure
capabilities to recognize previously visited locations and correct accumulated drift across
the entire trajectory. While SLAM provides global map consistency, it is computationally
more expensive than pure odometry [39–41].
2.3. Reconstruction Techniques
Underground 3D reconstruction techniques can be grouped into three primary cate-
gories: image-based photogrammetry, visual SLAM, LiDAR SLAM, and hybrid approaches
that combine elements of these methods. Depending on the mission requirements, each
category offers different trade-offs between reconstruction fidelity, robustness to environ-
mental conditions, and suitability for real-time autonomous operation in GNSS-denied
subterranean environments. These approaches have been applied in UAV-based under-
ground inspection and mine mapping systems that employ LiDAR-based and hybrid
SLAM pipelines for autonomous 3D reconstruction [16,42,43].
Photogrammetry is the process of obtaining accurate measurements from images to
produce 3D models or maps. Structure from motion (SfM) is an evolved photogrammetric
method for generating high-fidelity 3D reconstructions [ 44]. With multi-view stereo (MVS),
SfM can be used for rapid reconstruction even without information on the camera pose [ 45]
and, unlike plain photogrammetry, SfM-MVS is independent of scale [ 46]. SfM uses
keypoint matching to estimate camera poses and produce a sparse point cloud, while MVS
uses those estimated poses to calculate depth for most image pixels, producing a dense,
colored point cloud. While these pipelines enable high-resolution reconstructions with
lightweight, low-cost sensors, the quality is sensitive to illumination variability, motion
blur, and airborne dust. Furthermore, the significant offline computation required typically
limits their applicability for real-time, large-scale mapping in underground environments.
For online operations, Visual SLAM estimates sensor trajectories and incrementally
reconstructs the environment in real time using monocular, stereo, or RGB-D cameras.
Variants like VIO incorporate inertial measurements to improve the robustness of this
motion estimation [ 47]. While these methods generate visually interpretable maps using
compact, inexpensive sensors [ 48], their reliability depends heavily on scene appearance.
In subterranean settings, poor illumination, featureless regions, and dynamic elements
often lead to tracking loss or incomplete maps, particularly in large-scale flights [49–51].

Unlike image-based approaches, LiDAR SLAM relies on direct geometric range mea-
surements, enabling accurate spatial estimation independent of illumination conditions.
Integrating inertial measurements into LIO further improves motion estimation robust-
ness, providing precise localization even in dark, feature-poor environments [37,38,52,53].
Because the large, dense point clouds generated by these systems increase computational
and memory costs [ 54,55], they are often transformed into more compact voxel- or plane-
based maps, which reduce memory usage and enable efficient collision checking and path
planning [ 56]. Despite its robustness, LiDAR SLAM can still suffer from incomplete re-
constructions due to occlusions, limited sensor field of view, and loop closure failures,
particularly in large-scale environments [57–59].
Each of the above reconstruction methods presents complementary strengths and
limitations. Photogrammetry enables high-resolution offline reconstruction, Visual SLAM
supports real-time mapping using lightweight camera sensors, and LiDAR SLAM provides
robust geometric mapping independent of illumination. These trade-offs have motivated
hybrid SLAM approaches that combine vision and LiDAR sensing, as well as the joint
use of photogrammetry-derived and LiDAR point clouds to construct unified 3D maps,
thereby leveraging their complementary properties for accurate and reliable mapping in
complex environments [ 60,61]. Tightly coupled LiDAR–visual and LiDAR–visual–inertial
SLAM systems, such as FAST-LIVO2 [ 62], jointly optimize geometric, visual, and inertial
measurements to improve robustness and accuracy in real-time mapping.

3.3. Sensors and Payload for Data Collection
The sensors mounted on the drone serve two purposes: providing information to the
onboard computer and the flight controller for navigation, and collecting data for point
cloud reconstruction. The traditional sensors used for drone navigation are primarily
IMUs for acceleration, velocity, and position estimation; a barometer for estimating global
altitude; a compass to estimate global heading; a GNSS receiver for global positioning; and
a downward-facing range sensor for relative altitude. Due to the nature of the underground
mines, barometers and compass are unreliable, and GNSS is inaccessible, leaving us to
rely solely on IMUs and range sensors. This minimum configuration is enough to fly the
vehicle with inherent issues: drifting due to accumulated error and the lack of a global
reference frame. These issues ultimately make the system unstable and unreliable for
autonomous flights.
To address the accumulated error, two range sensors, one pointing up and one pointing
down, were added as a source of vertical positioning information. Additionally, to enrich
horizontal navigation information, a low-light-capable optical flow sensor with a Pixart
optical motion tracking sensor, capable of operating in low-light conditions (>9 lux), was
used. Once the robot was stable for underground flight, additional navigation sensors
were added to simplify its behavior and enable automated, low-power navigation. These

3.4. Hardware Integration
This section provides information on the UAV’s final design and its operation, includ-
ing flight time and power consumption under controlled conditions.
To deploy the UAV , an operator uses a remote controller (RC) for manual data logging
or to recover the vehicle in an emergency while supervised autonomous missions run.
A standard telemetry connection is established with a tablet running QGroundControl
(QGC) [ 66], which is mounted to the RC to monitor the drone’s status. Additionally,
a mobile access point is transported to a remote station, where a secure shell connection is
established to monitor, start, and stop the missions. Figure 4a shows this architecture. It is
important to note that mission control and the calculations required to generate velocity
commands are performed entirely on the onboard computer. Given that the UAV’s primary

3.5.3. Autonomous Pillar Inspection
The third mission focuses on high-resolution 3D reconstruction of specific mine pillars.
This mode requires a consistent point density, which is achieved by maintaining a fixed
distance and velocity relative to the structure. The drone follows a coverage path planning
strategy using a horizontal back-and-forth (sweeping) pattern. Since most pillars are
longer in the horizontal direction, a horizontal back-and-forth requires fewer stops at the
borders when compared to a vertical sweeping, thus yielding a faster and more efficient
coverage. The operator positions the drone in front of the target pillar and initiates the
mission. The algorithm adjusts the current distance to the wall and maintains it constant,
ensuring complete coverage of the pillar face. To execute the sequence of horizontal passes
at incremental altitudes, we use a single-point array of four LiDARs, with three pointing
forward to identify corners and one pointing up to measure the distance to the ceiling.
Using this combination, the pillar’s limits are identified in real time, keeping the process
simple and independent of complex SLAM algorithms.

4.1. Point Cloud Reconstruction
The initial 3D reconstruction is estimated using RGB-D SLAM and LiDAR-inertial
SLAM pipelines. The RGB-D SLAM method estimates camera motion and produces
dense maps in real time, while LiDAR-inertial SLAM methods directly reconstruct the
environment geometry using range measurements. In this work, RTABMap [ 69] is used
for RGB-D SLAM, while FAST-LIO2 [ 39] is used for LiDAR–inertial SLAM. RTABMap
used synchronized RGB and depth measurements from an Intel RealSense L515 sensor to
perform visual reconstruction.
LiDAR-based SLAM is particularly effective in subterranean environments due to its
robustness to illumination changes and its ability to capture accurate geometric structure
over large areas. As a result, LiDAR SLAM maps typically provide extended spatial cover-
age, often capturing entire mine sections. However, these maps generally lack fine surface
detail, texture, and visual information, which limits their suitability for detailed structural
inspection. Visual SLAM methods, while capable of producing visually interpretable maps,
are more sensitive to lighting conditions, dust, and texture degradation, which are com-
mon in underground settings. Large-scale geometric reconstruction is performed using
FAST-LIO2 [ 39], which estimates motion and mapping directly from range measurements
acquired by a Livox mid-360 LiDAR sensor.
The output of this stage is a globally consistent point cloud that serves as the geometric
backbone for subsequent reconstruction and refinement steps.
4.2. Point Cloud Registration
To construct a complete 3D map that combines global coverage with local detail, point
cloud registration aligns RGB-D-derived reconstructions with the global LiDAR-based
SLAM map. This process enables high-resolution local models, such as detailed pillar
reconstructions, to be integrated into a unified coordinate frame [43].
The registration pipeline follows a multi-stage approach that combines feature-based
initialization and geometric refinement. In the feature-based stage, keypoints are extracted
from both source and target point clouds, and Fast Point Feature Histogram (FPFH) [ 70]
descriptors are computed at these points to establish correspondences. An initial rigid
transformation is then estimated using a sample-consensus approach [ 43]. This initial
alignment is refined using the Normal Distributions Transform (NDT), which models the
reference point cloud as a set of local Gaussian distributions to improve robustness to
noise and partial overlap [71]. Finally, Iterative Closest Point (ICP) is applied to minimize
point-to-point residual error between the aligned point clouds, producing the final regis-
tration [ 72]. By anchoring locally detailed colored pointclouds to the globally consistent
https://doi.org/10.3390/robotics15030063

--- Page 16 ---
Robotics 2026 ,15, 63 16 of 25
LiDAR map, the resulting reconstruction preserves both large-scale spatial structure and
fine surface detail.
This reconstruction strategy allows for efficient mapping of extensive underground en-
vironments while supporting detailed inspection of critical structures, balancing scalability,
robustness, and reconstruction accuracy.
4.3. Photogrammetry
Photogrammetry is used to estimate high-resolution 3D reconstructions from im-
age data, typically through Structure-from-Motion (SfM) and Multi-View Stereo (MVS)
pipelines. In contrast to SLAM-based mapping, photogrammetric reconstruction is per-
formed offline and focuses on producing dense, colored point clouds with detailed sur-
face geometry.
In the proposed workflow, photogrammetry is applied to image data collected during
targeted inspection missions, such as close-range scans of mine pillars or structural features
of interest. These photogrammetry-derived point clouds provide greater local detail than
LiDAR-SLAM maps and capture surface texture and fine geometric variations that are not
observable in LiDAR-only reconstructions.
While photogrammetry offers high reconstruction fidelity, it is more computationally
demanding and sensitive to imaging conditions. Reconstruction quality depends on suffi-
cient image overlap, stable illumination, and minimal motion blur, and processing time
can be substantial for large datasets. For these reasons, photogrammetry is used selectively
to enrich the inspection process of specific regions of interest rather than as a standalone
solution for large-scale underground mapping.
5. Results
To test and validate the complete system, three types of experiments are performed.
We initially fly the drone in an environment equipped with motion-tracking cameras
(VICON), which serves as a position ground truth. Then we test the system and missions
in a simulated mine environment, and finally deploy them in an operational underground
limestone mine.

In all experiments reported in this paper, the robot’s configuration was version 3,
with the LIO as input to the EKF-based position estimation of the flight controller. The spe-

• LIO Algorithm: Uses FAST-LIO2 with default parameters and uses the Livox Mid-360

• VIO Algorithm: Uses OpenVins based on the Intel RealSense D457.
• Ground truth: VICON motion capture system with 32 cameras providing position
tracking at 200 Hz.

6. Discussion
This paper presented two main contributions. First, it describes the hardware inte-
gration of a UAV platform designed to carry a custom payload while withstanding the
harsh environmental conditions of an underground mine. Second, the implementation of a
multi-modal 3D reconstruction pipeline is presented and evaluated.
Rather than employing computationally expensive dynamic sensor switching, the pro-
posed drone architecture prioritizes reliability by selecting the appropriate localization
pipeline (VIO or LIO) pre-flight via ROS 2 launch files based on specific payload and
mission requirements. By executing distinct flight missions, the system captures a global
topology of the mine during one mission, and performs targeted autonomous inspections
to reconstruct detailed, texturized 3D models of specific pillars in another. To contextualize
the system-level trade-offs and limitations observed during these deployments, Table 7 pro-
vides a consolidated summary of the UAV’s physical and operational performance metrics.

The resulting system demonstrated a high degree of flexibility, successfully operating
in multiple flight modes: a manual stable flight with data logging and two supervised
autonomous modes. Data collection was validated in both manual and autonomous flights,
showing that the platform can serve as a reliable tool for both active remote inspection and
autonomous data logging. The system’s modular adaptability allowed for flight stability
with varying estimation sources. In the absence of any external odometry estimation,
the flight controller fuses the information from their sensors with the velocity estimation
of the optical flow. When VIO or LIO is accessible, either one can be used as a reference.
VIO is recommended in well-illuminated and texturized environments, whereas LIO is
recommended in environments with poor lighting.
A significant portion of the drone development focused on comparing the efficacy of
VIO against LIO in harsh conditions. While VIO showed promise in controlled environ-
ments, our results indicate that LIO remains superior for subterranean operations. This
aligns with current state-of-the-art hardware limitations. The visual algorithms frequently
suffered from drift or tracking loss due to the low-texture walls and intermittent lighting
characteristic of the mine. In contrast, LIO provided robust, low-drift localization regardless
of visual conditions. However, VIO remains a viable, lightweight, and less expensive alter-
native for specific sectors of the mine, where artificial lighting is present, or for surface-level
inspections where GNSS is still denied but visibility is high.
The additional contribution of this research is the integration of distinct sensor pay-
loads to generate multi-modal maps under uncontrolled harsh conditions. By merging
the global, large point clouds estimated by the mid-range LiDAR with the high-resolution,
texture-rich scans from the depth camera, the system produced a comprehensive digital
model of the environment. This mapping approach offers the “best of both worlds”: the
structural accuracy required for mine surveying and the visual detail needed to inspect
specific pillars or geological features.
Based on our field tests, general guidelines for payload selection can be established to
maximize mission efficiency:
• Deep Mine / Dark Zones: LIO configuration (e.g., using Livox Mid-360) is required.
The weight penalty is justified by the necessity of active sensing in total darkness.
• Well-Lit / Infrastructure Inspection: For inspections in lit corridors or near light
sources, the VIO configuration (e.g., using RealSense L515, RealSense D457, or Re-
alSense D435i) is preferable. This reduces the total weight, as the inspection payload
is simultaneously used for navigation.
• Multi-modal Missions: For complex scenarios requiring both global navigation and
detailed inspection, the combined payload is effective. The operator must account for
the reduced battery life due to the additional weight and plan for separate, sequential
flight missions (e.g., one for global mapping and one for pillar inspection) rather than
a single continuous flight.
While the developed platform is functional, field deployment highlighted areas for
hardware improvement. Beyond hardware constraints, environmental factors such as
airborne dust were effectively mitigated by the Livox Mid-360’s internal filtering, which
maintained localization stability even under high-particulate conditions. However, the com-
plete absence of ambient light in certain sectors still required the use of high-power onboard
LEDs for RGB-D data collection, thereby increasing the total power consumption. The cur-
rent frame size, while stable and standard, imposes weight penalties that limit flight
endurance. A more compact, foldable frame design would enhance portability and ma-
neuverability. Additionally, battery selection remains a critical trade-off. Transitioning to
higher-energy-density Li-Ion batteries could double the capacity of current Li-Po equiva-
lents with only a minor weight increase, further extending operational range. Ultimately,

despite these hardware and environmental trade-offs, the system demonstrates a viable and
robust methodology for subterranean inspection, bridging the gap between experimental
UAV research and the practical requirements of the mining industry.
