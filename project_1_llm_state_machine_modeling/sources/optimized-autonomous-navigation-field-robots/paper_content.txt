Abstract
Purpose This study introduces an optimized algorithm for autonomous navigation of field 
robots, aiming to improve navigation accuracy, reduce crop damage and shorten execution 
times in agricultural environments.
Methods The enhanced solution integrates advanced data filtering with sensor fusion 
techniques, combining LiDAR and IMU inputs to produce precise 3D point cloud repre -
sentations for reliable navigation in structured crop rows. Both the legacy and improved 
algorithms were evaluated through simulation and physical trials on the FarmBeast robotic 
platform.
Results The improved algorithm reduced traversal time by up to 33% on certain field sec -
tions and lowered crop damage by 25% compared to the previous version.
Conclusions Results confirm the robustness and effectiveness of the enhanced navigation 
system in complex agricultural field conditions, demonstrating its potential for practical 
deployment within farming automation.

Introduction
The automation of agricultural field tasks has gained increasing importance with the rise 
of precision farming, where accuracy and efficiency are essential (Raj et al., 2024 ). Auton -
omous navigation in structured crop environments, such as regulary spaced maize rows, 
introduces both challenges and opportunities, particularly when robots must minimize crop 
damage while maintaining high operational speed (Panda et al., 2023 ). The FarmBeast 
project, launched as a student collaboration, addresses these challenges by developing and

Autonomous navigation in agricultural robotics has advanced significantly through multi-
sensor fusion, intelligent perception, and adaptive path-planning approaches. Multi-sensor 
fusion remains a cornerstone of robust field navigation, enabling robots to operate reliably 
in dynamic and semi-structured environments. Rakun et al. ( 2022 ) demonstrated a practi -
cal sensor fusion–based localization framework for the Rovitis 4.0 vineyard robot, com -
bining odometry, IMU, and RTK GPS data via a sensor-specific state machine to achieve 
centimeter-level positioning accuracy under occluded and vibration-prone field conditions. 
Similarly, Lv et al. ( 2022 ) proposed a loosely coupled Extended Kalman Filter for agricul -
tural scene-based multi-sensor fusion, enhancing robustness when individual sensors fail or 
degrade. In a comparable line of research, Li et al. ( 2025 ) presented an octree-based LiDAR 
RRT navigation method for orchard robots, focusing on computational efficiency and high-
precision path following rather than sensor fusion integration.
In parallel, path-planning research has evolved from deterministic and heuristic algo -
rithms toward adaptive hybrid and learning-based approaches. Yahaya et al. ( 2022 ) pro -
vided a comprehensive taxonomy of agricultural robot path-planning methods, emphasizing 
hybrid strategies that integrate classical graph search with bio-inspired optimization to 
enhance adaptability in irregular terrain. At the same time, deep reinforcement learning 
(DRL) has emerged as a promising framework for real-time, model-free navigation. Baby et 
al. (2024 ) successfully applied DRL to a four-wheel-steer field robot for crop-row tracking 
and adaptive maneuvering, achieving robust navigation without prior maps.Collectively, 
these studies highlight a rapidly advancing research landscape where sensor fusion, adap -
tive path planning, and learning-based control jointly drive progress toward precise, reli -
able, and efficient autonomous navigation in agricultural environments.
Within this context, the FarmBeast project, launched as a student collaboration, addresses 
these challenges by developing and refining an autonomous agricultural robot designed for 
field navigation and participation in events such as the Field Robot Event (Bernad et al., 
2019 ).The Field Robot Event (2024) emphasizes navigation through structured crop rows, 
such as regularly spaced maize rows, without causing plant damage, making reliable navi -
gation a central task (Rakun, 2015 ). Through its annual development cycle, FarmBeast has 
progressively incorporated advanced technologies, including sensor upgrades and algorith -
mic improvements, to meet these demanding requirements (FarmBeast, 2024 ). In its lat -
est version, the platform integrates a Velodyne VLP-16 LiDAR (Velodyne, 2015 ) and an 
Xsens MTi-610 Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) (Xsens, 2015 ), which together enable 
high-precision 3D point cloud mapping of the environment. These sensor inputs are further 
processed using advanced filtering and fusion techniques, ensuring robust navigation per -
formance even under challenging field conditions (Popič et al., 2024 ).
The main objective of this study is to develop and evaluate an optimized autonomous 
navigation algorithm for the FarmBeast robotic platform. The work specifically aims to 
improve upon the previously developed legacy approach (Popič, 2021 ) by enhancing posi -
tioning accuracy, increasing traversal speed, and reducing crop damage. To achieve this, 
the optimized algorithm is systematically compared against the legacy system through both 
simulation and controlled physical trials. In doing so, the study demonstrates how targeted 
optimization of perception and control strategies can significantly improve the reliability

Materials and methods
This section outlines the hardware architecture, sensor fusion methods, and simulation tools 
employed to optimize the FarmBeast’s autonomous navigation, as well as the data filtering 
and control algorithms that enable precise operation in complex agricultural environments.
Hardware and Computational Setup  – FarmBeast is a modular agricultural robot plat -
form designed for adaptability across different tasks. Its computational framework com -
bines a Raspberry Pi and an Intel NUC. The Raspberry Pi manages actuator control, while 
the Intel NUC—equipped with an Intel i7 processor—handles computationally demand -
ing processes such as sensor data integration and navigation algorithms. Figure 1 shows 
the latest version of the FarmBeast robot, with both its mechanical design and practical 
implementation.
Sensors  – The FarmBeast platform is equipped with a Velodyne VLP-16 LiDAR and an 
Xsens MTI-610 IMU. The LiDAR captures 3D representations of the surrounding environ -
ment, producing a dense point cloud with a 360° horizontal field of view and a 30° vertical 
range, essential for accurate mapping and obstacle detection. The IMU provides critical 
orientation and acceleration data, utilizing quaternion-based orientation tracking (Zhang 
et al., 2020 ) to overcome the discontinuty limitations of Euler angles, thereby improving 
navigation robustness in agricultural conditions. This distinctive feature of the proposed 
algorithm replaces traditional Euler angles with quaternions to enhance robustness and reli -
ability during turns.
Comparative system overview  – The FarmBeast navigation system builds on a legacy 
framework (Popič, 2021 ) that provided the first implementation of LiDAR, IMU and odom -
etry-based navigation in structured crop rows. Several key elements of that system were 
retained in this work, including the hardware platform (Intel NUC and Raspberry Pi), the 
primary sensor suite (Velodyne LiDAR and Xsens IMU), and the general principle of using 
crop row detection to guide the robot along a centered trajectory .

Legacy approach to navigation –  The navigation system developed by Popič ( 2021 ) 
formed the basis for comparison with the optimized algorithm presented here. It was 
originally designed and tested in Robot Operating System (ROS, n. d.) and Gazebo (Open 
Robotics, n. d.), focusing on autonomous driving of a simulated agricultural robot in struc -
tured row environments. The algorithm utalized data from LiDAR, IMU and odometry to 
maintain robust navigation in fields with partial irregularities.
Row detection was performed using LiDAR measurements. Raw point clouds were 
restricted to a forward-looking region to reduce disturbances from leaves protruding into 
the inter-row space. By averaging distances from the left and right crop rows, the algorithm 
adjusted steering dynamically to keep the robot centered. For curved rows, deviations in 
LiDAR readings triggered proportional angular corrections and speed adjustments, reduc -
ing forward velocity when sharper turns were required.
At the end of a row, navigation switched to IMU-based control. When LiDAR no longer 
detected plants, the robot recorded its yaw orientation and executed a 180° turn to align with 
the next row. The direction of the initial turn (left or right) was set via a configurable launch 
parameter, enabling adaptation to different field layouts.
Data Filtering  – To manage the large volume of LiDAR data, a multi-stage filtering 
pipeline is applied. A voxel grid filter (Safe Software, n.d.) first reduces data density by 
grouping points into spatial cells, lowering computational load while preserving structural 
detail. Subsequently, a RANSAC (Random Sample Consensus) algorithm (Fischler & 
Bolles, 1981 ) identifies and removes ground points, isolating relevant data for navigation. 
This combined approach increases efficiency by focusing on essential features. Figure 2 
illustrates the difference between unfiltered and filtered data.
To further streamline processing, additional filtering was implemented to discard irrel -
evant data, keeping only points corresponding to crop rows near the robot. Several poly -

by a finite-state machine (FSM) implemented in ROS using the SMACH library (Bohren, 
n.d.). This structure enables flexible control across different navigation states, including row 
following, turning, and obstacle avoidance, while dynamically adapting to changing field 
conditions. The modular FSM design not only improves robustness but also facilitates the 
integration of new functionalities for future upgrades.

Navigation represents a core component of autonomous mobile robot operation, defining 
both the movement direction and control strategies required to achieve reliable field per -
formance. The input to the navigation algorithm consists of data obtained after filtering and 
limiting the sensor field of view. For reference, the start of the coordinate system (0,0) is 
defined at the beginning of the field, corresponding to the robot’s initial position. This data is 
then divided into two subsets: points located to the left and points located to the right of the 
robot. Each subset forms a smaller point cloud, which is processed independently .
During motion, the algorithm calculates the center point of each subset by averaging 
the X and Y coordinates of all points on the respective side. If one side lacks a sufficient 
number of points—typically indicating missing maize plants—the center is not computed. 
In such cases, the center from the opposite side is mirrored across the robot’s coordinate 
axis to generate a virtual point on the missing side. This process requires prior knowledge of 
maize row spacing, as the correct mirroring is achieved by adding or subtracting the known 
inter-row distance.
To validate functionality, the algorithm generates markers in Rviz, showing the positions 
of the computed centers. Figure 6 illustrates this process: the left panel displays the case 
when valid centers are available on both sides, while the right panel demonstrates a scenario 
where the right-side data is missing and replaced by a mirrored coordinate. This approach 
enables continuous navigation even in partially populated fields.
If neither side provides sufficient data to calculate a center point, the algorithm enters a 
loop to check whether the condition persists across several iterations. Should this situation 
remain unchanged, the system concludes that the robot has reached the end of the row and 
initiates the turning procedure.
Driving speed and steering corrections are determined by comparing the Y-coordinate 
differences of the calculated centers. Based on this comparison, the resulting center falls 
into one of three categories:

Turning - For successful navigation through the polygon test field, the robot must reliably 
turn into the next row after completing the current one. In this implementation, turning is 
based on orientation data from the IMU, represented as quaternions (Zhang et al., 2020 ). 
While quaternions provide a robust framework for handling rotations, they are less intuitive 
than Euler angles, as they use four parameters to represent three degrees of freedom. For 
certain conditions, such as controlled simulation environments with known orientations, 
Euler angles may suffice. However, in real-world operation, discontinuities (e.g., jumps 
from − 180° to + 180° or 0° to 360°) must be handled, making quaternions the preferred 
choice.

Results and discussion
The FarmBeast robot’s autonomous navigation system was evaluated under multiple sce -
narios to verify the improvements of the new algorithm compared to the legacy version. 
Key performance indicators—navigation speed, crop safety, and algorithm robustness—
were used to assess effectiveness in both simulated and physical environments. Figure 7 
illustrates a simulated cornfield created in Gazebo, while Fig. 8 presents the physical test 
setup with artificial maize rows used for comparative testing.
For each setup, ten trials were conducted with the old algorithm and ten with the new 
one, across both simulation and real environments. During each trial, navigation time was 
recorded and the number of maize plants knocked over was counted. Tests were terminated 
either upon successful traversal of the entire field or when more than five plants were dam -
aged. Only successful trials were included in the calculation of average driving time and 
crop collisions. Results are summarized in Tables 1, 2 and 5–6 for simulation tests, and 
Tables 3, 4 and 7–8 for controlled physical experiments with the real robot.

Crop damage and safety
Crop safety emerged as a key area of improvement. In straight-row tests, the new algo -
rithm reduced plant collisions by 88%, while in curved rows the reduction reached 91%. By 
contrast, the legacy algorithm frequently caused plant damage, particularly during turning 
and realignment maneuvers. These results confirm that the new algorithm achieves higher 
accuracy while preserving crop integrity.
Algorithm robustness and consistency
The robustness of the system was further validated by its consistency across repeated trials, 
depicted by Fig. 10. In structured row tests, the new algorithm successfully completed all 10 
runs, achieving a very low standard deviation of 0.39 s in traversal time. The legacy algo -
rithm, on the other hand, showed only a 60–70% success rate, with considerable variability 
and frequent failures during row transitions or obstacle encounters.

Conclusion
Overall, this study contributes by presenting the development and evaluation of an opti -
mized autonomous navigation system for agricultural applications. By comparing the new 
algorithm with its predecessor across performance metrics such as speed, accuracy, and 
crop preservation, the findings highlight the potential of autonomous field robots to play a 
significant role in precision agriculture and sustainable farming practices.
The modular structure of the proposed navigation framework also facilitates the integra -
tion of new functionalities for future upgrades. Specifically, the combination of advanced 
filtering methods, quaternion-based orientation tracking, and state machine control offers 
a scalable solution that can be extended to more complex agricultural scenarios. These 
design choices not only improve robustness and reliability but also establish a foundation 
for incorporating additional sensor modalities, adaptive decision-making, and task-specific 
behaviors.
The results confirm the hypotheses regarding improvements in navigation speed, accu -
racy, and crop safety. The enhanced navigation algorithm enables the FarmBeast robot to 
sustain high performance in terms of speed and precision while minimizing crop damage. 
In structured crop rows, the algorithm enabled the FarmBeast robot to maintain consistently 
high performance under regular field geometry. These enhancements led to statistically 
measurable improvements in traversal speed and reliability, as demonstrated by consistent
