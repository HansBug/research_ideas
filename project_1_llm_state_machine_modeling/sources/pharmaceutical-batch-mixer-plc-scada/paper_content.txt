Abstract  
This research results fr om the need for an effective, reliable, and highly flexible method of control of processes within a small 
pharmaceutical environment and will be programmed using Ladder Logic programming Language, Siemens Step 7, which 
entails integrating hardware and soft ware to provide dependable and effective operations. The mixer is controlled through a 
Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) system and programmed using Ladder Logic in Siemens Step 7 PLC 
Programming Software. Temperature control, agitation control, and ingredient addition sequences were all integrated by the 
automated system. The performance of the modeled system is validated through computer simulations.  The Batch Mixer is 
controlled using a Variable Speed Dri ve (VSD), enabling precise execution of the required processes throughout the entire 
mixing cycle. Each phase of the process is carefully controlled and monitored, with detailed simulation results demonstrating  
enhanced performance and efficiency in the pr oposed batch mixer system, as well as offering a mathematical model of the 
Batch Mixer and outlining the performance variables that the PLC systems use in detail. As a backup validation tool, Rockwell 
Automation's RSLogix 5000 PLC is used to verify the val idation. The study demonstrates the effectiveness of using Siemens 
STEP 7 PLC for automation and validation, and with RSLogix 5000 PLC for confirmation of validation, ensuring reliable and 
efficient pharmaceutical batch mixing operations . 
Keywords  
Automation, Batch Mixer, Human Machine Interface (HMI), Ladder Logic Diagram, Programmable Logic Controller (PLC), 
Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA)  
 
1. Introduction  
Automation makes use of control systems and information 
technologies to reduce the need for human work in the pr o-
duction of goods and services. In the scope of industrialisation, 
automation is a step beyond mechanisation [1] (Anon., 2015a). 
Whereas mechanisation provided human operators with m a-
chinery to assist them with the muscular requirements of work, automation greatly decreases the need for human sensory and 
mental requirements as well. An example is the Automated 
Teller Machine (ATM) used by the banks. Automation has 
had a notable impact in a wide range of industries such as the 
manufacturing, power and service  sectors [2] (Anon., 2015b). 
It requires a conscious effort on the part of plant managers and

2.1. Design Concept and Criteria  
The design concept is shown in Figure 3  as the proposed 
automated batch mixing system. The design criteria for the 
proposed batch mixer are as follows:  
1) The system should minimise spillages and human errors;  
2) Information on real-time plant performance should be 
made available to Management;  
3) Ingredients to be mixed should be readily available to 
the batch mixing system; and more so there should be 
automatic haulage of sugar crystals;  
4) Operation of valves and detection of level of material in 
the tank should be automatic; and  
5) Hard wired relay -contactor controls should be replaced 
with soft wired versions for efficient production  
2.1.1. Process Description  
The batch mixing processes are of both time and event 
driven operations. Th e following statement list for a reaction 
and blending process for which the process timing diagram is 
constructed is given as follows:  
Step 1: Prepare for batch mixing process for a nominal time 
of 10 mins  
a. Operator washes vessels  
b. Operator inspects washed vessels for debris  c. Ingredients are weighed  
d. Operator waits for START PB to close.  
Step 2: Charge the heating vessel with sugar crystals for a 
nominal time of 3 mins  
a. Initiated when operator presses START PB  
b. The sugar crystals are sucked into the heating vess el. 
Step 3: Initiate reaction for a nominal time of 45 mins  
a. Temperature controller set to MANUAL  
b. Additive added to sugar content and melted at a required 
temperature of 75 °C 
c. Cooling water ON after heating solution for 45 minutes  
d. Temperature then drops to 40°C 
e. Stirrer motor turned ON and stays ON until beginning of 
the drain operation.  
Step 4: Blend the sugar solution with other added additives 
for a nominal time of 60 mins  
a. Initiated after the start of reaction operation  
b. Ingredients are added  
c. Mix until homo geneous at a temperature of 75 °C. 
Step 5: Drain heating vessel for a nominal time of 45 mins  
a. Initiated when temperature falls to 40 °C 
b. Cooling water valve CLOSES  
c. Filter and Pump ON  
d. Mixing vessels stirrer motor turns ON  
e. Terminate stirring when heating vessel  level goes empty  
f. Heating vessel stirrer motor STOPS when vessel is 
empty.  
Step 6: Blend the sugar solution containing additives with 
preservatives for a nominal time of 60 mins  
a. Ingredients are added  
b. Valve OPENS to add distilled water  
c. Mix contents until ho mogeneous  
d. Distilled water valve opens.  
Step 7: Drain resultant contents (i.e. syrup) of mixing ve s-
sels to the storage vessels for a nominal time of 20 mins  
a. Mixing vessel stirrer motor stays ON  
b. Outlet valve opens  
c. Pump ON  
d. Storing vessel mixer motor turns ON  
e. Terminated when the mixing vessel goes empty  
f. Mixing vessel stirrer motor turned OFF.  
Step 8: Cool the syrup

3.1.3. SCADA HMI  Design and Implementation  
The design and implementation of the Human -Machine I n-
terface (HMI) for the automated pharmaceutical batch mixing 
system followed the ISA-101 stand ard to create an intuitive, 
efficient, and reliable system for operators to interact with the 
process. This HMI was designed to manage, monitor, and control 
the batch mixing operations while integrating seamlessly with 
the Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA)  system 
and the Programmable Logic Controller (PLC) . The design 
process, key features, and implementation steps are detailed 
below to illustrate the systematic approach taken.  
HMI Design Using ISA -101 Standard  
The HMI design was guided b y the ISA-101 h u-
man-centered approach , focusing on usability, task -specific 
screens, and ease of navigation for operators . The design 
incorporated a hierarchical structure to allow operators to 
view general information at a high level and drill down int o 
more detailed data as needed.  
Overview Display  
At the top of the hierarchy, the level 1 overview display  
provided a holistic view of the entire batch mixing process. 
This screen presented critical system status indicators such as 
batch phase progression, re al-time temperature readings, 
mixer speed, ingredient levels, and valve positions. A co l-
or-coded alarm banner was positioned prominently to signal 
any system issues, with red indicating critical alarms, yellow 
for warnings, and green for normal operations  as shown in 
Figure 22 . Navigation buttons allowed operators to easily 
access more detailed process -specific screens for further 
analysis or intervention.  
Process -Specific Displays  
Moving to the level 2 process -specific displays , operators 
could access deta iled screens dedicated to different stages of 
the batch mixing process, such as heating, mixing, and storage. 
For instance, the heating system display allowed operators to 
monitor and adjust the temperature and control the cooling 
system once the desired t emperature was reached. Similarly, 
the mixing system display provided real -time information on 
mixer speed, ingredient addition status, and homogeneity levels, 
allowing precise control over the blending process. The storage 
system display monitored syrup t ransfer, pump status, and 
valve positions, ensuring that all components of the batch were 
handled correctly. These detailed displays provided operators 
with the necessary tools to control the process more efficiently 
while maintaining situational awareness . 
Diagnostic Displays  
The level 3 diagnostic displays  offered a deeper level of 
detail for maintenance personnel or troubleshooting. These 
screens provided data on pump performance, sensor readings 
for temperature and flow rates, and valve statuses. Histor ical 
data and trends were also made available to help diagnose 
issues and optimize future processes. A detailed alarm log was 
included to track all system alerts, including timestamps, 
causes, and operator responses. This diagnostic level ensured that any anomalies could be identified and addressed quickly, 
minimizing downtime and improving system reliability.  
Layout and Graphics  
In terms of layout and graphics , the HMI followed ISA-101 
guidelines  to maintain a clean and consistent design across all 
levels.  Colors were used sparingly, with green representing 
normal operations, yellow for warnings, and red for critical 
alarms, ensuring operators could easily interpret system st a-
tuses. Standard symbols for tanks, pumps, and valves were used 
to provide a famili ar interface and minimize confusion. The 
overall navigation system was designed to be simple, with 
breadcrumb navigation helping operators track their location 
within the system and easily return to higher -level screens.  
Alarm Management  
Alarm management  was a critical component of the HMI, 
adhering to both ISA-101 and ISA-18.2 standards . The alarm 
system was designed to prioritize alerts, with critical alarms 
receiving more prominent visual and audible notifications. O p-
erators could acknowledge and clear alarms, but critical issues 
required confirmation before being dismissed. Additionally, all 
alarms were logged for later review, aiding in troubleshooting 
and process optimization. This structured approach to alarm 
management helped reduce alarm fatigue an d ensured operators 
remained focused on the most pressing issues.  
HMI Implementation  
The HMI system was implemented using InTouch Wo n-
derware SCADA software . The interface was designed to allow 
real-time communication between the PLC and the HMI, e n-
suring t hat operators received up -to-date information on the 
process status. The system included touchscreen controls , which 
enabled operators to adjust parameters such as temperature, 
motor speeds, and valve positions directly through the interface. 
Graphical tre nds and charts displayed real -time data for key 
process variables, helping operators identify any irregularities or 
deviations during operation. The integration with SCADA a l-
lowed for comprehensive data logging and remote monitoring, 
further enhancing the system's functionality .

Discussion of Simulation Results  
Saraswathi [19] created an automation system for a mixing 
process that may be applied in the pharmaceutical, food, 
chemical, and process industries as well as in oil refineries. The entire suggested system is controlled by a Programmable 
Logic Controller (PLC). Ladder Diagrams, which are very 
user-friendly and simple to grasp, are used to program the 
MicroLogix 500 Rockwell Automation software. With the aid 
of a stirrer, the system automatically mixes the solutions from 
two separate tanks ba tch by batch in the third tank.  
In contrast the  automation project utilized Siemens S7 -300 
TIA Portal for simulation validation, which ensured reliability

88 and accuracy by comparing with real -world data, testing, and 
performance. High -perfo rmance computing and model si m-
plification helped to overcome issues with data integration, 
system complexity.  
The batch system discusses the complete batch production 
process, including heating, mixing, storage, and bottling. The 
accuracy of the simulation  model was demonstrated by the RS 
Logix 5000 Emulate, which was used to validate the results. It 
showed an average variation of less than 5% and a correlation 
coefficient of 95%.  
A 20 percent boost in predicted output, which led to an 
additional annual re venue increment, was another notable 
financial benefit of the method. Increased safety and a 20% 
decrease in accident and defect rates are further advantages.  
To support different batch mixer installations, the ladder 
logic software's I/O mapping, timing,  control logic, recipe 
management, and alarm/notification settings must be mod i-
fied. 
5. Conclusions  
Automating a mixing or blending operation does not only 
lessen operator error and variations; ultimately, it increases 
overall process efficiency in multipl e ways. The Batch Mixer 
System was successfully automated using Siemens S7 -300 
TIA Portal and InTouch Wonderware SCADA HMI Sof t-
ware’s. The simulation results were validated using the Sie-
mens S7 -300 TIA Portal software.  
This work also demonstrates that PLC is a better solution to 
the current batch mixer operated at the pharmaceutical co m-
pany. As for reliability, development time and system pe r-formance concern, PLC is better compared to relay logic 
implementation.  
The automation of the PLC -SCADA based batch m ixer 
provides safe and reliable system for the batch mixing process, 
which will increase the total production output thereby 
yielding significant financial benefits and savings.  
The ladder logic program can easily be used to control any 
similar Batch Mixer  Setup with some form of modification in 
the program.
