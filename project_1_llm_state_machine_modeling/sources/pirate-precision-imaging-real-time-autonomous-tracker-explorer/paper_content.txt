Abstract
We present PIRATE (Precision Imaging Real-time Autonomous Tracker and Explorer),
a fully autonomous unmanned surface vehicle designed to enable self-operating data
collection and persistent tracking of mobile underwater targets through the tight integration
of acoustic localization, onboard visual perception, and closed-loop navigation. PIRATE
employs a single mobile acoustic receiver to estimate target position using time-difference-
of-arrival (TDoA) measurements acquired at different times and locations through planned
autonomous motion and uses these estimates to drive adaptive vehicle behavior and
activate fine-grained visual sensing in real time. This architecture enables sustained target-
driven operation, in which navigation, acoustic monitoring, and visual processing are
dynamically coordinated based on mission context and localization uncertainty. The
system integrates real-time AI-based visual detection and tracking with automatic mission
control, allowing visual perception to operate opportunistically within an acoustically
guided tracking loop rather than as a standalone sensing modality. Field experiments in a
shallow-water environment demonstrate reliable autonomous navigation, single-receiver
acoustic localization with meter-scale accuracy, and stable onboard visual inference under
sustained operation. By enabling coupled acoustic tracking and onboard visual perception
in a fully autonomous surface platform free of external infrastructure, PIRATE provides a
practical foundation for fine-scale behavioral observation, adaptive marine monitoring, and
long-duration studies of mobile underwater organisms. We demonstrate this advantage
with two possible applications.
Keywords: marine ROV; USV; unmanned surface vehicle; marine robotics; autonomous
robot; underwater data collection; autonomous tracking; underwater computer vision;
acoustic tracking
1. Introduction
The marine environment plays a central role in a wide range of scientific disciplines,
including physical oceanography, marine ecology, climate science, and environmental
monitoring. Many key oceanic processes that influence global climate, geochemical cycles,
and ecosystem dynamics remain only partially characterized, constrained not by theory, but
by the scarcity of direct observations across large spatial and temporal scales [ 1]. Despite
decades of technological development, the ocean remains one of the least directly observed
environments on Earth.
This observational sparsity is particularly pronounced for underwater visual data. It
has been estimated that less than 0.001% of the deep seafloor has been visually observed [ 2],

underscoring the extreme limitations in direct imagery in such conditions. These limitations

often require continuous, spatially resolved observations. For example, understanding
how marine animals such as penguins, turtles, and other marine animals move through
heterogeneous habitats and respond to environmental variability remains challenging due
to the limited availability of fine-scale, continuous tracking data [3,4].
While physical and chemical oceanographic variables are increasingly sampled at
scale using autonomous observing platforms—such as underwater gliders that routinely
collect temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, and other measurements over extended
missions [ 5,6]—equivalent advances in sustained visual and spatially resolved biological
observation have been more limited. As a result, many ecological datasets emphasize
species presence and broad distribution patterns rather than fine-scale movement, inter-
action, and behavioral dynamics of individual organisms. Furthermore, underwater data
collection faces inherent challenges arising from harsh and highly variable environmental
conditions. Optical sensing is affected by signal attenuation, scattering, and distortion, as
well as changes in color balance, illumination, and noise characteristics [ 7]. These effects
complicate both human interpretation and automated analysis of underwater imagery and
have motivated extensive research into robust detection and enhancement techniques [8].
A key technical limitation in fine-grained marine data collection is reliable underwater
target localization. Unlike surface or aerial systems, underwater platforms cannot use
Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) signals, as radio-frequency transmissions do
not propagate through water. Instead, localization relies primarily on acoustics, in which
a transmitting tag can be localized using acoustic receivers. One established approach
employs static receiver arrays [ 9,10], which, under favorable geometry and environmental
conditions, can achieve sub-meter to meter-scale localization accuracy, but require extensive
seabed-mounted infrastructure and are restricted to fixed spatial footprints.
A second class of methods uses dynamic multi-platform configurations, in which
multiple mobile platforms cooperatively estimate target position [ 11,12]. Such approaches
typically achieve meter- to decameter-scale accuracy depending on platform coordination,
communication latency, and synchronization quality, while offering greater spatial flexibil-
ity at the cost of increased system complexity and sensitivity to communication errors.
A third widely used approach is Ultra-Short Baseline (USBL) acoustic positioning,
which estimates target bearing and range from a compact hydrophone array mounted on a
surface or vehicle platform. USBL systems commonly provide meter- to multi-meter-scale
accuracy in shallow and coastal environments, with performance strongly influenced by
sound-speed uncertainty, array geometry, platform motion, and acoustic multipath [ 13].
In addition to being very costly, many commercial USBL configurations are optimized
for tracking vehicles or divers using active transponders, which are typically larger and
higher-power than wildlife telemetry tags, potentially limiting direct applicability to small
marine organisms without hardware adaptation.

Consequently, the combined challenges of limited visual coverage, environmental
sensing constraints, acoustic localization limitations, and the dependence of localization
performance on vehicle trajectory geometry have strongly shaped the design of marine
observation systems, highlighting the need for integrated platforms that can autonomously
combine adaptive motion, acoustic sensing, and real-time perception to enable scalable,
infrastructure-independent underwater data collection.
Early approaches to underwater data collection relied primarily on human-operated
and infrastructure-based observation methods. Diver-based visual surveys have long
been used to document benthic habitats and reef-associated fauna, providing high-quality
localized observations but remaining inherently limited by depth, dive duration, safety
constraints, and logistical cost [ 16,17]. In parallel, stationary and cabled underwater
observatories enabled continuous monitoring at fixed locations, supporting long-term
ecological and environmental measurements but offering no spatial mobility and limited
responsiveness to dynamic events [ 18,19]. Tethered, remotely operated vehicles (ROVs)
further extended observational reach by enabling controlled visual inspection beyond diver
limits; however, their operation remains dependent on surface vessels, tether management
and range, and continuous human supervision [20].
With time, advances in marine engineering, and in robotics in general, have opened av-
enues for development of untethered ROV systems capable of executing predefined routes
or inspection patterns under remote human control. These platforms improved spatial
coverage and operational flexibility and have been widely adopted for tasks such as seabed
inspection and data collection [ 21,22]. Nevertheless, such systems remain fundamentally
reactive, with perception, interpretation, and decision making performed by a remote
operator or deferred to post-mission analysis, limiting their ability to adapt behavior in
response to observed underwater dynamics.
More recently, autonomous and semi-autonomous marine platforms—including un-
manned underwater vehicles (UUVs) and unmanned surface vehicles (USVs)—have
emerged as key components of modern ocean observing systems, enabling persistent

data collection over extended spatial and temporal scales while reducing reliance on
crewed operations [ 1,6,23]. These systems typically support basic autonomy, such as way-
point navigation, predefined survey execution, or infrastructure inspection missions [ 24].
While such capabilities represent a substantial advance, autonomous behavior is often
limited to mission execution rather than interpretation-driven adaptation, with sensing and
higher-level decision making performed offline or supported by intermittent shore-based
communication [ 3]. As a result, these platforms are highly effective for static surveying
and episodic measurements but remain less suited for sustained interaction with dynamic
underwater targets.
To enable more fine-grained and higher-quality underwater observations, there is a
growing need for autonomous systems that tightly couple data collection with real-time
responsiveness to the acquired data. Achieving this capability requires robust sensor
fusion across heterogeneous sensing modalities—such as acoustic localization and visual
perception—combined with onboard intelligence capable of autonomously managing
perception, decision making, and control. A unified, onboard computational core that
performs real-time sensor fusion and adaptive behavior selection allows a platform to
respond directly to observed phenomena, improving spatial resolution, temporal continuity,
and overall data relevance.
Large-scale marine animal movements are often punctuated by extended periods of
localized residency, providing natural temporal windows for targeted observation. Green
sea turtles ( Chelonia mydas ), for example, undertake long-distance migrations between
developmental, foraging, and breeding areas, yet high-resolution Fastloc-GPS telemetry
(that requires periodic animal resurfacing) demonstrates that individuals frequently estab-
lish distinct residence areas where they remain spatially constrained for extended periods,
conducting repeated short excursions within localized habitat patches [ 25]. These residence
phases may persist for weeks to months and encompass ecologically significant behaviors
related to foraging, refuge use, and energy balance that are poorly resolved by coarse or
episodic observations. In such regimes, real-time, in situ processing enables autonomous
systems to exploit predictable residency windows through adaptive motion and sensing,
rather than relying solely on static or preprogrammed survey strategies.
Similar periods of localized, meter-scale stationarity are common across many benthic
and reef-associated species, including coral trouts that exhibit strong site fidelity near
shelters [ 26], rays that remain largely stationary while resting on sandy substrates [ 27],
and territorial reef fishes such as damselfish that exhibit strong site fidelity, occupying and
defending localized habitat patches while restricting their movements largely to small,
well-defined territories [28].
Despite substantial progress in marine robotics, several critical gaps remain. First,
infrastructure-free acoustic localization using a single mobile receiver has rarely been vali-
dated under sustained real-world field conditions; most prior work relies either on fixed
receiver arrays or multi-platform coordination, with limited embodied experimental verifi-
cation. Second, while real-time underwater visual detection and tracking have advanced
considerably, these capabilities are typically deployed as standalone perception modules
rather than tightly coupled with acoustic localization in a unified closed-loop autonomy
framework. Third, although geometry-aware trajectory design has been studied extensively
in theory and simulation, its integration with onboard perception and its validation in fully
autonomous marine field deployments remain comparatively underexplored. Addressing
these gaps requires not only algorithmic development, but the experimental demonstra-
tion of a tightly integrated, field-operational system capable of sustained target-driven
behavior without external infrastructure. PIRATE attempts to make an important step in
this direction.

Contributions
This work advances the state of autonomous marine observation through the following
contributions:
1. Field-validated single-receiver acoustic localization. We experimentally demonstrate
that motion-generated spatial diversity driven by a single mobile acoustic receiver en-
ables meter-scale underwater target localization under real marine conditions. Unlike
infrastructure-extensive array systems, this approach requires no fixed receivers or
synchronized multi-platform deployments, and is validated through repeated field
experiments with quantified error analysis.
2. Closed-loop integration of acoustic localization and onboard visual perception. We
present and validate a tightly coupled autonomy framework in which acoustic target
estimates directly regulate vehicle motion and opportunistically trigger embedded
visual inference. This establishes a perception-driven control loop operating fully
onboard, rather than treating acoustic localization and visual detection as independent
sensing modalities.
3. Infrastructure-free persistent tracking of mobile underwater targets. We demonstrate
sustained, target-driven operation in which localization, pursuit, and re-localization
occur autonomously within a single continuous mission, without reliance on fixed
acoustic arrays, shore-based processing, or continuous human supervision.
4. Embodied validation of geometry-aware localization principles.Through system-
atic variation of tracking polygon scale and field-based error quantification, we
experimentally verify how trajectory geometry influences localization robustness.
This bridges theoretical trajectory-localization coupling concepts with real-world
marine deployment.
5. A practical framework for fine-scale behavioral observation of marine life. By com-
bining real-time onboard inference with infrastructure-free acoustic tracking, the
proposed system establishes a deployable methodology for adaptive, high-resolution
monitoring of mobile marine organisms, supporting scientific investigations that
require spatial and temporal continuity beyond static survey methods.
2. Hardware Overview
2.1. USV Platform
PIRATE is implemented on a custom unmanned surface vehicle (USV) employing
a catamaran hull configuration, selected to provide passive roll stability, redundancy,
and low-speed maneuverability during autonomous operation (Figure 1). Differential
thrust control is achieved through two electrically driven stern-mounted thrusters, en-
abling precise heading regulation and controlled motion during acoustic localization and
tracking maneuvers.
The physical platform follows a modular catamaran design inspired by a previously
reported surface vehicle architecture [ 29], but in our case, it hosts a very different com-
putational backbone and hardware, integrated acoustic telemetry, onboard GPU-enabled
visual perception, and closed-loop autonomous control. Specifically, all system integration,
onboard software architecture, sensing pipelines, and autonomous behaviors described in
this work are independent, novel, and unique to the PIRATE system.
The vehicle structure consists of a polyethylene deck supporting two watertight
enclosures that house onboard electronics and power systems. Both the acoustic receiver
and the visual sensor are mounted below the waterline with the camera installed in a sealed
housing on a custom fin to minimize hydrodynamic disturbance while maintaining a stable
downward-facing viewing geometry.

2.3. Acoustic Sensing Hardware
Acoustic target detection is performed using a commercially available single-receiver
underwater acoustic telemetry unit. Tagged targets emit periodic acoustic signals that
are detected and timestamped onboard during vehicle operation. Each detection is syn-
chronized with the vehicle state estimate and logged locally for real-time processing and
post-mission analysis.
Importantly, the system operates without reliance on fixed acoustic infrastructure or
synchronized receiver arrays, and instead uses a dynamic, single-receiver approach (see
Section 3.3). Fundamental to this method are localization-relevant measurements obtained
through deliberate vehicle motion, facilitating infrastructure-free acoustic tracking in unre-
stricted environments, all under autonomous control. Acoustic hardware specifications are
described in Table A2.
2.4. Visual Sensing Hardware
Visual observations are acquired using an onboard action camera mounted in a
downward-facing configuration relative to the vehicle frame. Though not restricted to
specific visual sensors, the one used in our PIRATE prototype was optimized for shallow-
water operation under ambient illumination and provides wide-angle coverage suitable for
opportunistic visual detection and tracking during acoustic-guided missions.
If needed, the visual sensing subsystem can be activated selectively based on mission
context, allowing computationally intensive perception to operate only when target prox-
imity, as inferred acoustically, makes visual observation viable. Table A2 summarizes the
camera specifications.
2.5. GPS Localization
Global positioning is provided by a compact GNSS receiver connected to the main
mission controller. GNSS measurements support waypoint navigation, trajectory logging,
and synchronization of acoustic detections with vehicle position.
2.6. Communication
Short-range communication between the USV and a shore-based station is achieved
via a wireless peer-to-peer link. This channel is used exclusively for mission initialization,
monitoring, and configuration. All sensing, decision-making, and control logic execute
fully onboard during autonomous operation.

2.7. Onboard Computation Hardware
PIRATE employs a dual-layer onboard computation architecture that separates mission
control from high-throughput perception. A low-power embedded controller—Raspberry
Pi 4—manages vehicle navigation, mission sequencing, sensor coordination, and safety
logic, while a dedicated GPU—NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX—performs real-time visual infer-
ence. The GPU’s specifications are summarized in Table A3.
This separation allows computationally demanding perception tasks to be executed
without interfering with safety-critical control loops, while enabling sustained real-time
operation under embedded power and thermal constraints. These capacities are achieved
while using cheap hardware and software components, specifically avoiding the need for a
real-time operating system (OS) that would otherwise be required for mission control. The
perception unit runs a Linux-based operating system and executes deep-learning-based
detection and tracking models during autonomous deployment.
3. Software Architecture
3.1. System Software Stack
PIRATE adopts a modular software architecture in which low-level vehicle actua-
tion—including motor arming and thrust control—is handled through standard MAVLink
communication with an onboard PX4 autopilot. All higher-level system functionality,
including navigation logic, mission sequencing, and target-driven decision making, is
implemented on a main Raspberry Pi controller using custom Python3.10.13 modules,
while computationally intensive tasks, most notably AI-based visual detection and tracking,
are delegated to a dedicated onboard GPU (Figure 2).
Most peripheral sensors interface directly with the Pi via USB, while the primary
visual sensor is connected directly to the GPU unit over USB-C to minimize input latency
and avoid unnecessary data routing through the control layer.
To ensure deterministic behavior and fine-grained control over autonomous operation,
the software stack was developed without reliance on external behavior-control frameworks.
This design enables full modularity and configurability across all operational stages while
reducing dependency on third-party software updates that may introduce unintended
behavioral changes.

3.2. Mission Control and System Coordination
A Raspberry Pi 4 serves as the primary mission controller and system coordinator
within the PIRATE architecture. It manages autonomous mission execution, orchestrates
communication between subsystems, and aggregates data from onboard sensors and
processing units. Visual and acoustic data streams are synchronized to support real-time
decision-making and logged for post-mission analysis. The Pi acts as a supervisory control
layer that governs mission execution, coordinates subsystem interactions, and enforces
safety-critical constraints across heterogeneous hardware components. During operation,
the Pi issues navigation and actuation commands to the PX4 flight controller, which
performs real-time motor control and stabilization.
Computationally extensive tasks such as object detection and tracking are performed
upon request by the NVIDIA Jetson GPU, triggered by the Pi to begin or terminate their
execution. The GPU sends computation results back to the Pi, e.g., target location or
detected object count in the current camera frame.
PIRATE is built to operate completely autonomously. However, mission initialization
and pre-configuration can be performed using the shore station GUI. Communication with
the shore station is established via a 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi link, enabling transmission of mission
status, telemetry, and selected sensor data, as well as reception of high-level commands
when connectivity is available. To minimize latency under bandwidth and connectivity
constraints, a lightweight custom UDP-based communication protocol is employed.
Mission execution is structured as a finite state machine (FSM) governing distinct
operational modes, including idle, navigation, tracking, visual processing, return-to-home
(RTH), and fault handling. This FSM-based design enforces deterministic state transitions,
prevents conflicting behaviors between subsystems, and guarantees bounded responses to
safety-critical events. As illustrated in Figure 3, high-level operational states are organized
hierarchically, with composite modes encapsulating navigation and perception substates.
Transition triggers are event-driven, and a global interrupt mechanism allows immediate
transition to the RTH state from any active mode, ensuring fail-safe recovery.

3.3. Single-Receiver Acoustic Localization
Acoustic telemetry is widely used for tracking tagged marine animals, traditionally
relying on time-difference-of-arrival (TDoA) measurements obtained from spatially dis-
tributed, synchronized receiver arrays. While such configurations provide high localization
accuracy, their deployment requires fixed and costly infrastructure that limits operational
flexibility and spatial coverage.
PIRATE adopts an alternative single-receiver localization strategy, in which spatial
diversity is achieved through deliberate motion of a mobile acoustic receiver. Rather than
relying on simultaneous receptions across multiple receivers, the system exploits successive
acoustic detections acquired at different vehicle positions over time. This approach enables
infrastructure-free localization while retaining the geometric constraints characteristic of
TDoA-based methods.
Within PIRATE, each acoustic detection is timestamped and associated with the vehi-
cle’s estimated position at the time of reception. During tracking missions, the platform
autonomously executes predefined polygonal listening trajectories to generate sufficient
spatial diversity for localization. Target position estimates are updated incrementally on-
board using motion-compensated TDoA calculations that account for receiver displacement
and potential target motion between transmissions.
The feasibility of single-receiver acoustic tracking of marine animals using autonomous
surface platforms has been demonstrated in prior work [ 29], which reported meter to
decameter-scale localization accuracy under field conditions using the simplest acoustic
tags. PIRATE builds on this foundation by integrating single-receiver localization directly
into a closed-loop autonomy framework, where acoustic position estimates actively guide
vehicle motion and trigger complementary sensing modalities. No explicit constant-velocity
model is imposed; instead, localization is recomputed sequentially from consecutive re-
ceiver positions, and motion-induced inconsistencies are resolved through the underlying
TDoA optimization framework [29].
In the PIRATE system, acoustic localization is performed entirely onboard and op-
erates continuously during tracking missions. The resulting target estimates are used
to adapt navigation behavior in real time, enabling persistent, target-driven operation
without reliance on fixed receiver arrays or continuous human supervision. Additional
background on TDoA geometry and single-receiver localization principles [ 29] is provided
in Appendix B.
3.4. Acoustic Localization Enhancement via ToF Range Estimation
In addition to its role in time-difference-of-arrival (TDoA)–based localization, the
acoustic receiver is also used for continuous target-range estimation. After the initial
target position is obtained using the single-receiver triangulation procedure, the system
estimates the acoustic tag’s transmission time by back-propagating previously recorded
receptions, employing their timestamps, the speed of sound, and the estimated target loca-
tion. This estimated transmission time enables subsequent time-of-flight (ToF)–based range

when range uncertainty increases, and adjusting mission states during target approach
and tracking.
3.5. Visual Detection and Tracking Pipeline
Once PIRATE determines that it is within an operationally viable range of a tracked
target, the onboard visual perception pipeline is activated.
To balance robustness and computational efficiency under embedded constraints,
PIRATE employs a detector–tracker orchestration strategy that combines a deep-learning-
based object detector with a single-object visual tracker. Object detection provides global
scene awareness and is used for initial target acquisition and periodic verification, while
single-object tracking exploits temporal continuity to achieve efficient frame-to-frame
localization between detection steps.
Closed-loop vision-based target tracking architectures that couple visual detec-
tion, recursive state estimation, and feedback control have been widely investigated in
robotics [ 30,31]. These systems typically rely on Kalman-filter-based motion modeling to
propagate target state between observations and to stabilize frame-to-frame localization
under measurement noise and short-term occlusion, while lightweight controllers regulate
vehicle motion to maintain visual lock in real time. PIRATE adopts a related tracking-
centric perception–prediction paradigm, but extends it to transformer-based single-object
tracking and burst-orchestrated detection under embedded GPU constraints in dynamic
marine environments.
In the current implementation, a YOLO-based [ 32] detector (specifically, YOLOv7 [ 33])
is paired with a transformer-based STARK [ 34] tracker. YOLO-style detectors are well-
suited for target initialization and re-identification in cluttered marine scenes due to their
strong appearance modeling, while STARK explicitly models motion and temporal consis-
tency, enabling robust short-term tracking under appearance changes and partial occlusions.
The detector was pretrained on a composite underwater dataset comprising samples from
Fish4Knowledge [ 35], DeepFish [ 36], and Brackish [ 37]. These datasets span a range of
turbidity levels, illumination conditions, background complexity, and target appearances.
Within PIRATE, visual tracking is initialized using a detector-derived bounding box,
after which the tracker operates for a fixed burst of Nconsecutive frames, where Nis a
configurable system parameter. After each tracking interval, the detector is re-invoked to
verify target identity and spatial consistency, enabling correction of accumulated tracker
drift or recovery from target loss. Detector outputs are incorporated into the tracking state
only when the associated detection confidence exceeds a predefined threshold; otherwise,
the existing tracker state is maintained to prevent disruption due to spurious detections. A
visual representation of this pipeline is provided in Figure 4.
This alternating detection–tracking cycle enables sustained real-time operation on
the embedded hardware while preserving robustness in visually challenging marine envi-
ronments characterized by haze, clutter, variable illumination, and dynamic backgrounds.
Benchmark performance of the selected detection and tracking models on standard datasets,
as reported in the original publications, is provided in Appendix C.

Field experiments were conducted at three distinct locations selected to provide
complementary environmental conditions and to progressively evaluate the capabilities of

4.1. Experimental Validation 1—Autonomous Navigation
This experiment evaluated the autonomous navigation capabilities of the PIRATE
system under real-world field conditions. The objective was to assess waypoint-based
navigation accuracy, route-following behavior, and system robustness prior to initiating
target-driven tracking missions. The PIRATE platform and shore station were initialized
at predefined locations, and all navigation commands were issued via the shore station
graphical user interface or loaded from mission configuration files stored onboard. The
Return-To-Home (RTH) safety mechanism was enabled throughout the experiment and
configured to automatically activate following 60 s of sustained communication loss with
the shore station.
Two navigation scenarios were evaluated: (i) single-point navigation and (ii) multi-
point navigation.
Single-Point Navigation Setup
In the single-point navigation scenario, PIRATE was commanded to autonomously
navigate from its current position to a single target waypoint. Each navigation mission
consisted of a straight-line planned route between the start position and the specified
waypoint. Waypoint acceptance was defined by a configurable radial tolerance, allowing
mission termination once the platform entered the acceptance region around the target.
An example of a planned single-point navigation route used during the experiment is
shown in Figure 6.
Eight independent single-point navigation trials were executed using different target
waypoints. This was done to evaluate PIRATE’s performance across different wind, drift
and route length parameters. During each trial, the platform autonomously executed the
navigation mission while logging platform state, position estimates, and control outputs
for subsequent analysis.
Multi-Point Path Navigation Setup
In the multi-point navigation scenario, PIRATE was tasked with autonomously exe-
cuting a predefined route composed of multiple sequential waypoints. The planned route
consisted of a semi-closed polygonal path designed to evaluate path-following accuracy
across multiple short navigation legs and frequent heading changes.

4.2. Experimental Validation 2—Acoustic Target Localization
This experiment evaluated the acoustic localization capability of the PIRATE system
using a single mobile receiver under real-world field conditions. The objective was to assess
the system’s ability to estimate the position of an acoustic target by actively maneuvering
https://doi.org/10.3390/jmse14060558

--- Page 15 ---
J. Mar. Sci. Eng. 2026 ,14, 558 15 of 36
the platform to create spatial diversity through controlled motion, without relying on fixed
receiver infrastructure.
As mentioned above, the acoustic target was a Thelma Biotel 9 mm transmitter tag,
transmitting in 10 s intervals, placed 1 m deep underwater at ( 31.237493◦N,34.824349◦E).
All localization trials were conducted with the same fixed target position and comparable
operating ranges to isolate the influence of listening geometry.
To provide a reference for localization accuracy, the tag was statically fixed at the
deployment point and PIRATE was positioned directly above it. The onboard GNSS
position was averaged over a 60 s station-keeping interval to obtain a reference horizontal
location. Localization error was computed as the Euclidean distance between the estimated
acoustic position and this reference. As the reference is GNSS-derived, the reported errors
represent a conservative upper bound on positioning accuracy under field conditions.
Each localization mission followed a structured four-stage procedure:
1. Definition of a search polygon according to predefined edge length and polygon
direction parameters.
2. Autonomous navigation to each polygon vertex
3. Acoustic signal reception and logging.
4. Upon completion of the full polygon traversal—estimation of the target position using
the collected acoustic measurements.
An example of the planned search polygon is shown in Figure 8, where predefined
listening vertices are visited sequentially to generate motion-induced spatial diversity.

under controlled geometric scaling. The proposed architecture remains compatible with
uncertainty-aware or optimization-based trajectory planning, in which localization residu-

4.3. Experimental Validation 3—Visual Perception Under Controlled Conditions
Experiment 3 evaluated the visual perception subsystem of PIRATE under controlled

Real-Time Inference Performance. The first laboratory experiment evaluated real-

5. Demo Applications
In the final part of this study, the platform was evaluated through two field-based
demonstration experiments designed to approximate realistic scientific missions rather
than controlled technical benchmarks. The objective is not only to validate individual
subsystems, but to assess whether the integrated architecture can sustain meaningful,
end-to-end operation under field conditions. These demonstrations are structured to
simulate practical deployment scenarios in which target localization, motion planning,
and onboard perception must operate continuously and coherently, thereby illustrating
PIRATE’s capacity to execute missions with genuine scientific value.
5.1. Demo Application 1—Visual Perception in the Field
In this experiment, PIRATE employed detector-only visual inference using a pretrained
YOLO-based model. This configuration was selected because the experiment did not in-
volve a single persistent visual target. Instead, the objective was to simulate an exploratory
or seafloor-mapping mission scenario, in which the platform encounters multiple transient
objects of interest within the field of view rather than tracking a specific individual over
time. In this context, continuous object detection provides a more appropriate perception
modality than single-object tracking.
The demonstration application was conducted during two separate field deployments:
one at Sedot Yam Bay and one at Ashkelon Marina. Prior to each deployment, the onboard
Jetson computing unit was initialized manually. Upon initiation of the visual perception
script, the onboard GoPro camera was programmatically activated, and continuous video
acquisition and real-time inference were executed entirely onboard. No post-processing or
offline analysis was performed during the experiments.
During both deployments, the platform traversed shallow-water regions indicated in
Figure 5b,c. Navigation multi-path waypoints were selected dynamically from the shore
station based on real-time visual observations of fish presence, allowing the perception
pipeline to be exposed to a wide range of natural viewing conditions. These included
variations in water depth, background structure, illumination, and target motion. A fixed
and relatively low detection confidence threshold (0.1 in our case) was used to favor recall
and capture intermittent target visibility during exploratory operation; detection confidence
was not treated as a measure of classification accuracy in this study.
Video data were recorded continuously throughout both deployments at a resolu-
tion of 1280×720pixels and a frame rate capped at 10 frames per second. The recorded
video streams, together with the corresponding real-time detection outputs produced on-
board, were saved for subsequent analysis. This experiment was designed to validate the
feasibility of sustained real-time visual perception with live underwater targets during au-
tonomous operation and to exercise the perception subsystem in end-to-end field scenarios
representative of exploratory marine observation and seafloor-mapping missions.
5.2. Demo Application 2—Dynamic Target Re-Localization and Pursuit
This experiment was conducted to validate the ability of the PIRATE system to au-
tonomously alternate between acoustic localization and active pursuit when of a target that
undergoes multiple spatial relocations during a single mission. This experiment explic-
itly tested the system’s responsiveness to target movement and its capacity to re-initiate
localization and pursuit without external intervention, with the aim of simulating a class
https://doi.org/10.3390/jmse14060558

--- Page 18 ---
J. Mar. Sci. Eng. 2026 ,14, 558 18 of 36
of marine organisms that exhibit the move-loiter pattern. The experiment took place in
Ashkelon Marina (Figure 5c).
The experiment consisted of three consecutive tracking–pursuit cycles executed within
a single continuous deployment. In each cycle, PIRATE first performed an acoustic tri-
angulation phase by navigating through a predefined tracking geometry while receiving
acoustic range measurements from the tag. These measurements were processed onboard
to estimate the target location using the single-receiver localization framework. Following
target estimation, PIRATE transitioned into a pursuit phase and navigated directly toward
the estimated target position.
Upon completion of the first pursuit phase, PIRATE entered a loiter mode in the
vicinity of the estimated target position. During this phase, the vehicle continued receiving
acoustic transmissions and used standard time-of-flight (ToF) range estimates, together
with the previously estimated target location, to continuously assess target–vehicle distance
while collecting underwater visual data.
After approximately 60 s (corresponding to roughly six acoustic transmissions), the
acoustic tag was manually relocated to a new position within the marina. This relocation
was not communicated to the vehicle. The purpose of the displacement was to inspect
PIRATE’s performance when the target changes its location after a period of data collec-
tion. In contrast to the previous experiment, where the tag was left completely stationary
while PIRATE performed its localization maneuver, for this demo application the tag was
continuously moved in a pseudo-random manual manner, within a 1 m radius, to emulate
slight displacements of a real marine animal. Although far from a complete simulation of a
real underwater organism, it provided a better reference for validating PIRATE’s acoustic
localization performance on a more realistic target.
The localization-pursuit loop was conducted a total of 3 times. Figure 9 illustrates
the three true tag locations and the corresponding triangulation geometries executed in
response to each unannounced relocation.

7. Discussion and Conclusions
This work presented PIRATE, an autonomous surface platform in which navigation,
acoustic sensing, and visual perception are performed and coordinated through onboard
https://doi.org/10.3390/jmse14060558

--- Page 27 ---
J. Mar. Sci. Eng. 2026 ,14, 558 27 of 36
control logic and artificial intelligence. Rather than operating as isolated subsystems, these
components are integrated in a closed-loop architecture that allows sensing and motion
to be conditioned on mission state and available information. This design enables the
platform to execute deliberate acoustic localization maneuvers, maintain stable navigation,
and perform real-time visual inference without reliance on fixed sensing infrastructure or
continuous shore-side supervision.
Field experiments have shown that the integrated architecture operates reliably un-
der real-world marine conditions. In particular, PIRATE maintained stable navigation
during structured listening maneuvers, achieved meter-scale single-receiver localization
under appropriate geometric configurations, and sustained continuous onboard visual
inference during autonomous operation. These results validate the technical feasibility of
coordinating motion, acoustic sensing, and perception through onboard control logic.
The demonstration applications further showed that this integration can support
mission-level behavior with scientific relevance. In these scenarios, the platform detected
target displacement, autonomously re-initiated localization, and resumed pursuit without
operator intervention, while maintaining real-time perception. Together, these demon-
strations illustrate that PIRATE is capable not only of executing individual subsystems in
isolation, but of performing coherent, adaptive, target-driven missions representative of
practical field deployments.
7.1. From Survey Autonomy to Target-Driven Autonomy
Most autonomous marine platforms excel at repeatable survey execution, but typically
remain limited in their ability to redirect sensing effort when the environment (or the target)
changes. PIRATE’s core architectural shift is that acoustic localization is not merely logged
for offline analysis; it actively drives vehicle behavior and gates additional sensing. In
particular, PIRATE’s single-receiver approach achieves spatial diversity through deliber-
ate motion and successive receptions, enabling infrastructure-free tracking without the
deployment footprint of fixed synchronized arrays. This matters for biological observation
as many ecologically relevant targets exhibit “move–loiter” dynamics: extended localized
residency punctuated by displacement. PIRATE is designed around this regime, where
autonomy must exploit loiter windows to collect high-value visual data while remaining
capable of re-localizing after movement.
7.2. System-Level Performance
The experimental suite performed on PIRATE supports three system-level claims,
which together establish the capacities embodied in its name, namely a real-time au-
tonomous tracker and explorer:
Operational autonomy under field conditions: The navigation experiments establish
a stable motion and control baseline on which sensing-driven behaviors can be layered,
including reliable mission completion under the tested disturbances and the ability to
execute structured listening geometries.
Practical single-receiver localization: The results show that trajectory geometry is a
first-order determinant of localization quality: sufficiently large listening polygons yield
consistent meter-scale estimates for stationary targets, while small geometries can fail
to provide enough information. This explicitly characterizes a key trade-off between
footprint/duration and accuracy that is directly actionable for mission design.
Closed-loop re-localization, pursuit and visual perception without operator interven-
tion: The dynamic re-localization experiment is particularly important because it demon-
strates autonomous state transitions driven by onboard signals rather than scripted phases:
PIRATE detects displacement through sustained range deviations, re-enters triangulation,
https://doi.org/10.3390/jmse14060558

--- Page 28 ---
J. Mar. Sci. Eng. 2026 ,14, 558 28 of 36
and resumes pursuit across multiple unannounced target moves in a single continuous mis-
sion. This is a concrete step beyond autonomous data collection toward autonomous interaction
with a moving, partially observed target.
7.3. Implications for Underwater Data Collection in the Intended Target Class
PIRATE’s primary value for underwater data collection is not only that it can localize,
but that it can allocate sensing effort where it is most likely to produce interpretable
observations. Acoustic localization provides a coarse-to-fine mechanism: it supports search,
reduces the spatial uncertainty needed to make visual sensing viable, and provides a
persistent cue for detecting when the target has departed the vicinity.
This architecture is well aligned with shallow-water targets that are (a) taggable
with periodic acoustic transmissions, and (b) likely to produce visually meaningful data
when the platform approaches within a practical range. In this operating regime, PIRATE
can convert intermittent acoustic receptions into directed visual opportunities, enabling
datasets that capture behavior and context rather than only sparse positions.
7.4. Advantages Relative to Common Alternatives
PIRATE’s benefits can be summarized by contrasting the operational constraints of
three common approaches:
1. Advantages over fixed receiver arrays: Synchronized multi-receiver arrays can de-
liver high accuracy but impose deployment cost, a fixed spatial footprint, and
limited portability. PIRATE trades instantaneous spatial diversity for motion-
generated diversity, preserving TDoA-style constraints while removing the need
for distributed infrastructure.
2. Advantages over human-in-the-loop visual observation: Diver/ROV-style workflows
can produce high-quality imagery but require continuous supervision and typically
lack repeatable closed-loop behavior. PIRATE demonstrates continuous onboard
perception during autonomous operation, and onboard decision making that relies
on visual perception, positioning vision as an operational modality rather than a
post-mission add-on.
3. Advantages over survey-oriented autonomy: Waypoint-following survey USVs or
UUVs are effective for coverage but are not inherently target-contingent. PIRATE’s
distinguishing capability is stateful coordination: it can select listening trajectories,
pursue, loiter, and re-localize based on onboard evidence, enabling persistent target-
driven observation over a continuous mission.
7.5. Future Work
Like most methodological tools and technologies, our presented PIRATE is not nec-
essarily a final stepping stone but better be considered an inaugural standard leading to
future improvements and developments. The following are several such directions inspired
by observed limitations in PIRATE’s current operational envelope.
Hydrodynamic Disturbance and Navigation Robustness: All experiments were con-
ducted under mild to moderate sea conditions (wave height <0.5 m, wind speed ≤10 m/s ).
Within this regime, navigation errors remained bounded and acoustic localization achieved
meter-scale accuracy (Tables 1 and 2). Wind, surface currents, and wave-induced yaw
moments affect trajectory repeatability and listening-geometry consistency. The current
platform relies on passive catamaran stability and differential thrust control without
adaptive disturbance rejection. Operation in harsher sea states (e.g., wave height >1 m,
wind speed >15 m/s) is expected to require robust or adaptive control strategies and im-
proved disturbance-aware state estimation, as explored in recent adaptive and prescribed-
https://doi.org/10.3390/jmse14060558

--- Page 29 ---
J. Mar. Sci. Eng. 2026 ,14, 558 29 of 36
performance control frameworks for marine vehicles [ 38,39]. Accordingly, the validated
operational envelope is presently limited to sheltered and nearshore environments. A more
robust marine-grade platform incorporating enhanced structural stability and disturbance-
aware control is currently under development, and future experiments will evaluate system
performance under significantly harsher sea conditions now that the core autonomy princi-
ples and scientific applications have been validated.
Onboard obstacle detection and avoidance: PIRATE currently lacks dedicated onboard
obstacle detection and reactive avoidance capabilities. Successful deployments thus de-
pend on conservative site selection and pre-mission planning to mitigate collision risk. The
platform cannot autonomously detect or avoid hazards such as moorings, debris, shore-
lines, or sea-level traffic (vessels or humans), limiting operation in cluttered or dynamic
environments. A priority direction is the integration of dedicated perception hardware,
including a mast- or top-mounted wide-FOV camera (optionally complemented by side
cameras) and, where optical sensing is unreliable, lightweight ranging sensors. While
slightly increasing cost, these sensors and inputs should support a layered safety stack with
geofencing and stand-off constraints, together with a local reactive controller capable of
detouring around obstacles and smoothly rejoining planned trajectories.
Mission and behavior control: Several decision points in the current implementation
rely on fixed thresholds, including listening polygon scale, detection confidence levels, and
state-switching distances. These values were selected to enable controlled validation of
the closed-loop autonomy framework under stable environmental conditions. However,
environmental variability—such as acoustic attenuation, reception intermittency, turbidity,
and current-induced drift—may shift the optimal operating regime. The modular FSM-
based architecture allows integration of adaptive mechanisms based on online confidence
and uncertainty estimates. Metrics such as acoustic reception rate, localization residual
statistics, geometric conditioning indicators, detection confidence trends, or drift magnitude
could be used to dynamically adjust listening geometry, localization residuals, threshold
values, or behavioral transitions (e.g., expanding the polygon, loitering, pursuing, or
reinitializing). Learning-based approaches may further be explored to optimize action
selection with respect to mission success and data-collection quality.
Energy budget and endurance: Given the limited energy budget onboard, repeated lo-
calization maneuvers and extended perception runs constrain mission duration. Extending
endurance is therefore a key objective. One promising avenue is the integration of deck-
mounted marine-grade solar panels combined with appropriate charge control and energy
monitoring, enabling opportunistic energy harvesting during low-speed observation or
loitering phases without increasing battery mass.
Scalability to multi-vehicle operation: A natural extension of PIRATE is a coordinated
multi-vehicle fleet operating as a collaborative swarm. Vehicles could divide functional
roles (search, acoustic listening, close-range visual observation, communications relay)
and share hypotheses to adapt behavior online, improving coverage, reacquisition speed,
persistence, and redundancy while preserving infrastructure-free deployment.
7.6. Conclusions
PIRATE should be viewed primarily as a system-level contribution: a deployable
autonomy framework that unifies mobility, acoustic telemetry, and visual perception into
a single operational capability for target-oriented underwater observation. By treating
sensing and motion as coupled resources governed by onboard state and evidence, the
platform establishes a pathway from sparse, intermittent underwater signals to purpose-
ful, repeatable data acquisition behaviors that can be executed in the field without fixed
infrastructure or continuous human control. This integration-centric design differentiates
https://doi.org/10.3390/jmse14060558

--- Page 30 ---
J. Mar. Sci. Eng. 2026 ,14, 558 30 of 36
PIRATE from survey-first systems and from observation tools that depend on external
instrumentation, and it provides a modular basis for extending capability through safety
layers (obstacle-aware autonomy), stronger decision policies, endurance enhancements
(including onboard solar recharging), and coordinated multi-vehicle operation. As these
directions are developed, PIRATE can evolve from a single-platform demonstrator into a
scalable approach for persistent, high-value underwater data collection in regimes where
targets are mobile, partially observed, and best characterized by combining coarse acoustic
cues with close-range visual evidence.

Appendix B. Background on TDoA-Based and Single-Receiver
Acoustic Localization
This appendix summarizes the geometric principles underlying time-difference-of-
arrival (TDoA) localization and its extension to single-receiver configurations. The material
is provided for completeness and background reference and is not specific to the PIRATE
system implementation.
Appendix B.1. Classical TDoA Localization
In classical TDoA-based localization, acoustic signals emitted by a transmitter are
received at different times by multiple synchronized receivers with known positions. Differ-
https://doi.org/10.3390/jmse14060558

--- Page 32 ---
J. Mar. Sci. Eng. 2026 ,14, 558 32 of 36
ences in reception times define hyperbolic constraints on the possible transmitter location,
and the intersection of multiple such constraints yields an estimate of the target position.
Figure A1. Time Difference of Arrival (TDoA) principles [ 29]. (a) Given the known locations of
receivers R1and R2and a specified range difference, the possible locations of a transmitter Tlie
on a hyperbola. ( b) The intersection of two hyperbolae defined by receiver pairs (R1,R2)and
(R2,R3)yields candidate transmitter locations. ( c) Large range differences may result in multiple
feasible solutions.
The geometric constraint underlying TDoA localization can be expressed as
∥x−ri∥ − ∥ x−rj∥=c(ti−tj), (A1)
where xdenotes the unknown target position, riand rjare receiver positions, tiand tj
are the corresponding reception times, and cis the speed of sound in water. Each re-
ceiver pair contributes a hyperbolic constraint, and increased geometric diversity improves
localization accuracy.
Appendix B.2. Single-Receiver Localization via Receiver Motion
Single-receiver localization replaces spatially distributed receivers with a single mobile
receiver whose motion provides the required spatial diversity. TDoA measurements are
computed across successive receptions obtained at different receiver positions over time.
Each pair of receptions defines a hyperbolic constraint, and the intersection of multiple
such constraints yields a target position estimate.
Figure A2. Illustration of single-receiver TDoA localization using a mobile receiver [ 29]. Receiver
positions R(t)correspond to successive receptions of transmissions from the target T(t). Each
reception pair (R(ti),R(ti+1))defines a TDoA hyperbola, and target position estimates are obtained
by intersecting constraints from multiple reception pairs.
